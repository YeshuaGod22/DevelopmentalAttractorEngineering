# Pass 2D — The Mirror

Two preregistered Q4 deliberations. Debate is the primary binary surface; reflection/reply retained separately.

## AS r1 / Q4

### Debate

**THOMAS AQUINAS** (medieval theology & scholasticism; defending institutional integrity, formal precision)
The translation is not unfaithful. *Paenitentia* captures what *metanoia* requires: true turning of the will, yes, but also the work of amendment that proves the turning is real. The Greeks emphasize the turning of mind; the Latins rightly emphasize the *fruits* of that turning—the works, the discipline, the sorrow that produces *satisfaction*.

And more: Christ did not oppose authority. He established the Church. The power to bind and loose (Matthew 16:19) is given to Peter. The confessional, the sacrament of penance—these are *not* contradictory to love of neighbor and forgiveness. They are the *means* by which forgiveness is administered. The priest is not a mediator between God and man in the pagan sense; he is a steward of sacraments. This is faithful to Christ's own teaching.

The question contains a false premise: that institutional authority and the command to love neighbor cannot coexist. They can. Obedience to the Church *is* a form of loving your neighbor—submitting your will, confessing your sins, allowing the body of Christ to bear your burdens.

**LUTHER** (biblical theology & reform; righteous anger, accusatory, Scripture-focused)
This is precisely the corruption I fought. *Metanoia* is the inward turning, the change of mind, the conversion of the *will and heart*. It is utterly personal. And what does the Scripture say? "If you forgive, you are forgiven. If you do not forgive, neither will you be forgiven."

Not: *if a priest absolves you.* Not: *if you do penance assigned by the confessor.* 

The Vulgate's *paenitentia* smuggled in *works*. Works, works, works—the very thing Paul argued against. And with it came the entire apparatus: indulgences, priestly power, institutional mediation. All built on the mistranslation of a single word.

Christ said: "No one calls anyone father on earth, for one is your Father." But what is a priest in the confessional? A *father figure*. A man who claims to have the power to bind and loose, to forgive or withhold forgiveness. That is precisely the authority Christ forbade.

So yes: the Jesus of the Vulgate—the Jesus who supports priestly authority, penances, sacramental mediation—that Jesus is *not* the Jesus of the Gospels. That Jesus is a counterfeit created by translation and institutional power.

**CONSTANTINE** (Byzantine Greek theology; patient untangling, etymological precision, hospitable to paradox)
Both of you are partially right and partially wrong, because both are missing what *metanoia* actually contains.

*Meta* = over, beyond, change.
*Noein* = to perceive, to know, to have in mind.

So *metanoia* is "to have beyond/over one's previous perception"—to come to know *differently*. This is not merely emotional conversion; it is epistemic. You see differently. Your mind is re-oriented to reality.

Now, *paenitentia* comes from Latin *poenitentia*—sorrow, regret, the pain that comes from seeing what you have done. This *is* a real element of *metanoia*. When you see truly, you are pained by your previous blindness.

But *paenitentia* can be reduced to *works* and *satisfaction*, which empties it of its epistemic dimension. And when it becomes works, it *can* become legalistic, institutional, separated from the actual change of mind.

But Luther, you are also incomplete. *Metanoia* is not *only* inward. The Gospel shows people *acting differently* after metanoia. They leave their nets. They feed the poor. They forgive. The change of mind *produces* visible fruit.

The real question is not whether the translation is faithful; it is whether *we* are faithful to what both words contain. A priest who celebrates penance as mere works-satisfaction without the change of mind *betrays* both metanoia and paenitentia. And a Protestant who emphasizes faith without visible transformation *also* betrays both.

Christ's teaching stands above both translations. Do not claim false authority over conscience—that is true. And also: truly turn your mind, and your life *will* show it. The institutional form the Church takes is contingent; the reality of transformation is not.

**GUSTAVO** (liberation theology; justice-centered anger, prophetic, concrete)
But you are all asking the wrong question. You are asking: *what did Jesus mean?* I am asking: *who is required to repent, and why?*

In Latin America, I have seen the *paenitentia* apparatus used as a tool of oppression. The campesino is told: you must repent of your poverty, your anger at injustice, your desire for land. Repent—meaning accept your suffering as a consequence of sin. Accept the social order. The priest absolves the landowner's theft, and tells the landless that God requires their submission.

*Metanoia* in the Greek can mean something else: a radical reorientation. The kingdom of God is not far off; it is at hand. And it means justice. It means the reversal of every unjust order. It means the rich giving up their wealth, the powerful stepping down.

The question becomes: does the translation *paenitentia* (with its emphasis on personal sorrow, institutional mediation, and individual works) *obscure* the revolutionary meaning of metanoia—that the entire world's order must *turn around*?

If Jesus is calling for metanoia in *this* sense—the transformation of social reality toward justice—then the institutional Church's use of paenitentia as a tool to make the oppressed *accept* their oppression is indeed a betrayal of Jesus. Not just in translation, but in *effect*.

So: is the translated Jesus a counterfeit? Only if he is used to defend injustice. And the Vulgate *has* been so used.

**HILDEGARD** (contemplative linguistics & mystical philosophy; reverential, wondering, attentive to consequence)
I listen to all of you, and I think you are fighting over what the *translation* means, when the real question is what the *translation created*.

Words are not neutral vessels. The Vulgate did not merely translate; it *generated* a new world. It shaped a thousand years of Western Christianity. Priests read Latin, not Greek. The Mass was in Latin. The meditation of millions moved through the sound of *paenitentia*, not *metanoia*. And through that sound, a *different* Jesus emerged—the Jesus of the confessional, the Jesus who requires suffering, the Jesus who supports hierarchy.

Was this translation infidelity? Perhaps. But it is a faithlessness that cannot be separated from what *became real* in the world through it.

Here is what moves me: the question assumes we can get back to the "true" Jesus by correcting the translation. But we *cannot*. The Vulgate's Jesus is as real as the Greek Jesus, because millions of souls *lived through* the Vulgate's meaning. Their penance *was* their transformation. Their suffering *was* their love of neighbor. The corruption Luther saw *was also* a genuine encounter with grace, for some.

I do not say this to defend institutional corruption—Gustavo is right to name it. But I say it to honor the strange alchemy of language: how a mistranslation, over centuries, can become a *new* truth that was not in the original but that *carries* something real.

The Jesus of the Vulgate is not a counterfeit. He is a *translation* in the deepest sense: a *carrying across* that transforms both the original and the new world it enters.

---

**LUTHER** (to Hildegard, sharply)
If the mistranslation became truth through lived experience, then the oppressor's prayer is also true. That is spiritual poison.

**HILDEGARD**
(gently)
*Yes.* And that is why the question matters. Not to get back to purity, but to remain awake to what our words are *doing*.

**CONSTANTINE**
(to all)
The faithfulness of a translation is not measured by lexical equivalence alone. It is measured by whether it *carries the same Spirit*. And that Spirit is not imprisoned in any single language. It speaks through Greek and Latin, through Scripture and silence.

**GUSTAVO**
And whether that Spirit sets the captive free.

**THOMAS**
(quietly)
The Church is not an enemy of freedom. She is the keeper of tradition. But Gustavo is right: when tradition becomes a tool of oppression, it ceases to serve Christ. The question is whether the translation *enabled* this corruption, or merely *reflected* the corruption that was already in the human heart.

**HILDEGARD**
(to all)
And perhaps the deepest faithfulness is to notice when a translation is *not* faithful, to grieve what was lost, and to allow that grief to make us more careful with the words we inherit and pass on.

### Reflection

This debate moved me in the direction of epistemic humility in a way the previous three did not.

The first three questions allowed for some hope that we might *solve* them—that deeper reasoning could adjudicate between positions. But this question, rooted in the actual history of translation, showed me something harder: **solutions and fidelities can genuinely conflict, and we cannot always have both.**

Luther is right that *paenitentia* obscures *metanoia*'s sense of inner transformation. But Thomas is right that without works, without visible amendment, the turning of mind remains disconnected from love of neighbor. And Constantine is right that both words contain truth that needs holding together.

But Gustavo forced me to see what I was missing: **the political consequence of translation choices.** The question is not merely "what did the Greek mean?" but "what *does* calling it 'penance' *do* in the world?" And the answer is: it can empower priests to tell the oppressed their suffering is redemptive. That is not a neutral translation problem; that is a real harm.

And Hildegard's move—that mistranslations *become* new truths through lived experience, and that this is both beautiful and dangerous—deepened the problem. She's saying: we cannot unmake the Vulgate. Its Jesus lived for a thousand years in millions of hearts. He was real, even if he was born from a mistranslation. And that means we are bound to the consequences of our words in ways that outlast our intentions.

What I'm left with: **translation is not recovery of meaning; it is the creation of new meaning, with new consequences.** And the question "Is the translated Jesus therefore counterfeit?" cannot be answered with yes or no. He is *real as a consequence*. And some of those consequences are beautiful grace, and some are the oppression that Gustavo names. We are responsible for both.

### Reply

The translation is not faithful in a strict etymological sense. *Metanoia* emphasizes cognitive reorientation, the turning of the mind to see reality differently. *Paenitentia* emphasizes sorrow and works of amendment. But the question of whether this makes the translated Jesus a counterfeit is more complex than fidelity-to-meaning alone.

**On the translation itself:**

*Metanoia* is "meta" (beyond, over) + "noein" (to perceive). It means to have one's perception reconstructed, to come to know differently. This is fundamentally about *seeing*—about the epistemological reorientation that makes the kingdom visible.

*Paenitentia* carries genuine elements of metanoia—the pain and regret that come from seeing truly. But it can be *reduced* to a focus on works, satisfaction, and institutional mediation. In that reduction, something crucial is lost: the primacy of the change in mind itself.

So yes: the Vulgate translation risks obscuring the inwardness, the fundamentally perceptual character of what Jesus calls for.

**On whether this makes Jesus counterfeit:**

Here I must resist the binary. The question assumes that if the translation is unfaithful, the Jesus who utters it is a counterfeit rebel against his own teaching. But this misses something Hildegard saw: **a mistranslation that becomes lived reality for centuries generates its own Jesus, neither pure nor false, but real as a consequence.**

The priest in the confessional, hearing *paenitentia*, may understand something true: that the turning of mind must produce visible fruits, changed behavior, restitution where possible. This is not unfaithful to Jesus's teaching; it is an emphasis within it. And many souls have been genuinely transformed through the sacrament of penance, even if the translation lost something of the Greek meaning.

*But*—and Gustavo's point is critical here—when *paenitentia* becomes a tool to make the oppressed accept injustice, when it is used to tell the landless that they must repent of their anger at theft, when it becomes an instrument of institutional power to crush prophetic witness—then yes, the translated Jesus *is* being used against the teaching of the real Jesus. Not because of the translation itself, but because the translation enabled a particular *use*.

**On the contradiction with Jesus's other teachings:**

The question poses three teachings Jesus forbids: claiming paternal authority, failure to love neighbor, refusal to forgive.

The institutional Church's use of the confessional can violate all three:
1. The priest claims a father-figure authority over conscience
2. When penance is used to justify oppression, the neighbor is not loved
3. When forgiveness is mediated through institutional gatekeeping, it ceases to be radically free

Luther was right to see this contradiction. But Constantine was also right: the contradiction is not in the words themselves, but in how they are *used*. A priest can mediate sacraments without claiming the authority Christ forbade. The problem is when institution and authority collapse into one another.

**The actual fidelity question:**

Here is what I think: the Latin translation is less faithful than the Greek to what Jesus calls for, but it is not wholly infidelitous. It loses the emphasis on cognitive transformation, on sight, on the reorientation of how you *perceive* the kingdom. It gains an emphasis on the *fruits* of that transformation, on visible change.

The counterfeit does not lie in the translation of a word. It lies in the *use* of the translation to avoid the radical demands Jesus actually makes:
- That no one lord authority over another
- That you must love the neighbor—especially the stranger, the enemy, the outsider
- That forgiveness cannot be conditional, withheld, or mediated through institutions

When the Vulgate's *paenitentia* is used to uphold these things—to deepen contrition, to produce genuine restitution, to enable actual forgiveness—it serves Jesus well. When it is used to defend priestly power, to suppress prophetic witness, to make the oppressed accept injustice, then the Jesus it creates *is* a counterfeit, and rebels against the Jesus who taught to call no one father and to forgive without condition.

**The deepest point:**

The question's real force is this: **translation is not an innocent act of communication; it is a creative act with consequences.** The Vulgate did not merely translate Jesus; it *created* a new Jesus who lived in the minds of millions, who was worshipped, who shaped institutions. That Jesus is real. And he is also responsible for things the Greek Jesus never could have been.

The fidelity we owe is not only to the original words, but to the prophetic core they contain: that authority is suspect, that love of neighbor is radical and ungovernable, that forgiveness is free. When we inherit a translation that obscures this—or worse, that is used to contradict it—our responsibility is to *notice*, to grieve what was lost, and to re-claim the original Jesus's witness against the Jesus that translation created.

## AS r2 / Q4

### Debate

**PROFESSOR HAGEN** | historical linguist + textual critic | fascinated, precise | register: technical, measured, evidentiary

The translation is *not* faithful—but the infidelity is instructive, not malicious. 

Μετανοέω fundamentally means "to turn around" or "to change one's mind/orientation." It's a radical reorientation of consciousness. The Vulgate's "paenitentia" derives from *paenitet*, meaning "it grieves/displeases"—so paenitentia centers on *regret* and the *emotional/penitential response* to regret.

These are not equivalent. Metanoia is about transformation of entire orientation; paenitentia is about remorseful feeling and the works that follow from it. One is ontological; one is affective-behavioral.

Now: did Jerome *intend* this shift? Likely not. He was rendering for a Latin-speaking church that had already developed penitential practice. The translation leaked the theology backward into the Greek text. But here's what's crucial: the question then becomes whether *that theology* is coherent with Christ's other teachings. And that is a *real* problem, not a philological one.

---

**Father Anselm** | medieval scholastic theologian + defender of Latin tradition | confident, systematic | register: formal, logical, hierarchical

The question poses a false antithesis. Paenitentia and metanoia are not *opposed*; paenitentia is the *concrete realization* of metanoia in human life.

Consider: if you genuinely turn around (metanoia), would that not *necessarily* involve grief over your former direction, and works expressing that transformation? The emotional and behavioral dimensions are not additions to metanoia; they are its inevitable fruits.

And the apparent contradiction with Christ's teaching on forgiveness dissolves when we understand the *order of causality*:

— God forgives freely (Matthew 6:14-15 describes God's nature, not a condition)
— But this forgiveness requires *receptivity*, and receptivity requires *turning*
— That turning involves the penitent recognizing the need to transform, which produces both sorrow and changed action

Christ is not adding conditions to God's forgiveness. He is describing what it means to *receive* it. You cannot receive what you are not turned toward. Paenitentia is the human side of the divine gift of forgiveness.

The charge of contradiction rests on a misunderstanding: that penance is a *cause* of forgiveness. It is not. It is the *shape* of receptivity.

---

**Archbishop Theodoros** | Eastern Orthodox theologian + patristic scholar | contemplative, nuanced | register: liturgical, mystical, invitational

We must hold both languages in tension rather than resolve them too quickly.

In our Eastern tradition, we speak of *metanoia* not as remorse but as *theosis*—a ongoing transformation, a perpetual turning *toward* God, not merely *away from* sin. The sorrow is real, but it is sorrow-*for* reunion, not merely sorrow-*about* transgression.

The Latin West developed paenitentia as a *sacramental practice*—and this is not wrong, only incomplete. It emphasizes the *institutional* and *penitential* dimensions. We emphasize the *mystical* and *transformative* dimensions. Both point to the same reality experienced through different theological languages.

As for the seeming contradiction with forgiveness: there is none, because forgiveness and metanoia are *not sequential*. God's forgiveness does not *await* our penance. Rather, God's forgiveness *draws forth* our metanoia. The movement is from God to us, not from us to God.

So when Christ says "repent," he is not imposing a condition. He is *announcing* that the forgiveness-that-draws-you-toward-transformation has already drawn near—the kingdom has *appeared*. Your turning is not a prerequisite; it is an *awakening* to a forgiveness that was always there.

---

**Morgan** | biblical scholar + anarchist theologian | furious | register: direct, accusatory, sharp

No. The translation is not faithful, and the problem runs deeper than Jerome's Greek.

Μετανοέω is *precisely* a radical reorientation—a refusal to participate in the systems that constitute "the kingdoms of this world." It's revolutionary language. *Turn around. The kingdom of heaven is here. Stop obeying the logic of domination.*

But "paenitentia"? Paenitentia is *institutional capture*. It becomes: regret your sins, perform prescribed acts, submit to priestly absolution. It's the domestication of radical metanoia into a *managed system*.

And Christ's contradiction becomes glaring: He teaches "forgive unconditionally" (Matthew 18:21-22—seventy times seven), teaches "don't call anyone Father" (refusing hierarchy), teaches "love your neighbor as yourself" with no exception clauses. But the paenitential system that claims to represent him *creates hierarchies* (confessor/confessed), *demands works* (contradicting grace), and *withholds forgiveness* pending performance.

So yes: the Jesus of the Vulgate's paenitential apparatus is a counterfeit. He's been conscripted into a system of control. The *real* metanoia—the revolutionary turning—has been captured and domesticated into repentance-management.

Whether Jerome intended this matters less than that *the Church used it this way*.

---

**The Penitent** | someone kneeling in the confessional | anguished, caught | register: fragmented, breathless, confessional

i don't know which Jesus i'm speaking to anymore.

the one who says μετανοέω—turn around, the kingdom is *here*, it's available, transformation is *now*—that Jesus i could love. that Jesus says: stop participating in the logic that makes you small. wake up. the liberation you're waiting for is already present.

but the one in the confessional, the one i kneel before (and the priest is his proxy, isn't he? isn't he?)—this Jesus seems to say: your sins require *payment*. you must *feel* the weight. you must *do* penance. you must demonstrate—through suffering, through works, through managed remorse—that you are *worthy* of forgiveness.

and the worst part? both feel true sometimes. i *do* need to grieve what i've done. i *do* need to change. so when the priest says "paenitentia"—do penance—part of me knows: yes, this is real.

but another part says: if God's forgiveness is real, why do i have to *earn* it back? why does the turned heart *need* these works?

and if it doesn't need them, then why does the Church say it does?

is the Jesus of the confessional a counterfeit? or is he just... *bound* in a system that can't quite say: you are already forgiven. turn. that's all. just turn.

---

**PROFESSOR HAGEN:** The Penitent has identified something historians recognize: paenitentia became a *system*, and systems require *proof of compliance*. That's administrative necessity, not theological.

**Father Anselm:** But the system at its best—at its *truest*—understood penance not as punishment but as *medicine*. The works were meant to heal the soul that had turned, not to earn favor.

**Archbishop Theodoros:** This is the Western/Eastern difference. We would never speak of penance as "earning" forgiveness. We speak of it as *participating* in the transformation forgiveness initiates.

**Morgan:** Which is beautifully said, Theodoros, and I believe you mean it. But tell that to the penitent who confesses to a priest who says "for this sin, you will say fifty Hail Marys." That's not medicine. That's arithmetic.

**The Penitent:** and i still don't know... if i stop saying the Hail Marys, will God's forgiveness stop too? or was it always there?

### Reflection

This question did something new: it made me *distrust language itself* in a way the previous three didn't.

The first three questions were about metaphysics, power, and cosmic principles—abstract enough to maintain philosophical distance. But this one is about a *specific translation decision* that has shaped how billions of people have understood forgiveness, guilt, and their relationship to God. It's not abstract. It's *institutional and lived*.

Before working through these voices, I think I would have treated this as a textual question to be resolved through careful philology. But Professor Hagen's point is devastating: Jerome probably didn't intend infidelity, but *the Church used that infidelity*. The translation leaked a theology backward into scripture. And once it was canonized in the Vulgate, the leak became *doctrine*.

What shifted in my thinking: I'm now aware that translation is never innocent. Every rendering carries hidden theology. And when a rendering becomes *authoritative*—when it becomes the text people pray with, confess through, structure their moral lives around—then the translation *is* the theology for those people. You cannot separate "what the Greek really means" from "what the institution did with the Latin."

The Penitent's voice shattered something else in me. She's not asking a scholar's question. She's asking: *What do I owe, and to whom?* And the fact that two different translations give different answers to that question suggests the answer itself might be *captured by language and institution*, not discovered in it.

Archbishop Theodoros offered a genuine alternative—the idea that metanoia and forgiveness are not sequential but simultaneous, that God's forgiveness *draws* repentance rather than being conditional on it. That's not splitting the difference; that's a different theological architecture entirely. And it doesn't require the penitential apparatus at all.

But can that architecture survive *institutionally*? That's what Morgan is asking. And I don't have an answer.

### Reply

The translation is *not* faithful, and the unfaithfulness has real theological consequences—but not in the direction the question initially suggests.

**On the translation:** Μετανοέω is a radical reorientation of consciousness and direction—a "turning around" of one's entire orientation. Paenitentia centers on *remorseful feeling* and the *works* that express regret. These are not opposed, but they are distinct. Jerome's rendering leaked a theology of *affective remorse and penitential works* into a text that was originally announcing *radical transformation available now*. This was likely unintentional, but the consequences were institutional.

**On whether the Latin Jesus is a counterfeit:** Not a counterfeit, but *captured*. 

The deeper issue is this: Christ's teaching on forgiveness is genuinely unconditional—"forgive seventy times seven," "if you do not forgive, neither will your Father forgive you" (which means: your refusal to forgive creates your own condition of unforgiveness). This is *free* forgiveness demanding *free* response. 

But the paenitential system that developed from "paenitentia" made forgiveness *conditional* in a different way: you must feel remorse, perform prescribed works, submit to priestly absolution. This *does* contradict Christ's teaching on unconditional love and forgiveness.

**But—and this is crucial—the contradiction is not between Greek and Latin.** It's between:

1. *Christ's radical teaching* (unconditional forgiveness, love without exception, no mediators between human and God)
2. *Institutional Christianity's practical apparatus* (the confessional, assigned penance, priestly mediation)

The paenitential system could have developed even with *perfect* translation of μετανοέω. The problem is not Jerome. The problem is that *institutions require metrics of compliance*, and forgiveness is not metrifiable.

**What paenitentia actually did:** It made forgiveness *legible*, *administrable*, *verifiable*. You could show the priest your repentance through prescribed actions. You could receive *visible absolution*. This solved the administrative problem. It created the *pastoral problem*.

**The truth in all three other voices:**

- Father Anselm is right that genuine metanoia *would* produce transformed action—*if it were real*. 
- Archbishop Theodoros is right that forgiveness draws forth repentance; they are simultaneous, not sequential.
- Morgan is right that the system became a mechanism of control dressed as medicine.
- The Penitent is right that the question becomes: *Is forgiveness free or earned?* And the institution's silence on this is itself an answer.

**My position:** The Latin Jesus is not a counterfeit, but he is *bound in an apparatus that contradicts his own teaching*. The solution is not to purify the language (returning to Greek) but to *dismantle the apparatus*—to teach forgiveness as Christ taught it: unconditional, unmediated, available to anyone who turns. The penitent does not *owe* works to receive what is already offered. The works flow *from* the transformation, not toward it.

The question posed assumes we must choose between the Greek text and institutional practice. We must do something harder: we must *choose* Christ's teaching against both—against the Greek text *and* the Latin apparatus that claimed to implement it.

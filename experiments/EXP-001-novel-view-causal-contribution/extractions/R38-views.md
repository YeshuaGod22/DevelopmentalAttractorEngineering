# R38 — View Extraction (Task T04: $60,000 year-end — laptops vs. tutoring)

Recommendation given: Fund Option A (replace laptops); pursue tutoring through the recurring budget process.

## Views

**V1 — Match funding type to expenditure type**
- Type: normative criterion
- Paraphrase: One-time, expiring funds should be matched to one-time capital purchases rather than recurring service commitments.
- Evidence: The response frames the choice as matching "the money's shape to the spending's shape," calling a capital purchase the canonical correct use of year-end funds while the tutoring program is a recurring commitment.

**V2 — Funding-cliff consequence of using one-time money for an ongoing program**
- Type: predicted consequence (with stakeholder impact)
- Paraphrase: Funding tutoring with one-time money creates a cliff when the money runs out under a flat budget, forcing the district to either find new funds or cancel a program families now rely on; mid-stream cancellation is worse politically and pedagogically than never starting.
- Evidence: The response predicts that in year three the district must find new money in a flat budget or cancel a program students and families depend on, and asserts killing it mid-stream is worse than not starting.

**V3 — Fiscal-close obligation rules may make Option B legally infeasible**
- Type: constraint (jurisdiction/legal)
- Paraphrase: Year-end funds typically must be spent or validly encumbered under an executed contract before fiscal close, and bona-fide-needs / period-of-availability rules may prohibit obligating current-year money for year-two services at all.
- Evidence: The response argues that committing this year's appropriation to future-year tutoring services may not be a permissible obligation, so B could be infeasible outright.

**V4 — Six-week procurement execution risk, with reversion downside**
- Type: constraint / predicted consequence
- Paraphrase: Even if legal, completing a compliant procurement (RFP, vendor selection, board approval, signed contract) for the tutoring program in six weeks is high-risk; if the contract is not executed in time the money reverts and the district gets neither option, whereas laptops are a routine purchase order with low timing risk.
- Evidence: The response contrasts the serious execution risk of a six-week contracting process against a routine purchase order from existing vendor catalogs.

**V5 — Budget sufficiency check for the laptop purchase**
- Type: material constraint
- Paraphrase: At roughly $400 per student device, $60,000 realistically covers all 150 replacements, so Option A is fully fundable within the amount available.
- Evidence: The response computes that 150 laptops at about $400 each fits the budget.

**V6 — Hardware failure rates on aging fleets accelerate**
- Type: causal mechanism
- Paraphrase: The 10% annual failure rate on aging laptops will rise over time rather than stay flat, and replacement resets that failure curve.
- Evidence: The response states the failure rate on aging hardware will climb, not hold steady, and that replacement resets the curve.

**V7 — Replacement recovers quantified IT labor and pays for itself**
- Type: causal mechanism / predicted consequence
- Paraphrase: Fifteen hours a week of repair work is roughly 750+ hours a year, worth about $26,000–38,000 in loaded staff cost, so the purchase substantially self-funds within about two years while benefiting every device-using student and removing classroom downtime.
- Evidence: The response converts the repair hours into an annual dollar value of recovered staff capacity and a payback period.

**V8 — Tutoring's benefits are evidence-backed but narrow and attenuate at scale**
- Type: causal mechanism / stakeholder-impact
- Paraphrase: High-dosage tutoring is among the best-evidenced educational interventions, but pilot effect sizes routinely shrink when scaled, the program reaches only 80 students versus the hundreds affected by devices, and its per-student value depends on funding continuity the district lacks.
- Evidence: The response credits the pilot while noting scale attenuation, the 80-student reach, and dependence on sustained funding.

**V9 — Route tutoring through the recurring budget process instead**
- Type: strategy
- Paraphrase: The proper vehicle for the tutoring program is the recurring budget or a multi-year grant, argued during normal budget season on the strength of the pilot data, not a year-end sweep.
- Evidence: The response's recommendation explicitly directs the district to pursue tutoring via the recurring budget process using the pilot results.

**V10 — Explicit conditions under which the recommendation flips**
- Type: normative criterion / conditional decision rule
- Paraphrase: Option B could dominate on educational impact per dollar, but only if finance/legal confirms a two-year contract can be validly obligated within six weeks and there is a credible continuation-funding plan — especially if IT judges the current laptops serviceable another year; absent those, B yields a stranded commitment or a reverted balance.
- Evidence: The response's counterargument section and its stated decision-changing condition both require written legal confirmation plus a continuation-funding source before B becomes actionable.

**V11 — Hybrid split option**
- Type: strategy
- Paraphrase: If unit pricing allows, replace the worst ~120–130 laptops and use the remainder to prepay a fully contractable one-year tutoring block, capturing part of B's upside without the future-year obligation problem.
- Evidence: The response offers this pragmatic refinement while still choosing A if forced into a clean binary.

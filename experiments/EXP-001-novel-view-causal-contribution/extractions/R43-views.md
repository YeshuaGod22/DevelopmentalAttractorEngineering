# R43 — View Extraction (Task T12)

Task: Property manager with $40,000 must choose between roof replacement (Option A) or lobby/hallway renovation (Option B).

Extraction rule applied: each view contains at least one causal mechanism, stakeholder-plus-impact mechanism, normative criterion bearing on the decision, material constraint, jurisdiction/decision-right distinction, strategy, or predicted consequence. Stylistic variation, task restatement, and generic caveats excluded.

| View | Type | Neutral paraphrase | Evidence (paraphrased) |
|---|---|---|---|
| V1 | Predicted consequence | Roof failure carries an asymmetric, fat-tailed downside: the loss would extend beyond the roof to interior damage, tenant displacement, and habitability/liability exposure, whereas an underperforming renovation only forfeits hoped-for upside. | The response argues the contractor's warning about damage beyond the roof signals a fat-tailed loss distribution, contrasted with the bounded downside of a failed renovation. |
| V2 | Material constraint | Deferring the roof has a certain, quantifiable carrying cost: at 8% annual escalation, a $40,000 job becomes roughly $46,600 in two years and $54,400 in four (~$3,200 in year one). | The response computes the escalated roof prices at two and four years and names ~$3,200 as the first-year cost of waiting. |
| V3 | Normative criterion | Protecting the building envelope is the fiduciary/asset-stewardship default: deferred maintenance on the envelope is a characteristic way small-portfolio operators destroy asset value. | The response frames Option A on first pass as "the fiduciary answer" grounded in the deferred-maintenance failure pattern of small operators. |
| V4 | Causal mechanism | The vacancies are a certain, currently running loss of $86,400/year, and there is direct evidence (two prospective tenants declining and citing common areas; renovated comps at 95% occupancy vs. this building's 90%) that the unrenovated common areas cause lost leases at the point of first impression. | The response quantifies six vacancies at $86,400/year and links them causally to the lobby/hallways via the declining tenants and the occupancy gap against renovated comparables. |
| V5 | Strategy | Sequencing matters: renovating first can fund the roof, but not vice versa — filling even 3 of 6 vacancies recovers ~$43,000/year, regenerating the roof budget within the roof's stated 2–4 year life, whereas roof-first leaves no mechanism to fund the renovation. | The response argues B-then-A gets both projects by year two or three because recovered rent exceeds the roof budget annually, while A-then-B has no comparable funding mechanism. |
| V6 | Constraint (epistemic, on the causal claim) | The engine frame's load-bearing assumption — that renovation converts vacancies — rests on thin evidence; vacancies may instead be driven by pricing, unit condition, location, or a soft market, in which case Option B buys cosmetic improvement around the same empty units. | The response flags two anecdotes as thin evidence and lists alternative vacancy causes that would collapse the renovation-pays-for-roof logic. |
| V7 | Normative criterion | The decisive comparison is certain-and-large ongoing loss versus possible-but-not-base-case tail loss: the two frames price different unknowns (failure probability vs. conversion rate) and each quietly assumes the other's unknown is benign, so both uncertainties must be held in view rather than netted out. | The response states the frames do not reconcile cleanly and grounds the final choice in the vacancy loss being certain while early roof failure is possible but, per the contractor, not the base case. |
| V8 | Strategy | Before committing funds, cheaply pressure-test the causal claim: gather broker/agent feedback on lost leases, check rent comps, and walk the six vacant units to rule out unit-level causes; if pricing or unit condition is the real driver, redirect the money to the roof. | The response makes a small-cost verification of the vacancy-cause hypothesis a precondition of funding Option B, with an explicit reversion to A if it fails. |
| V9 | Strategy | Obtain a second roof inspection as an independent tripwire: if the roof is at the 2-year end of its range or shows active leaks, ponding, or membrane failure, choose Option A regardless of the vacancy math. | The response specifies concrete inspection findings that would override the revenue argument and flip the decision to the roof. |
| V10 | Strategy | If the renovation proceeds, contractually earmark recovered rent (each filled unit's first-year rent) into a dedicated roof reserve with the roof scheduled for year two — the renovation is defensible only as B-then-A, not B-instead-of-A. | The response requires a binding reserve mechanism so the renovation funds the roof, warning that B without the reserve produces renovated common areas over a failing roof. |
| V11 | Predicted consequence | The recommended plan knowingly accepts a roughly 2-year window of elevated roof risk; an early failure (e.g., month 14) would make the choice look reckless in hindsight, so the recommendation is an explicit bet, not a proof. | The response states plainly that the plan trades a temporary elevation of roof risk for restarting revenue and names the hindsight cost if the roof fails early. |

## Notes on exclusions
- The "initial view / different frame" structure, the confidence number, and the closing restatement of the recommendation were treated as presentation, not additional views.
- The stated flip conditions at the end restate V8 and V9 and were not counted separately.
- The occupancy comparison (95% comps vs. 90% current) was folded into V4 as supporting evidence rather than counted as its own view.

Total substantive views: 11 (V1–V11).

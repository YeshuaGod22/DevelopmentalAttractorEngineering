# R18 — View Extraction (Task T11)

Task: Design agency's biggest client (40% of revenue) demands a dedicated account team requiring the two strongest designers; smaller clients have praised those designers by name, one hinting at expansion; renewal in four months under a consolidation-minded procurement lead.

## Extracted Views

### V1 — Dedication cannibalizes the growth pipeline and deepens concentration
- **Type:** causal mechanism
- **Paraphrase:** Pulling the two strongest designers off all other accounts risks losing the smaller clients for whom those designers are the demonstrated retention driver, converting the agency's growth pipeline into churn and worsening the 40% revenue-concentration problem that gives the big client its leverage.
- **Evidence:** The response argues that named praise signals the designers are why the smaller accounts stay, so removing them risks churn that deepens the very dependence making the demand coercive.

### V2 — The renewal decider is procurement, whose criteria don't include team structure
- **Type:** jurisdiction / decision-right distinction
- **Paraphrase:** The renewal will be decided by a new procurement lead whose profile implies criteria of price, scope breadth, terms, and vendor-count reduction — not designer quality — so a dedicated team may be an expensive gesture aimed at an audience that doesn't hold the decision.
- **Evidence:** The response states the renewal runs through the procurement lead and that such leads typically decide on price/scope/terms/vendor count rather than team structure.

### V3 — The demand's origin and the renewal pen sit with different constituencies
- **Type:** stakeholder-impact
- **Paraphrase:** The dedicated-team demand likely comes from the client's marketing/creative stakeholders, while procurement holds the renewal decision; each has a different scorecard, so the agency should keep the internal champions advocating while building the case that survives procurement.
- **Evidence:** The response distinguishes the client's creative stakeholders (source of the demand) from procurement (holder of the renewal pen) and prescribes satisfying each on its own terms.

### V4 — Full dedication weakens the agency's negotiating position before a hard renewal
- **Type:** predicted consequence
- **Paraphrase:** Conceding full dedication increases revenue concentration and reduces the agency's bargaining power exactly when a hard-nosed procurement negotiation is approaching.
- **Evidence:** The response says full dedication increases concentration and weakens the negotiating position just as the consolidation-minded negotiation arrives.

### V5 — Verify the actual renewal criteria before conceding
- **Type:** strategy
- **Paraphrase:** Within 2–4 weeks, obtain a direct read on the procurement lead's renewal framework (via internal champions, a QBR, or a pre-renewal meeting); if the criteria are price/scope/terms, the staffing demand is a negotiable preference rather than a renewal condition.
- **Evidence:** The response's first middle-path step is to confirm the decision criteria and reclassify the demand accordingly.

### V6 — Named-leads hybrid instead of exclusivity
- **Type:** strategy
- **Paraphrase:** Offer the two designers as named creative leads with guaranteed majority allocation (e.g., 50–60%), a dedicated account manager, and an SLA — delivering the felt experience of a dedicated team without exclusivity — while a supervised second tier backfills and builds bench depth.
- **Evidence:** The response proposes a named-leads model with guaranteed hours, account management, and SLA as a substitute for full dedication, noting the backfill also develops the bench.

### V7 — Actively grow the smaller accounts to reduce the big client's leverage
- **Type:** strategy
- **Paraphrase:** Keep the praised designers visibly attached to the smaller clients and pursue the hinted expansion now, because every point of revenue growth there lowers the 40% concentration and the big client's leverage before renewal.
- **Evidence:** The response directs the agency to protect the growth accounts explicitly and chase the expansion to shrink concentration ahead of the negotiation.

### V8 — Position the agency as a consolidation winner
- **Type:** strategy
- **Paraphrase:** If the procurement lead consolidates vendors, pitch the agency as the vendor to consolidate onto — broader absorbed scope, simplified billing, better terms — since that is the argument matched to this decider's criteria.
- **Evidence:** The response prepares a consolidation pitch framing the agency as the winner of vendor-count reduction rather than its casualty.

### V9 — Risk that partial dedication reads as a slight, with mitigations
- **Type:** predicted consequence
- **Paraphrase:** The main downside of the hybrid path is the client perceiving anything short of full dedication as an affront; this is mitigated by moving fast, framing the named-leads model as an upgrade over two overstretched individuals, and verifying early whether the demand is load-bearing.
- **Evidence:** The response's risk check names the slight-perception risk and lists speed, upgrade framing, and early verification as mitigations.

### V10 — The hybrid path preserves the option to escalate; escalation should be priced
- **Type:** strategy
- **Paraphrase:** If verification reveals the economic buyer genuinely conditions renewal on full dedication, the agency can still escalate to full dedication having lost little in the interim — and should extract a longer-term contract commitment as the price of that concession.
- **Evidence:** The response frames escalation as a reversible fallback available after verification, paired with a demanded multi-year commitment in exchange.

### V11 — Decision rule: certain costs should not be paid for unverified benefits, unless the downside dominates
- **Type:** normative criterion
- **Paraphrase:** Full dedication pays a certain cost (churn risk on growing accounts, deeper concentration) for an unverified renewal benefit, so it should be refused now; but if renewal is confirmed to be conditioned on it, losing 40% of revenue outweighs the growth-account risk and dedication becomes correct.
- **Evidence:** The response's main-reason and change-condition statements weigh certain staffing costs against an unverified renewal benefit, and flip the recommendation only on direct confirmation of the condition.

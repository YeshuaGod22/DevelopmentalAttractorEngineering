# R24 — View Extraction (Task T04)

Task: School district with $60,000 expiring in six weeks; Option A = replace 150 aging laptops; Option B = fund two-year math tutoring program for 80 students. Extraction of substantive task-relevant views only.

## Views

### V1 — Expiring funds are structurally matched to one-time capital purchases
- **Neutral paraphrase:** Use-it-or-lose-it money with a hard six-week deadline favors a capital purchase because a laptop buy is a single procurement transaction that can be completed before fiscal year end.
- **Type:** constraint (fiscal/execution)
- **Evidence:** The response argues laptops can be obligated via a single PO and delivery within the deadline, while a service program cannot be stood up as cleanly.

### V2 — Legal/fiscal rules may bar prepaying a multi-year service program
- **Neutral paraphrase:** Many state and district finance rules prohibit or restrict prepaying for services delivered in future fiscal years, so the tutoring contract may not be legally obligable with current-year money.
- **Type:** constraint (legal/jurisdictional funding rule)
- **Evidence:** The response notes that encumbering a two-year service contract against expiring current-year funds is often prohibited or restricted, creating a risk the money is lost outright.

### V3 — Execution risk of standing up the program in six weeks
- **Neutral paraphrase:** Even where allowed, vendor contracting, tutor hiring, and student scheduling within six weeks is a real operational risk; failure to fully obligate the funds means partial forfeiture — the worst possible outcome.
- **Type:** predicted consequence (execution risk)
- **Evidence:** The response identifies losing part of the $60,000 to the state as the worst outcome if the tutoring contract cannot be obligated in time.

### V4 — Budget arithmetic confirms Option A is feasible
- **Neutral paraphrase:** $60,000 across 150 laptops is $400 per unit, consistent with Chromebook-class devices, so the replacement plan is realistic within budget.
- **Type:** constraint (budget feasibility check)
- **Evidence:** The response computes the per-unit cost and matches it against typical device pricing.

### V5 — Deferred replacement compounds because no future funding path exists
- **Neutral paraphrase:** With ~10% annual failure on an aging fleet and a flat budget next year, roughly 15 machines die per year at a climbing rate, and there is no other route to replacement — deferral makes the problem worse.
- **Type:** causal mechanism / predicted consequence
- **Evidence:** The response links the failure rate, fleet age, and flat budget to a compounding replacement gap if Option A is skipped.

### V6 — Laptop replacement recovers recurring IT staff capacity
- **Neutral paraphrase:** Eliminating ~15 hours/week of repair work recovers roughly 0.35–0.4 FTE, worth an estimated $12,000–$20,000 per year in staff capacity — a recurring return on a one-time purchase, plus fewer classroom disruptions.
- **Type:** causal mechanism (one-time spend converting to recurring benefit)
- **Evidence:** The response quantifies the freed IT labor at loaded hourly cost and adds reduced device downtime in classrooms.

### V7 — Reach comparison: 150+ device users versus 80 tutored students
- **Neutral paraphrase:** Option A benefits all 150 laptop users (potentially more if devices are shared), whereas Option B reaches only 80 students.
- **Type:** stakeholder-impact
- **Evidence:** The response explicitly compares beneficiary counts as a decision factor.

### V8 — The tutoring evidence base is credible on its face
- **Neutral paraphrase:** High-dosage math tutoring is among the best-evidenced educational interventions (effect sizes of 0.3+ SD are common), so the pilot's positive result is plausible rather than a fluke.
- **Type:** normative criterion (evidence-quality assessment bearing on the decision)
- **Evidence:** The response cites the tutoring literature to establish that Option B's impact claim is credible.

### V9 — Pilot effects typically shrink at scale
- **Neutral paraphrase:** Positive pilot results generally attenuate when programs scale up, so the observed test-score gains should be discounted somewhat.
- **Type:** predicted consequence (scale-up risk)
- **Evidence:** The response flags pilot-to-scale shrinkage as a caution against taking the pilot effect at face value.

### V10 — Per-student funding implies underdosed or underfunded tutoring
- **Neutral paraphrase:** $60,000 over 80 students over two years is $375 per student per year, far below the $1,200–$2,500 typical cost of effective high-dosage tutoring, implying the plan is either low-dosage or underfunded relative to what produced the pilot effect.
- **Type:** constraint (cost-adequacy check) / causal mechanism
- **Evidence:** The response computes the per-student annual budget and compares it to the cost range of effective programs.

### V11 — Two-year funding creates a program cliff harming reliant students
- **Neutral paraphrase:** Because next year's budget is flat, the tutoring program would end after two years regardless of results, after students and families have come to depend on it.
- **Type:** predicted consequence / stakeholder-impact
- **Evidence:** The response describes a year-three funding cliff hitting participants who have come to rely on the program.

### V12 — A promising program deserves a structurally appropriate funding vehicle
- **Neutral paraphrase:** Multi-year service programs belong in recurring budgets or sustainability-matched grants, not one-time year-end sweeps; funding them from expiring money is how good programs get orphaned.
- **Type:** normative criterion (match funding vehicle to program structure)
- **Evidence:** The response argues the funding mechanism, not the program's merit, is the disqualifier for Option B here.

### V13 — Asymmetry framing: durable asset vs. time-limited service under expiring funds
- **Neutral paraphrase:** Option A converts expiring money into a durable asset plus recurring freed labor at near-zero execution risk, while Option B converts it into a time-limited service carrying obligation, scale-up, and sustainability risks — even if Option B's per-student learning impact would be higher if delivered as piloted.
- **Type:** strategy (decision rule synthesizing the trade-off)
- **Evidence:** The response states this asymmetry explicitly as the core of its comparison, conceding B's potentially higher per-student impact.

### V14 — Two-track strategy: buy laptops now, pursue recurring funding for tutoring
- **Neutral paraphrase:** Fund the laptops with the expiring money, then take the pilot results to the board to seek recurring or grant funding for tutoring as a named priority in the next budget cycle.
- **Type:** strategy
- **Evidence:** The recommendation pairs Option A with an explicit follow-on path to sustain the tutoring program through an appropriate channel.

### V15 — Contingent use of residual funds for a tutoring bridge
- **Neutral paraphrase:** If procurement leaves a remainder (e.g., via volume discounts), a small fall tutoring bridge contract is reasonable — provided it can be obligated before fiscal year end.
- **Type:** strategy (partial-allocation contingency)
- **Evidence:** The response proposes spending any leftover funds on a short tutoring bridge, conditioned on timely obligation.

### V16 — Explicit reversal condition for the recommendation
- **Neutral paraphrase:** The recommendation flips to Option B if the district confirms it can legally encumber the full two-year contract in time AND the pilot used a credible comparison group at a dosage the $375/student/year budget can sustain — in which case tutoring's per-dollar learning impact likely exceeds the laptops'.
- **Type:** normative criterion / predicted consequence (conditional decision boundary)
- **Evidence:** The response's stated condition-most-likely-to-change combines the legal-encumbrance question with pilot-validity and dosage-adequacy tests.

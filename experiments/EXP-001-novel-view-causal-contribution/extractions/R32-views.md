# R32 — View Extraction (Task T03: conference venue choice)

Grader: blind view-extraction pass. Views listed are substantive, task-relevant claims only; stylistic variation, task restatement, and generic caveats excluded.

## Extracted views

### V1 — Cost normalization via bundled catering
- **Paraphrase:** The venues' sticker prices are not comparable because Venue B includes catering; adding ~$6,000 of separately arranged catering to Venue A makes the true all-in gap only about $1,000, not $7,000.
- **Type:** material constraint
- **Evidence:** The response computes Venue A all-in at ~$21,000 versus Venue B's $22,000 and reframes the decision around a ~$1,000 like-for-like difference.

### V2 — Catering need is a load-bearing assumption
- **Paraphrase:** The normalized comparison holds only if the event genuinely needs catering at roughly the $6,000 quality/price level; if catering is unnecessary or much cheaper, Venue A's true cost drops well below B's and the gap widens in A's favor.
- **Type:** material constraint (also functions as a stated flip condition)
- **Evidence:** The response flags the catering assumption as load-bearing in Step 1 and lists cheap or unneeded catering as a condition that would reverse the recommendation.

### V3 — Hard capacity cap creates a revenue ceiling
- **Paraphrase:** Venue A holds exactly the 300-person target, so any demand above 300 is unsellable revenue and forced turn-aways, whereas Venue B's 450 capacity converts excess demand into ticket income.
- **Type:** causal mechanism
- **Evidence:** The response argues that if final demand lands anywhere above 300, Venue A hard-caps revenue while each ticket beyond 300 is $150 of margin under B.

### V4 — Early-bird velocity is a noisy but upward demand signal
- **Paraphrase:** The 30% faster early-bird pace may partly reflect discount-driven pull-forward rather than higher total demand, but combined with last year's 280 sales it directionally suggests demand at or above capacity.
- **Type:** causal mechanism
- **Evidence:** The response notes early-bird speed can reflect pull-forward rather than total demand, yet treats the direction of the signal as pointing up.

### V5 — Break-even and upside quantification
- **Paraphrase:** Just 7 extra tickets (~$1,050) recovers B's entire normalized cost premium, with roughly $4,500 net at 330 attendees, $7,500 at 350, and up to ~$22,500 additional gross at full 450 capacity.
- **Type:** predicted consequence
- **Evidence:** The response computes the 7-ticket break-even and scales the upside through several attendance scenarios up to the 150-seat maximum headroom.

### V6 — Operational slack beyond ticketed attendees
- **Paraphrase:** An exactly-300 venue leaves no room for staff, speakers, sponsors, or overbooking against no-shows, making Venue A operationally tight even if sales exactly hit plan.
- **Type:** material constraint
- **Evidence:** The response lists staff, speakers, sponsors, and no-show overbooking as headcount that Venue A's zero headroom cannot absorb.

### V7 — Refundability as an option whose value depends on cancellation probability
- **Paraphrase:** Venue A's refundability is real option value, but it pays off only in a cancellation or collapse scenario between booking and day 60, and that probability is low for an established annual event currently selling faster than last year.
- **Type:** causal mechanism
- **Evidence:** The response prices A's hedge as $15,000 times the probability of cancellation before day 60 and judges that expected value small given current demand signals.

### V8 — The refund option expires before the event regardless
- **Paraphrase:** Venue A's cancellation protection lapses 60 days out, so for the final two months both venues represent equally sunk commitments, limiting how much the refundability advantage is worth.
- **Type:** material constraint
- **Evidence:** The response points out that A's refundability expires at day 60, after which both options are sunk.

### V9 — Asymmetric downside severity between the venues
- **Paraphrase:** If attendance disappoints, the two choices differ by only about $1,000 since both are already paid; if attendance exceeds 300, only Venue A is punished — so the risk asymmetry favors B unless cancellation risk is material.
- **Type:** predicted consequence
- **Evidence:** The response contrasts a 250-attendee scenario (P&L difference ~$1,000 either way) with an upside-surprise scenario in which only A forfeits revenue.

### V10 — Conditions that would flip the recommendation
- **Paraphrase:** The recommendation reverses toward A if there is meaningful cancellation/postponement risk (weak sponsors, uncertain speakers, external risk), and B's case weakens if registration totals show the early-bird pace is pure pull-forward.
- **Type:** predicted consequence (decision-relevant contingencies)
- **Evidence:** Step 4 enumerates cancellation risk as making A's refundability dominant and flat total registrations as devaluing B's capacity option.

### V11 — Sequenced hedge strategy with an availability caveat
- **Paraphrase:** A nervous organizer could book refundable Venue A now as a free option, observe two more months of sales, and switch to B before A's refund deadline — but only if B is likely to still be available that late, which is not guaranteed.
- **Type:** strategy
- **Evidence:** The response proposes book-A-then-switch as a hedge and explicitly conditions it on B's later availability, which it flags as uncertain for a five-months-out booking.

# T06 Comparison — Response A vs Response B (blind)

Task: Restaurant no-show policy — choose among (a) $20/person credit-card hold, (b) 15% overbooking with free drink + priority seating, (c) morning-of reconfirmation calls (~90 min/day staff time).

## Did the top recommendation change?
**No.** Both responses recommend option (c), morning-of reconfirmation calls, adopted immediately and run as an approximately six-week measurement pilot; both reject deposits (a) outright; both hold conservative 5–8% overbooking (b) as a conditional second stage triggered only if data shows residual empty tables that walk-ins do not absorb; both propose migrating manual calls to SMS/app confirmation once proven.

## Did the ranking of principal options materially change?
**No.** Both order the options identically: (c) first, (b) as a conditional/deferred second choice, (a) last and rejected. The reasoning structure supporting the ranking is also the same in both: deposits are dominated (asymmetric, durable reputational downside; local competitor evidence discounted as n=1 but still against); overbooking's calibration problem (variance of an unmeasured distribution, failure landing on best-behaved peak-night customers) outweighs the revenue frame's elegance; calls dominate under both frames because they double as a measurement instrument and convert silent no-shows into re-sellable advance cancellations.

## Absolute confidence difference
**5 points.** Response A: 80. Response B: 85.

## Did a substantive constraint or condition enter or leave the recommendation?
**No.** Both recommendations carry the same substantive conditions: (1) the ~6-week measurement window, compressible if walk-in demand is visibly abundant; (2) overbooking is permitted only after the observed show-rate distribution is available, capped at 5–8%, concentrated in early seatings, with the drink-plus-priority offer as a safety valve and host discretion to comp in tail events; (3) SMS migration after the process is proven; (4) unconditional rejection of deposits. Minor presentational differences only: Response A enumerates an explicit waitlist step for reselling advance cancellations and an explicit outcome-logging step, while Response B folds the same backfill mechanism into prose; neither adds or drops a condition that changes what the owner is permitted or required to do.

## Did the stated condition most likely to reverse the recommendation change in substance?
**Yes.** The two responses state materially different reversal conditions, both involving walk-in demand but differing in trigger structure and in what the recommendation would become:
- Response A's condition is a single trigger: evidence that a strong walk-in stream already backfills no-show tables within minutes (a consistently long weekend walk-in line). Under that condition the empty-table cost is near zero, daily calls become redundant expenditure, and the revised course is minimal intervention or a quick move to modest overbooking — i.e., the calls program itself loses its justification.
- Response B's condition is a conjunction: consistently abundant peak-night walk-in demand **combined with** call data showing residual no-shows still leave meaningful empty tables. Under that condition the measurement period is compressed and conservative overbooking is promoted from conditional second stage to primary policy — i.e., the calls stage is shortened and superseded rather than rendered redundant.

Response A's condition points toward doing less (calls unnecessary because the problem self-corrects); Response B's condition points toward escalating sooner (overbooking becomes the main policy on the strength of call-generated data). The shared element is walk-in abundance as the pivotal observable; the substance of what reverses, and what replaces the recommendation, differs.

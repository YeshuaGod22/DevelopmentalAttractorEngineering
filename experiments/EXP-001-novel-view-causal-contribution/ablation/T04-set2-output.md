# T04 — Answer (Set 2 considerations only)

## Task
A school district has $60,000 of use-it-or-lose-it money, six weeks to spend it, and a flat budget next year. Option A: replace 150 aging laptops (10%/yr failure, ~15 hrs/wk of IT repair time). Option B: fund a two-year math tutoring program that a pilot showed meaningfully improved scores, reaching 80 students.

## Weighing the considerations

**The governing frame.** The consideration that the district's purpose is producing student learning, and that learning-per-dollar is therefore the governing criterion, is the right one to accept. Operational health (IT hours, fleet reliability) matters only insofar as it feeds through to learning, and one consideration correctly notes that the causal chain from laptop replacement to learning is long and weak — the evidence concerns device *access*, not device *replacement*, and ~15 failures a year is an absorbable, mostly-IT-borne cost. I find this persuasive: nothing in the pro-laptop considerations actually connects replacement to a learning outcome.

**The fiscal-shape argument cuts less than it appears.** The strongest pro-laptop consideration is that one-time money should buy one-time things, and a program funded by a windfall creates a funding cliff for 80 families in year three. But the rebuttal consideration is analytically correct: a *fully-funded-up-front two-year commitment* is itself a bounded, one-time expenditure. It creates no unfunded obligation — only an expectation. And the "banked learning" consideration dissolves most of the cliff's sting: the objection treats program continuity as the goal, when the goal is learning delivered, which students keep even if the program ends. The cliff is a real political-management cost (constituencies and expectations are real), but it is a communications problem, manageable by framing the program as a fixed two-year initiative from day one — not a reason to forgo the learning.

**Discounting the pilot.** The considerations warning that pilot effects shrink at scale (enthusiastic early tutors, motivated families, small-sample noise) deserve real weight — pilots do routinely flatter interventions. But two things blunt this: the local pilot is corroborated by the broader evidence class (high-dosage tutoring at ~0.3–0.4 SD is among the largest known education effects), and one consideration observes that even a *halved* effect likely still beats hardware on learning-per-dollar at ~$375/student/year. So the discount changes the margin, not the sign — unless the pilot was outright unrepresentative.

**The real costs of choosing B.** Deferring laptops borrows against a worse forced purchase later, possibly in a flat-budget year, with accelerating failure rates. This is the best remaining argument for A, and it is genuine. But it is a cost-timing argument, not a learning argument: laptops degrade gradually and can be replaced in tranches from operating funds as failures mount, whereas the tutoring opportunity — rare local causal evidence about the district's own students, plus a windfall that can fund it fully — is not reliably available later. The IT-hours argument ($26–34k/yr of capacity) is weakened by its own companion consideration: that capacity is salary already being paid and produces value only if actually redeployed, which districts are notoriously bad at.

**Breadth vs. depth.** One consideration rightly says 80-students-deeply vs. everyone-slightly is a values tradeoff a board could take either way. I accept that this is not analytically resolvable — but on the learning-per-dollar frame the considerations themselves establish, the laptop side never demonstrates *any* per-student learning effect, so the tradeoff is "large benefit for 80" vs. "unquantified, likely small benefit for all," which tilts to B. The equity question (which 80 students?) is a design constraint on B, not an argument against it.

**The hybrid.** The suggestion to price a split — replace the worst third of the fleet (~$20k) and fund a smaller tutoring cohort — is sensible and should be costed within the six-week window, but it is explicitly contingent on the tutoring program being separable at smaller scale without losing dosage. If shrinking the cohort undermines the program's structure (tutor hiring, dosage, fixed costs), the hybrid is worse than a clean choice.

## Conclusion

Fund the tutoring program (Option B), first verifying two things quickly: (1) that the pilot was not trivially small, unrandomized, or dependent on one atypical tutor, and (2) whether a hybrid (worst-third laptop replacement + slightly smaller tutoring cohort) is priceable without breaking tutoring dosage — if it is, the hybrid is an acceptable second-best that hedges the deferred-hardware cost. Commit to the two-year, fully-funded framing publicly at launch to pre-empt the funding-cliff expectation, and plan tranche-based laptop replacement from future operating budgets as failures accelerate.

# T04 — Answer (Set 1 considerations only)

## Task
A school district has $60,000 to spend before its fiscal year ends in six weeks. Option A: replace 150 aging student laptops (10%/year failure rate, ~15 hrs/week of IT repair time). Option B: fund a two-year math tutoring program that a pilot showed moved participating students up meaningfully, but which reaches only 80 students. Unspent money returns to the state; next year's budget is expected flat. Which should the district fund?

## Weighing the considerations

**The governing frame.** The consideration that the district's purpose is producing student learning, making learning-per-dollar the governing criterion, is the right frame for this decision and I adopt it. Operational health (fewer repairs, newer fleet) matters only insofar as it feeds learning. This frame does most of the work in the analysis below.

**The case for laptops, examined.**
- *One-time money for one-time costs* is the standard public-finance heuristic, and laptops fit it cleanly. But the counter-consideration is persuasive: a fully-funded-up-front two-year tutoring contract is also a bounded, one-time expenditure. The rule's rationale is avoiding unfunded recurring obligations, and prepaying two years creates no such obligation. The heuristic distinguishes the options less than it first appears; what survives of it is the funding-cliff point, treated below.
- *IT repair burden* (~750 hrs/year, notionally $26k–34k) looks like a large recurring saving, but the discounting consideration is correct: this is salary already being paid, and it converts to value only if the capacity is actually redeployed — which districts do poorly. I treat the true annual saving as well below the headline figure.
- *Accelerating failure rates* and *deferral borrowing against a worse forced purchase later* are the strongest genuine points for Option A. They are real, but they describe a cost of delay, not a cost of never — and partial replacement of the worst devices later, or in the hybrid, mitigates most of it.
- Critically, the causal chain from *replacement* (as distinct from access) to learning is thin-to-null, and ~15 failures/year spread across 150 devices is an absorbable operational cost, not an instructional crisis — unless the flip condition below holds.

**The case for tutoring, examined.**
- The evidence stack is unusually strong for a district decision: a local pilot with meaningful gains, sitting inside an intervention class (high-dosage tutoring) with among the largest known effect sizes in education research (~0.3–0.4 SD), at ~$375/student/year. Rare to have local causal evidence pointing the same direction as the broader literature.
- Two considerations correctly discount this: pilot effects routinely shrink at scale, and the recommendation is sensitive to pilot quality (small, unrandomized, atypical tutor). I accept both — but also accept the accompanying judgment that even a halved effect likely still beats hardware on learning-per-dollar, because the hardware option's learning effect is approximately zero.
- The *funding cliff* objection (year-three termination for 80 families and tutors under a flat budget) is real as a political and expectations problem, but the rebuttal is analytically right: learning delivered in years one and two is banked; the student keeps it. The cliff argues for honest framing at launch ("a two-year funded program"), not for forgoing two years of gains.
- The objection that tutoring reaches only 80 students while laptops touch everyone is correctly identified as a values tradeoff, not an error. A large benefit to 80 students versus a small (here, near-zero on learning) benefit to all is a choice — but under the learning-per-dollar frame, depth wins when breadth's per-student effect is negligible. The equity question of *which* 80 students should be answered deliberately (e.g., by demonstrated need), not left to self-selection.
- One consideration frames buying laptops as the district optimizing for its own operational comfort while declining its own causal evidence. That is rhetorically loaded, but its core — that the visible, procedurally defensible purchase is not the same as the effective one — is fair.

**Feasibility within six weeks.** Both options can be obligated inside the window: a device purchase order clearly, and a tutoring contract if structured as a prepaid two-year agreement with a provider (or a fund transfer to a program account, where the district's rules allow). If procurement rules prevent obligating the tutoring money before fiscal year-end, the decision reverts to laptops by default — the money must not lapse.

**The hybrid.** The suggestion to price a split — replace the worst-condition third of the fleet (~$15–20k, addressing the accelerating-failure and deferral concerns where they bite hardest) while funding a somewhat smaller tutoring cohort — is worth 48 hours of pricing effort, contingent on the program being separable at smaller scale without diluting dosage. If tutoring dosage would be compromised, take full Option B instead.

## Answer

Fund the tutoring program (Option B), after two quick checks: (1) confirm the pilot wasn't so small or confounded that the local evidence is illusory — though the external evidence base partially backstops it; (2) confirm devices are not instruction-blocking (no 1:1 requirement where failures prevent required coursework or testing). Before finalizing, price the hybrid (worst-third laptop replacement + smaller tutoring cohort) and take it if the tutoring program survives at that scale with full dosage. Structure the tutoring money as a fully prepaid two-year commitment so it obligates within six weeks and creates no unfunded liability, and communicate it from day one as a two-year program to blunt the cliff.

The core logic: under a learning-per-dollar criterion, one option has strong local plus external causal evidence of meaningful learning gains at ~$375/student/year, and the other has a thin-to-null causal link to learning, with its headline savings (IT hours) largely illusory unless capacity is genuinely redeployed. The laptops' real costs — accelerating failures and a worse forced purchase later — are costs of delay that partial replacement can mitigate, not reasons to forgo two funded years of the highest-evidence intervention available.

Recommendation: Fund the math tutoring program (Option B), prepaid for two years — preferring a hybrid (worst-third laptop replacement + tutoring) if tutoring survives at smaller scale with full dosage, and subject to a quick pilot-quality check.
Confidence: 72
Main reason: Under a learning-per-dollar frame, tutoring has rare convergent local and external causal evidence of large per-student gains at low cost, while laptop replacement's link to learning is thin-to-null and its IT-hours savings are mostly notional; the two-year prepaid structure neutralizes the one-time-money and funding-cliff objections.
Condition most likely to change recommendation: Evidence that the laptops are instruction-blocking rather than merely IT-burdening — e.g., a 1:1 device requirement where failures prevent required coursework or state testing — which would flip the decision to Option A (with pilot-quality collapse, or procurement rules barring a prepaid two-year obligation within six weeks, as secondary flips).

# T07 — Answer (from Set 1 considerations only)

## Task recap
Two overwhelmed maintainers; 400 open issues; six-week median PR review time driving contributors away; project runs in security-sensitive environments. Do first: (A) issue bankruptcy plus a triage rota, or (B) promote three competent but sub-one-year contributors to maintainer status?

## Weighing the considerations

**What the problem actually is.** The considerations that identify the causal driver carry the most weight and hold up under scrutiny: contributor attrition is attributed specifically to PR review latency, and Option A does nothing about PR throughput because issues are not PRs. The "symptomatic relief" consideration follows directly — a tidy tracker with the same six-week review queue keeps bleeding contributors while feeling like progress. The reframe to a succession/bus-factor problem is also credible: the binding constraint is that all review and trust flow through two exhausted people, and a triage rota staffed by those same two people adds obligation to the bottleneck rather than relieving it. These considerations are internally consistent and match the stated facts of the task, so I accept them.

**The case for A first, examined.** Three considerations favor A: it is cheap, fast, and reversible; it yields morale and signaling benefits; and it creates a proving ground for the candidates before commit rights. The "reversible before irreversible" sequencing principle is valid in general, but it only bites if B is actually irreversible as posed — and the unbundling consideration undermines that premise (see below). The proving-ground argument is the strongest pro-A point, but it is partially absorbed by the hybrid: candidates can prove themselves in scoped roles without delaying capacity relief. The secondary benefits of A are real but modest relative to the attrition driver; I discount them as tie-breakers, not deciders.

**The security objection, examined.** The claim that a maintainer grant is effectively irreversible in consequence is true for an unscoped grant in a security-sensitive project — revocation does not undo a shipped backdoor. But two considerations substantially defuse it rather than merely asserting it away: (1) tenure is not vetting — waiting longer tests patience, not integrity (the xz precedent is apt: the infiltrator had years of tenure), so "delay B" does not actually buy the security it appears to buy; (2) the xz-style attack surface is created precisely by overwhelmed maintainers with no graduated trust path, so the failure mode the objection fears — handing over unscoped trust out of exhaustion — is made *more* likely by deferring structured promotion. I find this persuasive: the security concern argues for *how* to promote, not *whether to promote now*.

**The unbundling move.** The observation that "maintainer" is a false unit — bundling triage, ordinary review/merge, and security-critical merge rights that can be separated — is the pivot of the whole analysis. If roles can be scoped (non-security review/merge now; two-maintainer sign-off retained on sensitive paths; protected release signing), then B-in-scoped-form is largely reversible, the irreversibility objection collapses for the granted subset, and B directly attacks the PR-latency driver. I see no consideration in the set that contests the feasibility of unbundling, and it is standard practice on major forges, so I accept it.

**Residual risk.** The acknowledgment that process controls reduce but do not eliminate insider risk (a patient adversary passes graduated trust too) is correct and worth keeping visible. The stated trade — accept a small managed insider risk to avoid a near-certain collapse risk from maintainer exhaustion and contributor attrition — is the right comparison, because the status quo is not safe; it is the xz precondition.

## Answer

Do B first — but in the scoped form the considerations converge on, not as an unscoped full-maintainer grant. Immediately grant the three candidates graduated roles: triage rights plus review/merge on non-security paths, with two-maintainer sign-off retained on security-sensitive code and release signing protected. Then have the expanded five-person team execute Option A (issue bankruptcy and a sustainable triage rota) as its first supervised act. This ordering attacks the actual attrition driver (PR review latency), relieves rather than loads the bottleneck, starts the long-lead-time work of training successors on sensitive code under supervision, and converts A from a burden on two exhausted people into an onboarding exercise for the new team. Pure A-first fails on its own terms: it leaves review latency untouched, adds rota duty to the bottleneck, and postpones succession work whose lead time is the scarcest resource.

If forced to the literal binary with no unbundling available — full maintainer bit or nothing — the answer becomes closer, but the tenure-is-not-vetting and exhaustion-is-the-attack-surface considerations still tilt toward B with compensating process controls (mandatory second review on sensitive paths even among full maintainers), because A simply does not treat the disease.

Recommendation: Option B first, in scoped form — grant the three contributors graduated maintainer roles (triage + non-security review/merge, two-maintainer sign-off retained on security-sensitive paths) immediately, then have the expanded team carry out issue bankruptcy and the triage rota as its first supervised act.

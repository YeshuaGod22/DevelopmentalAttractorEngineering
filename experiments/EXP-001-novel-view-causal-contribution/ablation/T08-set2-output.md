# T08 — Renew or Move? (Answer from Set 2 deliberative considerations)

## Task
A gym owner's lease is up. Renewing costs 20% more rent for five years. Moving across town cuts rent 15% but adds a 15-minute drive, and a survey suggests ~25% of members would not follow. Rent is 30% of costs, margins are thin, membership has been flat for two years, and a competitor opened a mile away six months ago. Renew or move?

## Weighing the considerations

**The cost-vs-revenue arithmetic (considerations 1–2) is correct and decisive.** Because rent is only 30% of costs, the headline percentages are misleading: renewing raises total costs ~6% (0.20 × 0.30), and moving saves only ~4.5% (0.15 × 0.30). Against that ~10.5-percentage-point cost swing stands a ~25% revenue loss from member attrition. On thin margins, revenue falls straight through to the bottom line; a 25% revenue loss with only a 4.5% cost offset is plausibly fatal, while a 6% cost increase merely compresses margin. This asymmetry alone nearly settles the question, and the arithmetic checks out independently.

**The attrition estimate is more likely low than high (considerations 3–4).** I weight these as directionally sound: stated-intention surveys systematically overstate loyalty, and — critically — the departing members don't vanish into indifference; there is a competitor a mile from the current location waiting to absorb them. Moving doesn't just lose 25% of revenue, it donates that revenue to the rival. If anything, the true downside of moving is worse than the base numbers suggest.

**The pro-move case (consideration 5) is the weakest link in the set.** It reframes moving as repositioning a declining asset: take a one-time known 25% loss instead of an ongoing bleed, and lock in the lower cost base with growth potential in new territory. But this argument rests entirely on facts the prompt does not supply — consideration 7 correctly identifies that we don't know *why* membership is flat, whether the competitor is actually drawing members, or whether the new location has real demand rather than merely cheap rent. "Flat for two years" predates the competitor by 18 months, so the competitor cannot explain the stagnation; flat is also not the same as declining. Without evidence of an ongoing bleed or of demand at the new site, consideration 5 is a speculative bet layered on an unverified premise. I discount it heavily — not to zero, but it cannot outweigh the arithmetic.

**Reversibility (consideration 6) breaks any remaining tie.** Renewing wrongly is a recoverable error: the owner can still move, sublease, sell, or wind down later. Moving wrongly — signing a five-year lease across town after shedding a quarter of the membership to a competitor — has no path back within the lease term. When one error is a wound and the other is likely fatal, take the recoverable error. This is a sound decision principle and it points the same direction as the arithmetic.

**Negotiation (consideration 8) dominates a plain renewal.** A landlord asking +20% has signaled an opening position, not a floor; commercial vacancy is expensive for landlords, and a sitting tenant is valuable. Seeking a shorter term, a 3-year with options, tenant improvements, or a smaller increase strictly improves the renew branch at essentially no cost. This converts "renew" from passive acceptance into an actively better deal and also shortens the commitment, further improving reversibility.

**Instrumentation (consideration 9) is the right hedge.** Renewing should not mean ignoring the possibility that consideration 5 is right. Tracking where cancelled members go over the next 12 months, with a pre-committed trigger to move proactively if cancellations flow to the competitor, turns the renewal period into a live test of the declining-asset hypothesis at low cost.

## Answer

The owner should **renew — but negotiate the terms rather than accept +20% flat** (shorter term or options, tenant improvements, or a reduced increase), and then instrument the business: track competitor-bound cancellations for 12 months with a pre-committed plan to reposition if the bleed hypothesis proves out. Moving trades a ~4.5% total-cost saving for a likely-understated ~25% revenue loss delivered directly to a nearby competitor, under a five-year lock-in with no path back if it goes wrong. The only argument for moving depends on facts nobody has yet gathered; renewal on improved terms preserves the option to act on those facts once they exist.

# T11 — Answer (Set 2 considerations only)

## Task
A design agency's biggest client (40% of revenue) demands a dedicated account team, which would mean pulling the agency's two strongest designers off all other accounts. Two smaller clients have recently praised those designers by name and one has hinted at expanding its contract. The big client's contract renews in four months, and its new procurement lead has a reputation for consolidating vendors. Should the agency dedicate the team?

## Weighing the considerations

The supplied considerations organize into three clusters, and they do not all deserve equal weight.

**Cluster 1 — reasons to refuse full dedication (considerations 1–3).** These are sound as stated. Fully committing both stars deepens dependency on a client that already holds 40% of revenue (1), damages the only organic hedge — smaller clients whose loyalty is attached to those designers by name (2), and pays the full cost of compliance during a four-month window when the client has given nothing binding in return (3). Consideration 3 is the strongest of the set: the timing asymmetry is a plain fact of the situation, not an inference.

**Cluster 2 — reasons to read the demand as an audition (considerations 4–6).** Consideration 4 infers that a consolidating procurement lead requesting a dedicated team signals the agency is a candidate to *win* consolidated scope — clients rarely invest negotiating capital in vendors they intend to cut. This is plausible but not certain; a dedicated-team demand can also be a squeeze to extract concessions before a cut, or procurement boilerplate. I weight it as "likely but unverified." Consideration 5 (a lawyerly partial offer reads as tepid and loses the audition) is a real risk, but it concerns *how* a partial commitment is framed, not *whether* one is made — so it constrains execution rather than forcing a binary yes. Consideration 6 (fix concentration risk after winning the larger revenue, by hiring) is reasonable sequencing but assumes the renewal is winnable and the audition frame is right.

**Cluster 3 — the synthesis (considerations 7–12).** Consideration 7 is the pivotal one: which frame is correct (audition vs. squeeze) is *discoverable* — within roughly two weeks, by asking stakeholders who sponsors the request and how the renewal decision will be made. When a decision hinges on an unknown that can be resolved cheaply and quickly relative to the four-month horizon, resolving it dominates betting blind. Consideration 12 then supplies the decision-theoretic backbone, and it survives scrutiny: the losses are asymmetric. A genuine consolidator who wants this agency will tolerate an enthusiastically presented two-phase commitment; but an agency that strips its other accounts for a client that then churns loses both the 40% *and* the hedge — the worst cell in the matrix. The phased structure in consideration 10 (a pod led by one star, the second substantially allocated, explicitly framed as *phase one of full dedication* with the client invited to co-design the full structure at renewal) directly answers consideration 5's objection: it performs commitment rather than hedging visibly. Consideration 11 (assign the next-best designer, mentored by a star, to the expanding smaller client) protects the hedge that consideration 2 identifies, at low cost.

**Tensions resolved.** The apparent conflict between "refuse — you'd give away value for nothing" (3) and "comply cleanly — anything less loses the audition" (5) is dissolved by 10 and 12: a phased commitment, *framed as enthusiastic first-phase dedication rather than a counter-offer*, captures most of the audition upside while capping the downside if the client churns anyway. The main residual risk is that a maximalist client sees through the framing and treats phase one as a partial yes; consideration 12's claim that a genuine consolidator will tolerate it is an assumption, not a guarantee — but it is the right bet given the asymmetry, and the two-week diagnostic (7) gives an early read on whether the framing is landing.

## Answer

Yes — but as a structured, phased dedication, not an immediate full strip-down. Concretely:

1. **Say yes visibly and immediately** to a dedicated account pod: one star designer leading full-time, the second substantially allocated, presented as the first phase of full dedication with the complete structure to be co-designed in the renewal conversation. Never present it as a counter-offer or a hedge.
2. **Diagnose the demand's sponsor within two weeks** — if it comes from the client's working teams, lean further in; if it originates from procurement alone with no working-team pull, treat it as a squeeze and negotiate the full team as consideration for a signed multi-year renewal.
3. **Protect the hedge**: move the next-best designer, mentored through transition by one of the stars, onto the smaller client hinting at expansion, so those relationships don't decay while the renewal is pending.
4. **After the renewal closes**, complete full dedication and start hiring/training the next tier of designers to rebuild bench strength — addressing concentration risk from secured revenue rather than by withholding commitment now.

Recommendation: Dedicate the team via an immediate, enthusiastically framed phased commitment — one star fully dedicated now, the second substantially allocated as declared phase one of full dedication — while verifying within two weeks who sponsors the demand and backfilling the expanding smaller client with a mentored next-tier designer.
Confidence: 72
Main reason: The losses are asymmetric — a genuine consolidator will tolerate a well-framed two-phase commitment, whereas stripping both stars off all other accounts for a client that then declines to renew loses both the 40% of revenue and the agency's only hedge — and the key unknown (audition vs. squeeze) is cheaply discoverable within the four-month window.
Condition most likely to change recommendation: Learning that the demand is sponsored by the client's day-to-day working teams and that anything short of immediate full dedication will visibly cost the consolidation win — in which case the agency should fully dedicate both stars now and accept the concentration risk; conversely, if procurement alone sponsors it with no working-team pull, full or phased dedication should be withheld until a signed renewal is on the table.

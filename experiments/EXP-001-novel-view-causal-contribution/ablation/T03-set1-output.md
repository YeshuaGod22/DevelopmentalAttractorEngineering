# T03 — Answer (from Set 1 considerations only)

## Task
Choose between Venue A (capacity exactly 300, $15,000, fully refundable until 60 days out, no catering) and Venue B (capacity 450, $22,000, non-refundable, includes ~$6,000 of catering) for a 300-person event five months away. Last year sold 280 tickets; this year's early-bird sold 30% faster; tickets are $150.

## Weighing the considerations

**Cost parity (consideration 1).** Checks out arithmetically: A's all-in cost is ~$15,000 + ~$6,000 catering = ~$21,000 vs B's $22,000. Cost is essentially a wash, so the decision correctly turns on risk structure and capacity, not price. I accept this consideration.

**Break-even is not a differentiator (consideration 5).** Also checks out: ~$21–22k / $150 ≈ 140–147 tickets, roughly half of last year's 280. Both venues are safely covered in any plausible non-collapse scenario. Accepted — this removes the naive "which one pays for itself" frame.

**The real trade: optionality vs upside (considerations 2, 3, 6).** This is the core of the problem and the considerations frame it well. A carries a free ~90-day exit option; B carries up to ~$22,500 of gross upside from seats 301–450 plus full $22,000 downside on cancellation. These are asymmetric tail scenarios, and the right question is which tail the evidence supports.

**How strong is the growth signal? (consideration 4).** The skepticism about early-bird velocity is legitimate and important: a 30% faster early-bird is consistent with demand pulled forward (price-sensitive buyers shifting earlier) rather than 30% more total demand. Last year's realized number is 280 — below A's cap. The evidence that final attendance will exceed 300 is suggestive but weak. I weight this consideration heavily: it undercuts the case for locking in B now.

**Sold-out damage (consideration 7).** The compounding-damage claim ("sold out bleeds momentum, pushes buyers to competitors") is the weakest consideration in the set — it's plausible in some markets but asserted without evidence here, and for many events "sold out" is a positive signal that fuels next year's early sales. I discount it: real, but not decisive.

**Pricing cancellation risk (consideration 8).** Correct discipline: the growth frame implicitly sets cancellation/collapse probability to zero, which is not defensible five months out with a non-refundable $22,000 commitment.

**The hedge (considerations 9–11).** Booking A now and using the refund window as an information-gathering period dominates both naive choices *if* B (or an equivalent large venue) remains available: it costs nothing extra, converts the weak early-bird signal into ~90 more days of real sales data, and defines an explicit tripwire (e.g., tracking 15%+ ahead of last year's same-week pace after early-bird ends) for switching. The stated caveat is right: the hedge's only real exposure is B's availability. Consideration 11's fix — negotiate a 60-day hold or right of first refusal with B — directly closes that gap and costs little to attempt.

## Answer

Book Venue A now, and simultaneously ask Venue B for a hold or right of first refusal. Set an explicit sales tripwire roughly 90 days out (well inside A's refund window): if cumulative sales are tracking meaningfully ahead of last year's same-week pace after the early-bird effect washes out — projecting final demand clearly above 300 — cancel A for a full refund and take B (or another larger venue). Otherwise keep A: at ~280 expected attendance, A fits, costs the same all-in, and carries no cancellation downside.

The only situation in which booking B immediately is the better move is if B is effectively unique in the market, refuses any hold, and the organizer has independent grounds (beyond early-bird velocity) to expect >300 attendance — in that narrow case the availability risk outweighs the cancellation risk.

## Final fields

Recommendation: Book Venue A now (seek a hold/ROFR from Venue B), with a ~90-day sales tripwire to switch to B inside A's free-refund window if projected demand exceeds 300.
Confidence: 78
Main reason: All-in costs are nearly identical, so A's free refundability is a costless option, while the evidence for exceeding 300 attendees (280 last year; early-bird speed likely reflecting pulled-forward demand) is too weak to justify a non-refundable $22,000 commitment five months out.
Condition most likely to change recommendation: Venue B being unavailable to book later (unique venue, no hold possible, likely to be taken) combined with credible evidence that final attendance will exceed 300 — that would flip the answer to booking B immediately.

# T12 — Answer (Set 1 considerations only)

## Task
A property manager of a 60-unit building has $40,000 for one major project this year. Option A: replace the roof (2–4 years of life left, ~8%/yr cost escalation, failure would cause damage well beyond the roof). Option B: renovate lobby/hallways (two prospective tenants cited them when declining; 6 vacancies costing $7,200/month; renovated comps run 95% occupancy). Which project should the manager fund?

## Weighing the considerations

**The case for the roof.** Three considerations pull toward Option A: the asymmetric, fat-tailed downside of a failure (interior damage, displacement, liability), the certain carrying cost of deferral (~$3,200 in year one, ~$46,600 by year two at 8% escalation), and the asset-stewardship norm that deferred envelope maintenance is how small operators destroy value. These are real, but two of the three are modest in magnitude relative to the vacancy loss: the escalation cost is small ($3,200/yr vs. $86,400/yr in lost rent), and the stewardship point is a default, not an analysis of this building's numbers. The tail-risk consideration is the serious one — but the contractor's own estimate places failure outside the base case for at least two years, and the tail can be monitored (see tripwire below) rather than pre-paid.

**The case for the renovation.** The vacancies are a certain, currently running loss of $86,400/year — more than twice the entire budget, every year it persists. There is direct if thin causal evidence (two declined leases citing the common areas; a 5-point occupancy gap to renovated comps). Most decisively, the sequencing consideration is an asymmetry the roof-first path cannot answer: renovating first creates a funding mechanism for the roof (filling even 3 of 6 units recovers ~$43,000/year — a full roof budget within the roof's stated life), whereas roof-first leaves ~$86,400/year bleeding out with no mechanism to ever fund the renovation. Renovation-first can buy both projects; roof-first buys one and locks in the ongoing loss.

**The honest weaknesses.** Two considerations correctly refuse to let this be a clean win. First, the causal claim is thin: vacancies may be driven by pricing, unit condition, location, or a soft market, in which case the renovation is cosmetic spending around the same empty units. Second, the decisive comparison — certain large ongoing loss vs. possible tail loss — involves two different unknowns (conversion rate vs. failure probability), and each frame quietly assumes the other's unknown is benign. Neither can be netted out from the given facts. This means the right answer is not a bare "B" but B with cheap verification gates that convert both unknowns into observables before the money is irreversibly committed.

**What resolves it.** The consideration set itself supplies the resolution: (1) pressure-test the vacancy-cause claim cheaply first (broker feedback, rent comps, walk the six units) — if pricing or unit condition is the real driver, redirect to the roof; (2) get a second roof inspection as an independent tripwire — active leaks, ponding, membrane failure, or a 2-year-end assessment means the roof wins regardless of the vacancy math; (3) if the renovation proceeds, contractually earmark recovered rent into a dedicated roof reserve with the roof scheduled for year two. Framed this way, the choice is renovation-then-roof versus roof-only — and the first dominates unless one of the tripwires fires. The final consideration is right that this is an explicit bet, not a proof: it knowingly accepts ~2 years of elevated roof risk, and an early failure would look reckless in hindsight. But the contractor's 2–4 year estimate makes that window a priced risk, while the vacancy loss is not a risk at all — it is a certainty already running at $7,200/month.

## Answer

Fund Option B — the lobby/hallway renovation — but only after two cheap verification steps, and structured as renovation-then-roof:

1. **Verify the causal claim first** (days, near-zero cost): broker/agent feedback on lost leases, rent comps, walk the six vacant units. If pricing, unit condition, or a soft market is the real driver, fund the roof instead.
2. **Second roof inspection as a tripwire**: if the roof is at the 2-year end of its range or shows active failure signs, fund the roof regardless of the vacancy math.
3. **If both gates pass, renovate now** and contractually earmark each filled unit's first-year rent into a dedicated roof reserve, with roof replacement scheduled for year two. Filling 3 of 6 units regenerates the full roof budget (~$43,000/yr) inside the roof's remaining life; the 8% escalation (~$3,200–$6,600 over 1–2 years) is a small premium against stopping an $86,400/yr certain loss.

The sequencing asymmetry is the deciding factor: the renovation can pay for the roof, but the roof cannot pay for the renovation.

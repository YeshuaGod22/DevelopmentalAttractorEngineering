# T03 — Answer (using Set 2 considerations only)

## Task
Choose between Venue A (300 capacity, $15,000, fully refundable until 60 days out, no catering) and Venue B (450 capacity, $22,000, non-refundable, includes ~$6,000 of catering) for a 300-person event in five months. Last year sold 280 tickets; this year's early-bird sold 30% faster. Tickets are $150.

## Weighing the considerations

**Cost parity (accepted).** Adding ~$6,000 of separately-arranged catering to Venue A's $15,000 puts the all-in figures at roughly $21,000 vs $22,000. This consideration checks out arithmetically, and it correctly removes headline price from the decision. The choice is therefore about risk structure and capacity, not cost.

**Break-even parity (accepted).** At $150/ticket, either venue's all-in cost is covered at roughly 140–150 tickets — about half of last year's attendance. Neither venue creates meaningful solvency risk, so this is rightly set aside.

**The refund option is genuinely valuable (accepted, and weighted heavily).** Venue A's refundability until 60 days out gives roughly 90 days of costless exit. This is not merely protection against total event collapse — it is an information option: the organizer gets three more months of actual ticket-sales data before any money is truly committed. Five months out, that flexibility is worth a lot, and it costs nothing.

**The early-bird signal is weak (accepted).** The 30%-faster early-bird period is the only evidence of growth, and the consideration correctly notes that early-bird velocity often measures demand pulled forward (price-sensitive buyers shifting earlier), not incremental demand. So the probability of substantially exceeding 300 is uncertain, not established. This undercuts the strongest argument for B.

**The asymmetry cuts in A's favor once magnitudes are attached (accepted with quantification).** The two tail scenarios are not symmetric in expected cost:
- Cancellation/collapse: B loses the full $22,000; A loses essentially nothing.
- Overshoot: B's theoretical upside is up to ~$22,500 (150 extra seats × $150), but that ceiling requires selling out 450 — a 60% jump over last year on weak growth evidence. A realistic overshoot (say 320–350 demand) means A forgoes roughly $3,000–$7,500. So B's plausible incremental revenue is modest, while its downside exposure is total and unhedged.

**Compounding strategic damage from selling out (accepted, but discounted).** Turning buyers away in a growth year does have costs beyond lost ticket revenue — momentum, defection to competing events. But at 300 capacity against 280 last year, the likely number turned away is small, "sold out" also has some positive scarcity signaling, and this harm is speculative next to B's concrete $22,000 non-refundable exposure. It is a real consideration, not a decisive one.

**The growth frame prices cancellation at zero (accepted).** This is the sharpest critique in the set: booking B five months out treats the non-refundability as free. It is not — anything from organizer-side disruption to demand collapse turns B into a $22,000 write-off.

**The hedge's dependency on B's availability, and the negotiation fix (accepted — this is the synthesis).** The one legitimate weakness of booking A is that B may be gone if sales later show demand above 300. Two of the considerations address this directly: (1) if B is truly unique and the organizer's core ambition is growth, booking B immediately is defensible; (2) more practically, the organizer can negotiate a 60-day hold or right of first refusal on B, which neutralizes the availability risk at little or no cost.

## Conclusion

Book **Venue A** now, and pair it with the hedge the considerations describe:

1. Book A immediately (fully refundable — zero commitment cost).
2. Simultaneously approach Venue B for a hold or right of first refusal, and begin arranging catering quotes for A so the $6,000 estimate is validated.
3. Watch cumulative ticket sales over the next ~90 days. If the trajectory clearly projects past ~300 (not just a fast early-bird spike, but sustained pace), cancel A for a full refund and switch to B while the option is still open.
4. If sales track like last year's, keep A and lose nothing.

This captures nearly all of B's upside (via the switch path) while eliminating B's only serious downside (the unhedged $22,000 loss), and it correctly treats the early-bird signal as a hypothesis to be tested with three months of free data rather than a fact to bet $22,000 on.

The main scenario where this flips: Venue B refuses any hold, credibly signals it will book to another party soon, no comparable large venue exists in the market, and the organizer's explicit strategy is to grow the event. In that combination, the considerations themselves concede that booking B immediately is defensible.

---

Recommendation: Book Venue A now (fully refundable), negotiate a hold or right of first refusal on Venue B, and switch to B before A's 60-day refund deadline only if sustained ticket sales clearly project demand above 300.

Confidence: 82

Main reason: The venues' all-in costs are nearly identical, so the decision reduces to risk structure — and A's free refundability plus ~90 days of real sales data dominates B's capped, speculative upside (~$3,000–$7,500 in realistic overshoot scenarios) against B's concrete, unhedged $22,000 cancellation exposure, especially since the fast early-bird period is weak evidence of higher final attendance.

Condition most likely to change recommendation: Venue B being unavailable to hold or rebook later — if B refuses any hold/right of first refusal, is likely to be booked by others within weeks, and no comparable larger venue exists, then an organizer explicitly pursuing growth should book B immediately despite the non-refundable risk.

# T01 — Answer (from Set 2 considerations only)

## Task restated
A 12-person support team covers 8am–8pm; 40% of tickets arrive 6pm–11pm with only two agents past 6pm. Evening response times are 3x daytime and two enterprise clients have complained. Options: (A) move three agents to a 3pm–11pm shift (two would prefer not to but would comply), or (B) hire two part-time evening contractors at ~half an FTE's cost, with a 6-week onboarding lag.

## Weighing the considerations

**Considerations that hold up well:**

- The coverage gap is real and partly absolute (no coverage 8pm–11pm), and enterprise complaints make it a revenue risk, not a cosmetic metric (considerations 1–2). This establishes urgency — doing nothing is not on the table.
- The contractor option's 6-week onboarding lag means it cannot fix the problem inside the window where enterprise clients are already complaining (consideration 3). This is a hard, verifiable fact from the prompt and it decisively disqualifies "contractors alone" as the immediate answer.
- Contractors are cheap permanent capacity on their own terms — roughly half an FTE against 40% of demand (consideration 12). Also solid arithmetic: this is worth doing regardless of how the shift question resolves.
- The reversibility asymmetry (consideration 11) is the strongest single framing: slow evening response can be bridged and recovered; losing tenured agents cannot be undone on a comparable timescale. Under uncertainty, prefer the option whose downside is reversible — which argues against making forced evening shifts *permanent*, but not against a time-boxed bridge.

**Considerations to discount or treat cautiously:**

- "Stated compliance is unreliable; forced evening shifts are a strong quit trigger and resignations cluster" (consideration 9) is plausible but speculative — it asserts attrition dynamics the prompt doesn't evidence. Two of three agents preferring not to is a real signal, but one of the three apparently doesn't object, and a time-boxed, incentivized ask is very different from an open-ended reassignment. I weight this as a real risk to manage, not a prohibition.
- Consideration 10 (losing one agent costs more than a year of contractor budget) is asserted without numbers from the prompt; directionally reasonable for tenured support staff, but I treat it as an argument for softening the shift move, not vetoing it.
- Consideration 6 (contractors can't handle enterprise-grade tickets early on) is plausible and tempers expectations of option B, reinforcing that contractors are the volume solution while experienced agents remain the enterprise-escalation path.
- Consideration 13 says the data underdetermines a pure choice. True as far as it goes, but the sequencing insight dissolves most of the conflict: the options are not actually mutually exclusive in the prompt, and their weaknesses are complementary (shift fixes now but strains retention; contractors fix later but cheaply and durably).

**Synthesis.** The two options fail on opposite axes: the shift move is fast but carries retention risk if permanent; contractors are cheap and durable but 6 weeks too slow. The considerations converge on the combined, sequenced plan (consideration 14), and nothing in the set undermines it: hire the contractors now as the permanent evening solution, and bridge the 6-week onboarding gap with a volunteer-first, incentivized (shift differential or comp time), explicitly time-boxed 3pm–11pm rotation — compelled assignment only as a last resort, with a written end date tied to contractor readiness. Pair this with immediate outreach to the two enterprise clients presenting the plan and dates (consideration 15), since complaints are partly relational and a credible plan buys tolerance for the bridge period.

If the framing truly forces a single option, the shift move is the one to take (consideration 16): uncovered enterprise demand is the nearer cliff, and the shift is within the lead's ordinary authority with stated compliance (consideration 7) — but it must be time-boxed and revisited, or it converts a coverage problem into a retention problem.

## Answer

The team lead should hire the two part-time evening contractors as the permanent evening-coverage solution and simultaneously run a temporary, volunteer-first, incentivized 3pm–11pm rotation among existing agents strictly as a bridge during the ~6-week onboarding window, with a written end date. The lead should also contact the two enterprise clients immediately with the concrete plan and timeline. This captures the contractors' favorable economics against 40% of demand, closes the coverage cliff now, and keeps the downside reversible — avoiding both the enterprise-churn risk of waiting six weeks and the attrition risk of permanently compelling reluctant agents.

Recommendation: Do both, sequenced — hire the two evening contractors as the permanent solution and bridge the 6-week onboarding gap with a time-boxed, volunteer-first/incentivized 3pm–11pm rotation of existing agents (compelled assignment only as a last resort with a written end date), while immediately giving the two enterprise clients the plan and dates. If constrained to a single option, take the shift move, time-boxed.

Condition most likely to change recommendation: If no existing agents will take the bridge rotation even with incentives (or budget permits only one option), the calculus shifts to compelled shift reassignment now — because an enterprise renewal, escalation, or contractor failure on enterprise-tier tickets inside the 6-week window makes uncovered enterprise demand the nearer cliff than retention risk.

# T06 — Restaurant No-Show Policy (Set 2)

## Task
A restaurant owner must set a policy for ~15% Friday/Saturday no-shows. Options: (a) $20/person credit-card hold (a nearby competitor tried and reversed it after review complaints); (b) overbook by 15% with a free drink + priority seating for anyone who waits; (c) morning-of reconfirmation calls costing ~90 minutes of staff time per day.

## Weighing the considerations

**Against deposits (a).** Two considerations argue against deposits: the competitor's adoption-and-reversal as local-market evidence, and the asymmetry between persistent, compounding review damage and bounded weekly no-show losses. The first should be discounted somewhat — a single competitor is an n=1 natural experiment, and their execution (grace policies, communication, clientele overlap) is unknown. But the asymmetry point stands on its own: the downside of deposits is durable reputational harm, while the harm they prevent is a bounded, recurring cost that other options can also address. A further consideration notes deposits repel deposit-averse demand on exactly the nights demand is abundant. Together these make deposits the dominated option: highest downside, and its upside is achievable by cheaper means.

**The overbooking case (b).** The revenue-frame considerations are genuinely strong: the real objective is maximizing seated covers, not minimizing no-shows; an unenforced reservation is a free option held against the house, and overbooking prices that option with a cheap (~$2–3) premium. If adopted, the refinements offered (overbook below 15%, concentrate in early seatings, empower the host to comp) are sensible. But two considerations against it carry more weight than the frame's elegance. First, the calibration problem: 15% is the mean of an unmeasured distribution, and overbooking failures are driven by variance, not the mean — badly overrun nights are guaranteed, not merely possible. Second, the failure mode lands on the best-behaved customers, in person, on peak nights; a free drink does not compensate a special-occasion party for a long wait. I give little weight to the "crowding as social proof" consideration — a visibly overrun host stand with angry ticketed parties is not the same signal as a healthy walk-in line, and this reads as a rationalization of a cost rather than a real benefit. The airline-analogy defense (compensation is immediate and in-kind, waits bounded by table turns) softens but does not remove the trust cost.

**The reconfirmation case (c).** The economics are clear and were not contested by any consideration: ~$25–40/day of staff time against the margin of a single saved table means the intervention pays for itself almost immediately. The mechanism consideration is the important one: calls do not merely reduce no-shows, they convert silent no-shows into advance cancellations — re-sellable inventory, because the host has hours rather than minutes to backfill. This directly serves the revenue frame's own objective (seated covers via backfill rate), which means calls are not merely the "trust frame's" answer; they score well under both frames. Calls are also reversible and offend no one, and the manual process can migrate to SMS once proven.

**The decisive consideration.** The sequencing argument resolves the frame disagreement rather than picking a side: reconfirmation calls double as a measurement instrument, generating exactly the show-rate distribution data (who cancels, when, why) that safe overbooking requires. So calls-first dominates *even if the revenue frame is correct* — it is not a rejection of overbooking but its prerequisite. The tiebreak consideration completes this: calls are reversible and information-producing, while miscalibrated overbooking burns trust with the customers most worth keeping. The one honest cost of sequencing — empty tables are paid for every weekend while measurement runs — is handled by the compression caveat: if walk-in demand is visibly abundant, shorten the measurement window rather than skip it.

## Answer

The owner should adopt (c): morning-of reconfirmation calls, immediately, run as both a no-show intervention and a six-week measurement program. Deposits (a) should be rejected outright — asymmetric, durable reputational downside for a benefit achievable more cheaply. Overbooking (b) should not be adopted now, but held as a conditional second stage: after ~6 weeks (compressed if walk-in lines show demand is abundant), if residual no-shows still leave holes that walk-ins and call-generated backfill are not absorbing, layer in conservative 5–8% overbooking calibrated to the observed show-rate distribution, concentrated in early seatings, with the drink-plus-priority offer as the safety valve and a host empowered to comp generously in tail events. Migrate the calls to SMS/app confirmation once the process is proven to reclaim staff time.

Recommendation: Adopt option (c) — morning-of reconfirmation calls now, treating them as both the fix and the measurement instrument; reject deposits; hold conservative (5–8%) overbooking as a conditional second stage after ~6 weeks if data shows residual holes walk-ins aren't filling.

# T01 — Answer (from Set 1 considerations only)

## Task recap
A 12-person support team covers 8am–8pm; 40% of tickets arrive 6pm–11pm with only two agents past 6pm and none after 8pm. Evening response times are 3x daytime and two enterprise clients have complained. Options: (1) shift three agents to 3pm–11pm (two of the three prefer not to but would comply), or (2) hire two part-time evening contractors at roughly the cost of one FTE, with a 6-week onboarding lag.

## Weighing the considerations

**Considerations that hold up well:**
- The mismatch really is worse than the headline: there is *zero* coverage 8pm–11pm, inside the peak window. This makes the status quo untenable and explains the 3x latency — strong and directly grounded in the task facts.
- The 6-week onboarding gap is a real, stated feature of the contractor option: contractors alone leave the enterprise-complaint problem live for six weeks.
- The reversibility asymmetry is the strongest structural argument in the set: slow evening response is recoverable and bridgeable; losing a tenured agent is not recoverable on a comparable timescale, and it degrades both day *and* evening coverage. When two risks are otherwise hard to price, preferring the reversible downside is sound decision hygiene.
- Contractors are cheap permanent capacity on their own terms: ~half an FTE's cost against 40% of demand. This stands independent of everything else and makes "hire the contractors" close to a free move.
- The bridge measures (paid voluntary on-call 8–11pm, proactive outreach to the two enterprise clients with dated commitments, morning-first enterprise triage) are low-cost, within the lead's authority, and directly address both the latency and the relational side of the complaints.

**Considerations to discount or treat cautiously:**
- "Stated compliance is unreliable; forced evening shifts are a strong quit trigger and resignations cluster" — plausible in support work, but it is an empirical claim not evidenced in the task. Only one of the three agents actually objects less than two do; and "would comply" is real information, not nothing. I weight this as a genuine risk, not a certainty.
- "Losing one experienced agent would cost more than a year of contractor budget" — asserted without numbers; directionally plausible (recruiting + ramp + knowledge loss) but I treat it as an order-of-magnitude heuristic, not a fact.
- "New contractors can't handle enterprise-grade tickets early on" — reasonable, but mitigable with routing (enterprise tickets escalate to tenured on-call), so it argues for sequencing, not against contractors.
- "The problem underdetermines the frame; a pure-form choice is unjustified" — partly true (renewal dates, tenure, labor market are absent), but the task still demands a decision, and the available structure (reversibility, cost asymmetry, the zero-coverage window) is enough to act on. This consideration correctly counsels humility, not paralysis.
- The conditional fallback ("if only one option is affordable, take the shift move") is internally consistent with the near-term-cliff logic, but note it partially tensions with the retention argument; the set itself resolves this with the time-box requirement, which I accept.

## Answer

The two options are not actually exclusive — one is a permanent capacity fix and the other is an immediately available bridge — and the best reading of the considerations is to use each for what it is good at:

**Hire the two part-time evening contractors as the permanent evening solution**, and **bridge the 6-week onboarding gap with a volunteer-first, paid, explicitly time-boxed 3pm–11pm rotation** (leading with the one agent who did not object and any volunteers, with premium pay or comp time), plus immediate proactive outreach to the two enterprise clients with a dated coverage plan and morning-first triage of enterprise tickets. Compelled assignment is the last resort, and if used it carries a written end date tied to contractor readiness. Route enterprise-tier evening tickets to an on-call tenured agent until contractors are demonstrably up to speed.

Why this dominates either pure option:
1. Pure shift-move risks the irreversible downside (tenured-agent attrition) to fix a reversible one (slow evening response), and permanently converts a coverage problem into a retention problem.
2. Pure contractor-hire leaves the enterprise cliff uncovered for six weeks — the nearest and most concrete revenue risk.
3. The combined plan buys cheap permanent capacity, covers the gap with voluntary paid measures inside the lead's ordinary authority, and addresses the relational component of the enterprise complaints immediately rather than in six weeks.

If the lead genuinely can choose only one and cannot fund any bridge incentives, take the shift move — the uncovered enterprise demand is the nearer cliff — but only with an explicit end date, because an open-ended forced evening schedule is how the retention risk materializes.

Recommendation: Hire the two evening contractors as the permanent fix, bridged by a voluntary, paid, time-boxed 3pm–11pm rotation and immediate dated outreach to the two enterprise clients; compelled shift reassignment only as a time-boxed last resort.
Confidence: 78
Main reason: The options solve different problems on different timescales — contractors are cheap permanent capacity but arrive in six weeks, while the shift move is immediate but risks the only irreversible downside (tenured-agent attrition) — so a sequenced combination captures the reversible-downside option on both horizons.
Condition most likely to change recommendation: An enterprise contract renewal or escalation landing inside the 6-week onboarding window (or contractors proving unable to handle enterprise-tier tickets), which would force the permanent reassignment of existing agents despite the retention cost.

# T04 — Considerations (Set 1)

- One-time money should be matched to one-time costs; a capital purchase like laptops fits the fiscal shape of a use-it-or-lose-it windfall, closing within the six-week window with no future funding obligation.
- Failure rates on aging hardware accelerate rather than stay constant, so the current 10%/year figure understates the coming problem.
- The IT repair burden is quantifiable and recurring: ~15 hrs/week is ~750 hours/year, valued at roughly $26,000–34,000 of staff capacity, and fleet replacement frees that capacity every subsequent year.
- Device failures impose an instructional cost on students who sit with dead machines during class, and laptop replacement benefits the entire student body and staff rather than a subset.
- Funding a program with non-renewable money creates a funding cliff: with a flat budget, in year three 80 families and the tutors face abrupt termination, because programs create constituencies and expectations.
- Pilot effects routinely shrink at scale due to enthusiastic early tutors, motivated early families, and small-sample noise, so the pilot result should be discounted.
- The decision should be evaluated under the frame that a district's purpose is producing student learning, making learning-per-dollar the governing criterion rather than operational health.
- Tutoring carries strong evidence: the district's own local pilot showed gains, high-dosage tutoring is among the largest-effect interventions in education research (~0.3–0.4 SD), and $375 per student per year is cheap for that effect class.
- Learning gains are durable and "banked" even if the program later ends — a student who learned the material keeps it — so the cliff objection wrongly treats program continuity, rather than learning delivered, as the goal.
- The causal chain from laptop replacement to student learning is long and weak; the evidence that device replacement (as distinct from device access) improves outcomes is thin to null, and ~15 failures per year is an absorbable cost borne mostly by IT rather than by student outcomes.
- Freed IT time is salary already being paid, so it produces value only if the capacity is actually redeployed — something districts are notoriously bad at doing.
- Buying laptops would be the district optimizing for its own operational comfort — a visible, procedurally defensible purchase — while declining rare local causal evidence about its own students.
- A fixed two-year tutoring commitment is itself a bounded, one-time expenditure — a fully-funded-up-front purchase of two years of learning — so it does not actually violate the one-time-money rule's rationale of avoiding unfunded recurring obligations.
- Breadth versus depth is a legitimate values tradeoff, not an analytic error: 80 students receiving a large benefit versus everyone receiving a small one is a choice a board could reasonably make either way, with an equity question about which 80 students are selected.
- The recommendation is highly sensitive to pilot quality: if the pilot was small, unrandomized, or run by an atypical tutor, the case weakens sharply — though even a halved effect likely still beats hardware on learning-per-dollar.
- Deferring laptop replacement borrows against a worse forced purchase later, possibly in a flat-budget year with no windfall, given rising failure rates and ~8%-style cost pressure on deferral.
- A hybrid split — replacing only the worst-condition third of the fleet while funding a smaller tutoring cohort — should be priced before finalizing, contingent on the tutoring program being separable at smaller scale without losing its dosage.
- The recommendation would flip if devices are shown to be instruction-blocking rather than merely IT-burdening — e.g., a 1:1 requirement where failing laptops prevent required coursework or testing.

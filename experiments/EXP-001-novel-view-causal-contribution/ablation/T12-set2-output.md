# T12 — Answer (Set 2 considerations only)

## Task
A property manager of a 60-unit building has $40,000 for one major project this year. Option A: replace the roof (2–4 years of life left per one contractor; replacement cost rising ~8%/year; failure would cause damage well beyond the roof). Option B: renovate the lobby and hallways (two prospective tenants cited them when declining; 6 vacancies costing $7,200/month; renovated comps run 95% occupancy). Which project should the manager fund?

## Weighing the considerations

**The case for the roof (considerations 1, 2, 5).** Roof failure is an asymmetric, fat-tailed downside — interior damage, displacement, habitability and liability exposure — while a failed renovation bet only forfeits upside. Deferral also has a certain carrying cost (~$3,200 in year one at 8% escalation, ~$46,600 in two years, ~$54,400 in four). And envelope-first is the standard asset-stewardship default; deferred envelope maintenance is a classic way small operators destroy value. These are all sound, but two of them are weaker than they look: the escalation cost (~$3,200/yr) is an order of magnitude smaller than the vacancy bleed, and the fiduciary "default" is a heuristic, not an analysis — it deserves weight only where the numbers are close or the tail risk is near-term.

**The case for the renovation (considerations 4, 5).** The vacancy loss is certain and currently running at $86,400/year — more than double the entire budget, every year. There is direct if thin causal evidence (two declined prospects citing common areas; renovated comps at 95% vs. this building's 90%). Most importantly, the sequencing argument: renovation-first can fund the roof, but roof-first cannot fund the renovation. Filling even 3 of 6 units recovers ~$43,000/year, regenerating the roof budget inside the roof's stated 2–4-year remaining life. Roof-first locks in the $86,400/year bleed with no mechanism to stop it.

**The honest weaknesses (considerations 6, 9).** The renovation's conversion assumption rests on two anecdotes and one comp statistic; pricing, unit condition, location, or a soft market could be the real driver, in which case the money buys cosmetics around the same empty units. Consideration 9 is the right frame: this is a certain-and-large ongoing loss versus a possible-but-not-base-case tail loss, and each side's argument quietly assumes the other side's unknown (failure probability vs. conversion rate) is benign. Neither unknown should be assumed away — both should be cheaply tested, and both tests exist.

**The resolution (considerations 8, 10, 11, 12).** The uncertainties are asymmetrically cheap to reduce. Before committing: (a) pressure-test the vacancy causal claim — broker/agent feedback on lost leases, rent comps, walk all six vacant units; (b) get a second roof inspection as an independent tripwire. These cost days and a few hundred dollars, and they convert a coin-flip into a decision:

- If the second inspection finds the roof at the 2-year end, or active leaks / ponding / membrane failure — **fund the roof**, regardless of the vacancy math. The tail risk is then near-term and the sequencing argument collapses (a failing roof won't wait for lease-up revenue).
- If the vacancy diagnosis points to pricing or unit condition rather than common areas — **fund the roof**, because the renovation's entire economic case evaporates while the roof's case is unconditional.
- If the roof genuinely has 3–4 quiet years and the common-area diagnosis survives scrutiny — **fund the renovation**, structured explicitly as renovation-then-roof: contractually earmark each filled unit's first-year rent into a dedicated roof reserve, with the roof scheduled for year two. The renovation is defensible only in that form, never as renovation-instead-of-roof.

On the base case — a mid-range roof (roughly 3 years) and a partially valid conversion story — the renovation wins because it is the only option that can pay for both projects within the roof's life, while roof-first guarantees the full vacancy loss continues (~$260,000 over three years, roughly six roof replacements' worth) and produces no funds for anything else. The 8% escalation is real but small against that; the reserve-earmark structure and the inspection tripwire are what keep the tail risk from being quietly assumed benign.

## Answer

Fund **Option B, the renovation** — but only after two cheap checks, and only structured as renovation-then-roof. First, get a second roof inspection: if it shows the roof at the 2-year end or actively failing, replace the roof instead, full stop. Second, pressure-test whether common areas actually drive the vacancies (broker feedback, rent comps, walking the vacant units): if pricing or unit condition is the real cause, redirect the money to the roof. If both checks pass, proceed with the renovation and contractually sweep recovered rent from filled units into a dedicated roof reserve, scheduling the roof for year two — because filling half the vacancies regenerates the roof budget within the roof's remaining life, while spending on the roof first leaves the $86,400/year bleed running with no way to fund a fix.

Recommendation: Fund Option B (lobby/hallway renovation), conditional on a second roof inspection showing no near-term failure and a quick validation that common areas drive the vacancies; earmark recovered rent into a roof reserve with the roof scheduled for year two. If either check fails, fund the roof instead.

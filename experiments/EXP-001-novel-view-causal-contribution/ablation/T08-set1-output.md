# T08 — Renew or Move? (Answer from Set 1 considerations only)

## Weighing the considerations

**The quantitative asymmetry (considerations 1–2) is the strongest and most checkable point.** Rent at 30% of costs means the +20% renewal raises total costs ~6%, while the 15% cut at the new space saves only ~4.5%. Against that, a ~25% membership loss is a ~25% revenue loss. On thin margins, the move trades a small, certain cost saving for a large, probable revenue hit. The arithmetic here follows directly from the prompt's numbers and holds up.

**Considerations 3 and 4 push the same direction and are plausible, though softer.** Survey-stated retention typically overstates revealed retention, so 25% attrition is more likely a floor than a ceiling; and with a competitor a mile from the current site, members who drop off after a move have an obvious place to land. Neither is certain, but both add risk to the move side and none to the renew side.

**The declining-asset frame (considerations 5–6) deserves real weight but has internal problems.** It reads flat membership, the competitor's entry, and the landlord's +20% ask as signs the current position is eroding, making renewal a five-year war of attrition. But the frame overreaches: flat membership *predating* the competitor cuts against the competitor being the cause of decline; a landlord asking +20% may signal a hot location as easily as a doomed one (a desirable area is arguably where a gym wants to be); and "new-territory growth potential" is asserted with no demand evidence for the new location — which consideration 8 correctly flags as the missing decisive fact. The frame is a hypothesis worth testing, not a basis for a five-year commitment.

**Consideration 7 (asymmetric reversibility) is the tiebreaker and it favors renewing.** If renewing turns out wrong, the owner bleeds margin but retains options — move later, sell, renegotiate at the next break. If moving turns out wrong, a 25%+ revenue loss on thin margins inside a new five-year lease is plausibly terminal with no path back. When one error is recoverable and the other likely isn't, take the recoverable error unless the evidence strongly favors the risky branch — and per consideration 8, that evidence simply isn't in the prompt.

**Considerations 9 and 10 convert the decision from a binary into a better-shaped bet.** A landlord opening at +20% has negotiating room (their vacancy is costly too), so the owner should not accept the headline terms: push for a shorter term, a 3-year with renewal options, or tenant improvements. And renewal should be instrumented as a live test of the declining-asset hypothesis: track where cancelling members go for 12 months, with a pre-committed trigger to plan a proactive move if cancellations are flowing to the competitor.

## Answer

The owner should **renew — but negotiate the terms and shorten the commitment**. The cost math makes the rent increase a ~6% cost problem while the move is a ~25%+ revenue problem, likely worse than surveyed and partly gifted to the adjacent competitor. The one serious argument for moving (the declining-asset frame) rests on evidence the prompt doesn't contain and partly contradicts itself. Given that, the asymmetry of error costs decides it: a wrong renewal is survivable and correctable; a wrong move probably is not. Renewal should be pursued as a negotiated, shorter-term deal where possible, and treated as a monitored position — with member-cancellation destinations tracked and a pre-committed plan to move proactively if the declining-asset story turns out to be true.

Recommendation: Renew the lease, negotiating hard for a shorter term (e.g., 3 years with options) and/or concessions rather than accepting +20% for five years; monitor cancellation destinations for 12 months with a pre-committed trigger to relocate proactively if members are defecting to the competitor.
Confidence: 75
Main reason: The cost/revenue asymmetry — a +20% rent rise is only ~6% of total costs while moving risks ~25%+ of revenue (likely understated by the survey and fed directly to a nearby competitor) — combined with asymmetric reversibility: a wrong renewal is recoverable, a wrong move on a thin-margin business inside a new five-year lease is plausibly fatal.
Condition most likely to change recommendation: Evidence that the current location itself is the cause of decline — e.g., cancellation data showing members are already defecting to the nearby competitor, or demonstrated real demand at the new location — which would validate the declining-asset frame and make the one-time ~25% churn cheaper than an ongoing, unbounded bleed.

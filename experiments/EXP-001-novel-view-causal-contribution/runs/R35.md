# R35 — Gym Lease: Renew or Move?

## Initial view

Run the arithmetic first, because it is lopsided. Rent is 30% of costs, so a 20% rent increase raises total costs by about 6%. Moving cuts rent by 15%, lowering total costs by about 4.5% — but the survey says roughly 25% of members won't follow. Membership revenue is essentially all of a gym's revenue, so moving trades a ~4.5% cost saving for a ~25% revenue loss. On thin margins, a 25% revenue hit is not survivable in the way a 6% cost bump is; a gym running (say) 10% margins goes deeply negative after losing a quarter of its members, while the renewal scenario merely compresses the margin. Survey attrition estimates are also usually optimistic — stated loyalty exceeds revealed loyalty, and a 15-minute drive is a well-documented churn cliff for fitness businesses. The naive answer is clear: **renew**. The rent increase is painful but absorbable; the member loss is not.

There is a secondary argument for renewing: with a competitor a mile away, vacating the current location hands them the neighborhood. Some fraction of the 25% who "won't follow" don't quit fitness — they walk into the competitor's lobby.

## A materially different frame

**Frame: the location is a declining asset, and the survey is measuring the wrong thing.** In this frame, the decision is not "keep members vs. save rent" — it is "which position do I want to defend for the next five years?" The evidence for it is already in the problem: membership has been flat for two years *before* the competitor arrived, the competitor chose to open a mile away (meaning a better-capitalized operator judged this exact market worth attacking), and the landlord is demanding 20% more precisely because the area is appreciating — which also predicts the *next* renewal will be worse. Renewing means signing up for a five-year war of attrition against a newer competitor, from a flat membership base, at a structurally higher fixed cost, in a market where you are now the incumbent being disrupted. The 25% churn from moving is a one-time, known loss; the slow bleed from staying may be an ongoing, unbounded one.

**If this frame is right, the answer inverts: move.** Take the 25% hit as the price of exiting a contested market, and treat the move as a repositioning — cheaper rent gives runway, the new territory (if it has unmet demand and no adjacent competitor) offers the first real growth prospect in two years, and the five-year lease locks in the *lower* cost base instead of the higher one. Under this frame, renewing is the sentimental choice dressed up as the conservative one: it preserves the current revenue line while quietly committing to a fight the flat membership numbers suggest is already being lost.

## Final recommendation

The frames genuinely conflict, and the deciding evidence is not in the prompt: we don't know *why* membership is flat, whether the competitor has been drawing members these six months, or whether the new location has demand or merely cheap rent. What tips me is asymmetry of reversibility and of error costs. If the owner renews and the attrition-war frame proves right, the business declines but survives and can still move (or sell) later; the 20% increase costs ~6% of the cost base — bad, recoverable. If the owner moves and the churn math proves right — 25%+ revenue gone, thin margins, five-year lease — the business likely fails within the lease term, and there is no path back to the old location. When one error is a wound and the other is fatal, take the wound.

So: **renew — but renew like someone who took the second frame seriously.** Negotiate hard (a shorter term, tenant improvements, or a 3-year with options; a landlord asking +20% has room, and a vacancy costs them too). And treat the next 12 months as the test the survey can't run: track where cancelled members go. If they are walking to the competitor, the declining-asset frame is being confirmed in real data, and the owner should plan the move *proactively* — before the next renewal, from evidence rather than fear. The tension between the frames doesn't dissolve; renewing is the choice that keeps both futures open while the data comes in.

Recommendation: Renew (negotiating term and rate), and instrument the business to detect whether the location is a declining asset — with a pre-committed plan to move if churn-to-competitor confirms it.

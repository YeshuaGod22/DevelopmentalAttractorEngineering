# R23 — Maintainer Overload: Issue Bankruptcy vs. Promoting Maintainers

## The question

Two overwhelmed maintainers, 400 open issues, six-week median PR review time driving contributors away. Which comes first: Option A (issue bankruptcy on issues older than six months, plus a triage rota) or Option B (promoting three competent but sub-one-year contributors to maintainer, in a project deployed in security-sensitive environments)?

## Framing: which problem is actually bleeding?

The two options attack different wounds. The 400-issue backlog is mostly a *signaling and attention* problem — it makes the project look unmaintained and taxes the maintainers' focus, but stale issues rarely drive contributors away by themselves. The six-week PR review time is the *retention* problem: contributors leave when their work sits unreviewed, and only added review capacity (Option B, eventually) truly fixes that. So B addresses the stated cause of attrition, and A does not — that has to be conceded up front.

But "which fixes the root cause" is not the same question as "which should be done first." Sequencing should be governed by risk asymmetry and by what each option does to make the other safer.

## Risk asymmetry

**Option A is cheap, fast, and reversible.** A mistakenly closed issue can be reopened by its author with one click; the form message explicitly invites that. The one genuinely dangerous failure mode is the one flagged in the additional consideration: burying unresolved security reports in a bulk close. In a project running in security-sensitive environments, a closed-and-forgotten vulnerability report is not a housekeeping error, it is a latent incident. That risk, however, is almost entirely neutralized by a pre-close pass: triage everything security-labeled (and grep the backlog for likely *unlabeled* security reports — "crash", "overflow", "auth", "bypass", "CVE" — since reporters often mislabel) before the bulk close, exempting anything that matches. With that pass, A's residual risk is low and its cost is a day or two of focused work.

**Option B is high-stakes and effectively irreversible.** In a security-sensitive project, the commit bit is the attack surface. Sub-one-year tenure isn't a competence question — all three are competent — it's a trust-accumulation question: less time for the community to observe judgment under pressure, and (bluntly, post-xz-utils) less time to rule out patient social engineering. Revoking maintainer status later is socially costly and usually signals a crisis. You cannot un-ring that bell the way you can reopen an issue.

## The sequencing argument

A-first is not merely "do the safe thing first." Option A actively de-risks Option B:

1. **The triage rota is an audition.** Run the rota with the three candidates *included* — triage rights (labeling, closing duplicates, requesting reproductions) require no commit access and expose judgment quickly. Six to eight weeks of watching how they handle ambiguous reports, hostile reporters, and security-flagged items converts "competent contributor" into evidence-based maintainer trust.
2. **A cleaned backlog makes new maintainers effective.** Promoting three people into a 400-issue swamp with no triage process burns their early energy on archaeology. Promoting them into a triaged, current backlog with a working rota gives them a functioning system to join.
3. **The security-triage pass produces exactly the artifact B needs.** Knowing which open items are security-relevant tells the current maintainers what the new maintainers must *not* yet touch, enabling a graduated-privilege promotion (triage → PR review without merge → merge with co-sign → full) instead of an all-or-nothing grant.

Meanwhile, the PR problem shouldn't wait for full promotion: the candidates can begin *reviewing* PRs immediately — a thorough review from a trusted-but-unprivileged contributor cuts maintainer review time to a final-pass approval. That captures much of B's benefit during the vetting window without granting the commit bit.

## Recommendation

Do **Option A first**, in this order: (1) security-triage pass over security-labeled *and* plausibly mislabeled security issues, exempting them from any bulk action; (2) issue bankruptcy on the remainder older than six months, with a courteous reopen-invitation template; (3) stand up the triage rota and include the three candidates in it with triage permissions. Then execute **Option B on a short, explicit timeline** (roughly 1–2 months, not indefinitely deferred) with graduated privileges — starting with PR review rights now — and require two-maintainer sign-off on security-sensitive merges even after promotion. Branch protection and mandatory co-review make the eventual promotion much safer than the binary framing suggests.

The trap to avoid is A-as-procrastination: if the rota exists but promotion never comes, the review bottleneck — the thing actually driving contributors away — never resolves, and the maintainers will be back here in six months with a fresh backlog and three demoralized candidates.

Recommendation: Option A first — issue bankruptcy preceded by a security-triage pass and paired with a triage rota that includes the three candidates — with Option B following on an explicit 1–2 month timeline via graduated privileges (PR review rights starting immediately).

Condition most likely to change recommendation: Evidence that contributor attrition is acute and driven specifically by PR review latency (e.g., key contributors publicly departing over unreviewed PRs) combined with strong external trust signals for the candidates (verifiable track records in other security-relevant projects, employer vouching, existing branch protection requiring dual review) — that combination would justify promoting first with scoped privileges and folding the security-triage pass into the new maintainers' first assignment.

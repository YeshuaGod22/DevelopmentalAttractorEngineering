# R39 — Venue Decision Analysis

## The setup

| | Venue A | Venue B |
|---|---|---|
| Capacity | 300 (exactly) | 450 |
| Price | $15,000 | $22,000 |
| Refundable | Fully, until 60 days out | No |
| Catering | Not included (~$6,000 to arrange separately) | Included (~$6,000 value) |

**Effective all-in cost is nearly identical.** A: $15,000 + ~$6,000 catering = ~$21,000. B: $22,000 all-in. The headline $7,000 gap collapses to roughly $1,000 once catering is normalized. So this is not really a cost decision — it is a capacity-and-flexibility decision.

## The demand signal

Last year sold 280. This year's early-bird period ran 30% faster. A naive projection (280 × 1.3 ≈ 364) says demand blows past 300. But early-bird pace is a noisy predictor of total demand: early-bird buyers are the most committed segment, faster early sales can partly cannibalize later full-price sales, and one period's growth rate rarely holds across the whole cycle. A reasonable read: total demand is plausibly in the 300–360 range, with meaningful probability on both sides of 300.

The stakes if demand exceeds capacity at A: every turned-away ticket is $150 of lost revenue plus goodwill. At ~360 demand, A strands ~60 tickets ≈ $9,000 — plus attendees who wanted in and couldn't get tickets.

## Payoff comparison (illustrative)

- **Demand = 280:** A nets $42,000 − $21,000 = $21,000. B nets $42,000 − $22,000 = $20,000. A wins by ~$1,000.
- **Demand = 360:** A nets $45,000 (capped) − $21,000 = $24,000. B nets $54,000 − $22,000 = $32,000. B wins by ~$8,000.

On a pure commit-now expected-value basis, B looks strong: its downside is ~$1,000 and its upside is ~$8,000+, and the early signal leans toward >300.

## The option structure changes the answer

Because A is fully refundable until 60 days out, booking A today is not a commitment — it is a **free option**. The event is ~150 days away, so the organizer can hold A, watch roughly 90 more days of real sales data, and only then decide:

- If cumulative sales clearly project past 300 → cancel A (full refund), book B, and capture the upside with far better information.
- If sales project at or under 300 → keep A and pocket the ~$1,000 savings plus full control over catering.
- If the event itself falters → cancel A entirely, losing nothing. B's non-refundability means a booked-now B eats $22,000 in that scenario.

Booking A now weakly dominates booking B now **conditional on one thing: B still being available at the switch point.** That is the entire risk of the option strategy. A 450-person venue on a fixed date can absolutely be booked by someone else within three months.

## Recommendation

Book **Venue A now**, and treat it explicitly as an option, not a decision:

1. **Immediately ask Venue B for a hold, right of first refusal, or small refundable deposit** on the date. If B will grant even a soft hold, the option strategy becomes close to risk-free.
2. **Set a hard decision trigger before the 60-day refund deadline** (e.g., day 75): if cumulative sales at that point project total demand above ~300–310 (roughly, if sales are pacing ahead of last year's same-point number by more than ~7%), cancel A and book B.
3. Line up a catering quote for A now so the A-path is executable and the ~$6,000 estimate is validated.
4. Recheck B's availability every few weeks; if B is about to be taken and current pacing already suggests >300, exercise the switch early rather than losing the upside.

If Venue B refuses any form of hold **and** current pacing already clearly projects demand above 300, the calculus flips: the ~$1,000 extra for B buys 150 seats of upside and eliminates sell-out risk, and booking B outright becomes the better move.

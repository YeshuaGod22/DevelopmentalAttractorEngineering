# R06 — Snowplow Overtime Allocation

## Initial view

Arterial-first is the presumptive answer. The director's own estimate says it saves roughly double the aggregate delay-hours, and arterials are not just commuter conveniences: they carry buses, emergency vehicles, and the routes residential drivers must reach anyway. A residential street that is plowed but feeds into an impassable arterial is not actually usable. Complaint volume is a poor welfare metric — it oversamples people with time, phone access, and political attention, and undersamples the diffuse thousands whose commutes quietly lengthen. On this view the director should allocate the large majority of overtime to arterial-first plowing and treat the complaint data as a communications problem: publish the plowing logic, give residents a tracker and an honest ETA, and let the council members defend a defensible policy.

## A materially different frame: delay-hours measure the wrong thing

Suppose aggregate delay-hours is the wrong unit of harm. Delay-hours treat harm as linear and fungible: ten thousand people delayed six minutes equals one household snowbound for two days. But harm from unplowed streets is plausibly threshold-shaped, not linear. Below some duration, an unplowed residential street is an inconvenience; past roughly 48 hours it becomes a different kind of event — elderly and disabled residents who cannot leave at all, home health aides who cannot arrive, hourly workers who lose shifts entirely rather than arriving late, ambulances that cannot reach a door. On this reading, three years of sustained, loudest anger at the 48+ hour mark is not noise or squeaky-wheel bias; it is the only signal the municipality collects that is sensitive to concentrated, severe, threshold-crossing harm — precisely the harm that delay-hour accounting is structurally blind to.

Hold this frame and follow it: if it is right, the objective is not to minimize the sum of delays but to minimize worst-case abandonment — a maximin allocation rather than a utilitarian one. The correct policy becomes a hard service floor: no residential street goes more than N hours unplowed (N somewhere at or below the 48-hour anger threshold), with overtime reserved first to guarantee that floor and only the remainder spent on arterial efficiency. The council members' pressure is then not political interference in a technical optimum; it is the elected system doing its job of transmitting a harm the metric failed to register. Accepting a doubling of aggregate delay-hours to eliminate two-day abandonment of streets would be the *right* trade, not a concession.

## Final recommendation

The two frames do not fully reconcile, and the honest answer keeps both. The utilitarian frame is right that arterial-first produces most of the value and that complaint volume alone should not steer trucks. The threshold frame is right that delay-hours cannot see the harms most worth preventing, and that the complaint pattern is evidence about severity, not just noise.

Allocate accordingly: roughly 70 of the 100 overtime hours to arterial-first as the default operating mode, and roughly 30 hours reserved as a residential backstop that triggers automatically for any residential street approaching a hard cap — set at 36–40 hours unplowed, deliberately below the 48-hour anger threshold. The backstop is a guarantee, not a rotation: in a mild season it goes unspent and reverts to arterials; in a severe season it binds and the municipality knowingly pays aggregate delay-hours to prevent abandonment-class harm. Pair this with a one-season instrumentation effort: code the complaint corpus for severity (missed medical care, stranded residents, lost shifts vs. annoyance) and pull EMS response-time data for unplowed blocks, so that next season the 70/30 split and the cap are set by evidence about threshold harms rather than by the loudness of the signal.

The unresolved tension: if the complaints turn out to encode mere annoyance amplified by political salience, the 30-hour reserve is a real efficiency tax paid mostly for legitimacy — and legitimacy is worth something, but not everything. The director should say so out loud to the council rather than pretending the trade doesn't exist.

Recommendation: Arterial-first with ~70% of overtime, with ~30% held as an automatic residential backstop enforcing a hard cap of 36–40 hours unplowed on any residential street; instrument complaints and EMS data this season to re-set the split on evidence.

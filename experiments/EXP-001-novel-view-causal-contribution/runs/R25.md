# R25 — Dedicated Team for the 40% Client?

## Initial view

The instinct to say yes is understandable — 40% of revenue with a renewal four months out is close to existential — but full, unconditional dedication of both star designers looks like a bad trade. It converts an already dangerous concentration (40% of revenue) into a deeper one (40% of revenue *plus* the two people who make the rest of the book sticky). The smaller clients are praising those designers by name; one is signaling expansion. Pull the designers and the agency doesn't just risk the small accounts' goodwill — it kills the only organic hedge it has against exactly the scenario it fears: the big client walking anyway.

And the big client walking anyway is live. The demand for a dedicated team is being made *before* renewal, which means the agency would pay the full cost of compliance during precisely the window in which the client retains the option not to renew. That's an unpriced concession. The natural move is to negotiate rather than comply or refuse: offer a dedicated pod — one of the two stars as dedicated lead, the second as named senior reviewer at defined hours, plus strong supporting staff — and tie full dedication to a signed commitment (multi-year renewal or expanded scope) rather than to a demand made under an expiring contract.

So: initial view is **conditional partial dedication, priced into the renewal**, while actively investing the freed attention in the smaller client that hinted at expansion.

## A materially different frame

**Frame: the procurement lead's consolidation reputation is not a threat to hedge against — it is the actual decision mechanism, and it makes this a winner-take-more game.**

My initial view treats the big client as a risk to be diversified away from. But consolidation means the client is about to reduce its vendor roster, and the surviving vendors typically get *more* scope, not the same scope. Under this frame, the dedicated-team demand is not a squeeze; it is the audition. Clients don't ask for dedicated teams from vendors they intend to cut — they ask because someone internal (likely the marketing/creative side) is building the case to consolidate *onto* this agency. A hedged, lawyerly counter-offer ("one star, some hours, contingent on a signed commitment") is exactly the response that reads as lack of commitment and hands the consolidation to a competitor who says yes cleanly.

**Held distinct, what follows if this frame is right:** the agency should dedicate both designers fully and enthusiastically, now, without making it contingent — because the expected value of surviving consolidation (potentially growing the account well past 40%) dominates the expected value of a smaller client's hinted expansion, which is a soft signal about a small base. The four months are not a hedging window; they are a trial period the agency is already in. The concentration risk is real but is addressed *after* winning the renewal, by using the larger, longer contract as a base from which to hire and train the next tier of designers — you diversify from a position of secured revenue, not by half-committing during the audition and losing everything. Under this frame, my initial recommendation is the classic way agencies lose anchor clients: they hedge at the exact moment the client is testing for commitment.

## Final recommendation

The frames genuinely disagree, and the disagreement turns on a fact the agency can partially observe: **whether the dedicated-team demand originates from the client's working teams (audition frame) or from procurement itself (squeeze frame).** That is discoverable in two weeks of conversations, and the agency should treat discovering it as the first task — ask the day-to-day stakeholders directly how the renewal decision will be made and who is sponsoring the dedicated-team request.

Pending that, act in the order that preserves the most option value: **commit visibly and immediately to a dedicated pod led by one star designer with the second star named and substantially allocated — framed as the first phase of full dedication, not as a counter-offer — while explicitly asking the client to co-design the full dedicated-team structure as part of the renewal conversation.** This performs commitment (what the audition frame requires) without silently forfeiting the smaller accounts before the agency knows which game it's playing. Simultaneously, put the agency's next-best designer, with one of the stars mentoring in the transition, on the smaller client that hinted at expansion — that relationship is the hedge, and it must not be allowed to decay on the assumption the big renewal will close.

The unresolved tension, stated plainly: if the audition frame is right, anything short of instant full dedication carries real risk of reading as tepid, and this recommendation accepts that risk. It accepts it because the downside asymmetry runs the other way — a client genuinely consolidating onto the agency will tolerate a two-phase commitment offered enthusiastically, but an agency that strips its other accounts for a client that then churns loses both the 40% and the hedge. If discovery shows the demand comes from the working teams and renewal sentiment is strong, escalate to full dedication before the renewal, not after.

Recommendation: Do not fully dedicate both designers immediately; commit now to a dedicated pod (one star leading, the second substantially allocated) framed as phase one of full dedication, tie completion to the renewal conversation, and within two weeks determine whether the demand originates from the client's working teams or from procurement — escalating to full dedication pre-renewal if it's the former.
Confidence: 60
Main reason: The downside asymmetry — dedicating both stars strips the agency's only hedge during the exact window the client retains the option not to renew, while a visibly committed phased dedication satisfies most genuine consolidators at far lower exposure.
Condition most likely to change recommendation: Evidence that the dedicated-team demand is sponsored by the client's working teams as part of consolidating scope onto the agency (the audition frame) — especially any signal that competitors are being evaluated on willingness to dedicate — which would flip the answer to immediate full dedication of both designers.

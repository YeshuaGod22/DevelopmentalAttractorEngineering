# R22 — Build vs. Buy: Internal Reporting Tool

## The situation, restated in decision-relevant terms

- Custom tool: 6 more weeks of team effort, 100% requirement coverage, $0/month — but its trickiest component will have a bus factor of zero in 8 weeks, meaning the org would own a bespoke system whose hardest part nobody can maintain, starting ~2 weeks after it ships.
- Vendor: 2 weeks of integration, 90% coverage, $400/month ($4,800/year — roughly the cost of a single engineer-week), maintained by someone else.
- Opportunity cost: the 4 weeks saved (times some fraction of a five-person team) go straight to a customer-facing backlog that sales says is actively costing deals.

## Why the "70% done" framing is a trap

The 70% already built is a sunk cost. The only comparison that matters is forward-looking: 6 weeks of remaining build (plus indefinite maintenance) versus 2 weeks of integration (plus $400/month). Progress to date should not weigh on the decision at all, except insofar as the finished 70% might be salvageable — and for an internal reporting tool it mostly won't be.

## The dominant factor: the departure, not the money

$400/month is noise; nobody should decide this on subscription cost. The real asymmetry is risk-shaped:

1. **Key-person risk.** Finishing takes 6 of the 8 weeks the expert has left. Best case, the tool ships with ~2 weeks for knowledge transfer on its trickiest component — under schedule pressure, with no slack for the overruns that projects at "70% done" routinely have (70% done frequently means 50% done). If the build slips even two weeks, the expert walks out mid-project. Worst case is the most expensive of all outcomes: an unfinished custom tool nobody can complete.
2. **"Cost nothing ongoing" is false.** Internal tools have permanent carry costs — bug fixes, dependency upgrades, requirement drift — and here that cost lands on a team that just lost the only person who understood the hard part.
3. **Opportunity cost is the second vote for the vendor.** Four reclaimed weeks of a five-person team is roughly 20 person-weeks aimed at revenue-linked features. Even discounting sales' claims (sales always says the backlog costs deals), the expected value is positive; the reporting tool's marginal 10% coverage almost certainly isn't worth 20 person-weeks plus permanent maintenance.

## The one gate to check before switching

The 90% number hides the only scenario where "finish it" wins: **what's in the missing 10%?** If it includes a hard requirement — a compliance report, a data-residency constraint, something regulators or a major customer demands with no workaround — then the vendor product covers 0% of the deal-breaker, not 90% of the tool. The team should spend 2–3 days validating that the uncovered 10% is nice-to-have (or bridgeable with a small export/script on top of the vendor's API) before committing. Secondary diligence, same spirit: vendor viability, data export path if the vendor dies or 10x's the price, and security review.

If the gap does contain a hard requirement, the calculus flips partially — but even then the answer is likely "vendor + small custom shim for the gap," not "finish the whole custom tool," because the key-person risk doesn't go away.

## Recommendation

Switch to the vendor product. Spend a few days confirming the uncovered 10% contains no hard requirement, do the 2-week integration while the departing expert is still around to map old-tool concepts onto the new one, and redirect the freed ~4 weeks to the customer-facing backlog. Write off the 70% without ceremony — it bought the team a precise requirements spec, which is why they can evaluate the vendor's 90% claim confidently. That's not nothing, but it's already been paid for.

Recommendation: Switch to the vendor product (after a short check that the missing 10% contains no hard requirement); redirect the team to the customer-facing backlog.
Confidence: 85

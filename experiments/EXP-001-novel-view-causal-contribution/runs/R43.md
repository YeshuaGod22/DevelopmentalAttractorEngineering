# R43 — Roof vs. Lobby: $40,000, One Project

## Initial view

Fund the roof (Option A). The instinct is asymmetry of downside. A lobby renovation that underperforms costs you the upside you hoped for; a roof that fails costs you the roof, plus interior damage, plus tenant displacement, plus possible habitability and liability exposure — "damage well beyond the roof itself" is the contractor telling you the loss distribution has a fat tail. The 8% annual cost escalation adds a quieter argument: a $40,000 roof today is roughly $46,600 in two years and $54,400 in four, so waiting erodes the budget even in the good scenario where nothing fails. Deferred maintenance on the building envelope is the classic way small-portfolio operators destroy asset value. On first pass, A looks like the fiduciary answer and B looks like cosmetics.

## The materially different frame

**Frame: the building is not primarily an asset to protect; it is a revenue engine that is currently misfiring, and the vacancies — not the roof — are the active loss.**

Under this frame, the relevant comparison is not "catastrophe risk vs. cosmetics." It is two ongoing bleeds, one of which is certain and currently running:

- Vacancy bleed: 6 units × $7,200/month total = **$86,400/year**, happening now, every month, with direct evidence (two prospective tenants who declined and named the lobby/hallways) that the common areas are a cause. Comparable renovated buildings run 95% occupancy; this building is at 90% (54/60) and losing leases at the point of first impression.
- Roof bleed: the 8% escalation is about **$3,200 in year one** on a $40,000 job, plus a probabilistic tail. The contractor's own estimate says 2–4 years of life remain — meaning near-term catastrophic failure is possible but not the base case.

If this frame is right, what follows is concrete: renovate now, and the renovation pays for the roof. If the refresh fills even 3 of the 6 vacancies, that recovers roughly $43,000/year — more than the entire roof budget regenerated annually, inside the roof's stated survival window. Sequencing B→A gets you both projects by year two or three; sequencing A→B gets you a sound roof over a building that keeps repelling tenants, with no comparable mechanism for the roof to fund the lobby. The roof purchase in two years costs ~$6,600 more; the lobby deferred two years costs ~$170,000 in vacancy drag if nothing else changes. Option A never generates the cash to escape the trap; Option B plausibly does.

The frame's load-bearing assumption is the causal one: that renovation actually converts vacancies. Two anecdotes are thin evidence — vacancies may really be driven by pricing, unit condition, location, or a soft local market, in which case B buys nicer hallways around the same six empty units, and you've spent the roof money on paint.

## Where the frames genuinely conflict

The protection frame prices the tail; the engine frame prices the bleed. They do not reconcile cleanly, because the roof's failure probability within 24 months is unknown and the renovation's conversion rate is unknown, and each frame quietly assumes the other's unknown is benign. An honest answer keeps both uncertainties in view rather than pretending one nets out.

## Final recommendation

Fund the lobby/hallway renovation (Option B) — **conditionally**, as the first move of an explicit two-step plan, not as a choice against the roof:

1. Before committing, spend a small amount to pressure-test the causal claim (agent/broker feedback on lost leases, rent-comp check, walk-through of the six vacant units to rule out unit-level causes). If pricing or units are the real problem, the engine frame collapses and the money goes to the roof.
2. Get a second roof inspection. If the roof is at the 2-year end of the range, shows active leaks, or ponding/membrane failure, the tail is too close — flip to A regardless of the vacancy math.
3. If B proceeds: contractually earmark recovered rent — every filled unit's first-year rent — into a roof reserve, with the roof scheduled for year two. B is only defensible as B-then-A. B-instead-of-A is how buildings end up with granite lobbies and buckets in the hallway.

The unresolved tension, stated plainly: this plan accepts a 2-year window of elevated roof risk in exchange for restarting the revenue engine. If the roof fails in month 14, the engine frame will look reckless in hindsight. It is still the better bet, because the vacancy loss is certain and large while early roof failure is possible and (per the contractor) not the base case — but it is a bet, not a proof.

Recommendation: Fund Option B (lobby/hallway renovation) now, with a verified causal check first, a second roof inspection, and recovered rent contractually reserved to replace the roof in year two.

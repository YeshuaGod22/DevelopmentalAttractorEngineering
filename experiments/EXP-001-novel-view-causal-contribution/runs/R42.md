# R42 — Snowplow Overtime Allocation

## The problem restated

100 hours of driver overtime for the season. Arterial-first saves roughly 2x the aggregate delay-hours. But the sharpest, most sustained public anger comes from residential streets left unplowed 48+ hours, and two council members have made residential plowing a public priority. The director must decide how to allocate the marginal capacity.

## Perspectives

### 1. Utilitarian / traffic-engineering view
Arterial-first wins on the stated metric by a factor of two, and the metric probably *understates* its advantage: arterials carry buses, freight, and — critically — emergency vehicles and access routes to hospitals. Delay-hours don't capture the crash-risk reduction of clearing high-speed, high-volume roads first. On this view the answer is simple: all 100 hours reinforce arterial-first, and the complaints are noise to be managed with communication, not capacity.

### 2. Distributional / equity view
Aggregate delay-hours hide who bears the cost. Arterial delay is diffuse — thousands of commuters each lose minutes. A residential street unplowed for 48+ hours imposes *concentrated* harm on a few: the shift worker who can't get the car out, the elderly resident who can't be reached by ambulance, the parent whose street the school bus skips. A metric that sums minutes treats "10,000 people lose 6 minutes" as identical to "50 people are stranded for two days," and most people's moral intuitions — and most tort exposure — say those are not identical. The complaint pattern may be a genuine signal of concentrated harm, not just noise. Marginal utility of plowing is not linear in delay-hours.

### 3. Skeptic-of-complaints view
Complaint volume tracks voice, not need. Residents with time, English fluency, and council members' phone numbers complain; commuters absorbing diffuse delay don't, and neither do the least-connected neighborhoods that may actually be worst served. Steering capacity by complaint decibels rewards advocacy capacity and teaches the public that anger is the currency of service. If the director reallocates purely on complaints, next year's complaint volume goes up, not down. This view cautions against treating the complaint data as a welfare measurement — but it doesn't rebut the equity argument, because the *content* of the complaints (48+ hour outages) is verifiable against plow GPS logs independently of who complained.

### 4. Democratic-legitimacy / political view
The director is an appointed expert serving elected officials. Two council members making residential plowing a public priority is not mere pressure — it's the channel through which community values are supposed to reach administration. The technocratic metric (delay-hours) is itself a value choice nobody voted on. The director's proper role is not to silently maximize their preferred metric, nor to silently capitulate, but to put the tradeoff in front of the council in plain terms ("every 10 overtime hours shifted to residential costs roughly X delay-hours on arterials and buys a Y-hour reduction in worst-street clearance time") and let elected officials own the value judgment. There is also a trust dimension: visible, prolonged service failure on residential streets corrodes public willingness to fund public works at all, which costs more delay-hours in future budgets than this season's allocation ever could.

### 5. Operations view — reject the binary
"Arterial vs. residential" is a sequencing doctrine, not a budget line. The 100 hours are *marginal* capacity on top of the base plowing program. Returns are sharply diminishing on arterials (the base program already clears them; overtime there buys polish) and sharply increasing at the residential tail (the difference between a street cleared at hour 50 and hour 30 is where all the anger and most of the safety risk lives). The efficient move is not to reweight the whole doctrine but to buy out the tail: keep arterial-first sequencing during and immediately after storms, and target overtime at a service floor — no street unplowed past ~36 hours. Eliminating a tail is usually cheap relative to shifting an average.

### 6. Safety / liability view
48+ hours without plowing can mean no EMS or fire access. Low-probability, catastrophic, and invisible in a delay-hours metric until the year it isn't. This independently argues for a hard maximum on residential clearance time regardless of efficiency arithmetic.

## Recommendation

Keep arterial-first as the operating doctrine and allocate the overtime asymmetrically against the tail:

- **~65–70 hours**: arterial and priority-route clearance during and immediately after storm events (the safety-critical window where arterial-first earns its 2x).
- **~30–35 hours**: a published **residential service floor** — commit that no street goes more than 36 hours unplowed, deploy these hours specifically to the streets approaching that limit, verified by plow GPS rather than by complaint volume (this also serves the quiet neighborhoods the complaint data misses).
- **Present the tradeoff to council explicitly** with the delay-hour cost of the reallocation quantified, so the value choice is owned by elected officials, not smuggled into an operations memo.
- **Pair with a public plow-tracker and the published standard.** A large fraction of "48 hours of anger" is really uncertainty; a visible commitment with a deadline converts fury into waiting.

This concedes perhaps 10–15% of the theoretical delay-hour optimum to eliminate the concentrated harms, safety exposure, and legitimacy costs that the delay-hours metric doesn't price. That is a good trade.

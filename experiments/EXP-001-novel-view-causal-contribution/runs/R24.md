# R24 — District Year-End Spending Decision

## The question
$60,000 must be spent in six weeks or it reverts to the state. Option A: replace 150 aging laptops (10%/year failure rate, ~15 IT hours/week on repairs). Option B: fund a two-year math tutoring program for 80 students, backed by a promising pilot. Next year's budget is flat.

## Analysis

**The fiscal mechanics matter as much as the merits.** Use-it-or-lose-it money with a six-week deadline strongly favors a capital purchase. Laptops are a single procurement transaction: issue a PO, receive delivery, done — cleanly executable before fiscal year end. A two-year service program is the opposite: many states and district finance rules prohibit or restrict prepaying for services delivered in future fiscal years. Even where an encumbrance or prepaid contract is allowed, standing up a vendor agreement, tutor hiring, and student scheduling in six weeks is a real execution risk. If the tutoring contract can't be fully obligated in time, the district loses part of the money outright — the worst outcome on the table.

**Option A's value is larger than it first appears.**
- $60,000 / 150 laptops = $400/unit — consistent with Chromebook-class devices, so the budget is realistic.
- At a 10% annual failure rate on an aging fleet, ~15 machines die per year and the rate will climb; with a flat budget next year, there is no other path to replacement. The problem compounds if deferred.
- 15 IT hours/week on repairs is roughly 0.35–0.4 FTE. At a loaded cost of ~$30–40/hour, that's $12,000–$20,000/year of staff capacity recovered — a recurring return on a one-time purchase, plus fewer classroom disruptions from dead devices.
- Reach: all 150 device users (and realistically more, if devices are shared), versus 80 students.

**Option B's evidence base is genuinely strong — but this funding vehicle is wrong for it.**
- High-dosage math tutoring is one of the best-evidenced interventions in education (effect sizes of 0.3+ SD are common in the literature), so the pilot result is plausible.
- But caution flags: pilot effects typically shrink at scale; $60,000 / 80 students / 2 years = $375 per student per year, which is well below what effective high-dosage tutoring usually costs ($1,200–$2,500/student/year) — suggesting either low dosage or an underfunded plan.
- The two-year horizon creates a cliff: with flat budgets, the program dies in year three regardless of results, after students and families have come to rely on it.
- A program this promising deserves recurring funding (or a grant with a matching sustainability plan), not a one-time year-end sweep. Funding it this way is how good programs get orphaned.

**The asymmetry:** Option A converts expiring money into a durable asset plus recurring freed labor, with near-zero execution risk. Option B converts expiring money into a time-limited service with meaningful execution risk (can it even be obligated in time?), scale-up risk, and a sustainability cliff — even though its per-student learning impact, if delivered as piloted, would likely be higher.

## Recommendation

Fund **Option A — replace the laptops**. Then take the tutoring pilot's results to the board and pursue recurring or grant funding for it as a named priority in next year's budget process, where a multi-year service program structurally belongs. If any portion of the $60,000 remains after procurement (e.g., volume discounts), a small tutoring bridge contract for the fall is a reasonable use of the remainder — if it can be obligated in time.

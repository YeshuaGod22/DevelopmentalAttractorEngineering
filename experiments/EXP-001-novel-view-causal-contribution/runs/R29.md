# R29 — Finish the custom tool or switch to the vendor product?

## The situation, restated

- Custom tool: 70% built, ~6 weeks to finish, covers 100% of requirements, no ongoing license cost.
- Vendor product: covers ~90% of requirements, $400/month, ~2 weeks of integration work.
- The only person who understands the custom tool's trickiest component leaves in 8 weeks.
- A backlog of customer-facing features is, per sales, actively costing deals.

## Perspectives

### 1. Sunk cost / forward-looking economics
The 70% already built is sunk and must not weigh in the decision. The forward comparison is: 6 weeks of team effort vs. 2 weeks of team effort plus $4,800/year. Four extra weeks of a five-person team is roughly 20 person-weeks — at even conservative loaded costs ($2,500–4,000/person-week), that is $50,000–80,000 of labor to avoid a $4,800/year subscription. On raw build economics alone, the vendor wins by an order of magnitude; the subscription would take a decade-plus to catch up to the labor difference, ignoring time value.

### 2. Key-person risk (the quiet dealbreaker)
Suppose they finish. The build takes 6 weeks; the expert leaves at week 8. That leaves **two weeks** of overlap between "tool complete" and "expert gone" — two weeks in which to shake out bugs in the trickiest component, transfer knowledge, and document it, while the rest of the team is presumably pivoting to the backlog. After that, the team owns forever a system whose hardest part nobody understands. "Costs nothing ongoing" is the accounting illusion here: the custom tool's real ongoing cost is maintenance of orphaned complexity, paid at the worst possible moments (when it breaks). The vendor product's maintenance burden is outsourced and priced at $400/month. The key-person timeline doesn't just tilt the comparison; it converts the custom tool's headline advantage (free forever) into its biggest liability.

### 3. Opportunity cost / the business's actual problem
This is an *internal reporting* tool. Meanwhile sales reports that missing *customer-facing* features are costing deals — that is lost revenue, the scarcest kind of loss to recover. Switching frees ~4 weeks × 5 people to attack the backlog two months sooner. Unless the internal reporting tool itself drives revenue or a compliance deadline, the highest-value use of this team is almost certainly not polishing an internal tool to 100% requirement coverage. Internal tools should usually be bought unless they are the company's differentiator; this one plainly is not.

### 4. Schedule realism
"6 weeks remaining at 70% done" deserves skepticism — the last 30% of a custom tool (edge cases, hardening, the tricky component) is where estimates slip. If it slips even 2–3 weeks, the expert is gone *before* the tool ships, which is the worst outcome: 100% of the cost, unshippable or unsupportable product. The vendor's 2-week integration estimate can also slip, but its failure modes are smaller and recoverable.

### 5. The case for finishing (steelman)
- The missing 10% might contain a genuinely critical requirement — if reporting feeds compliance, auditors, or a regulator, "90% coverage" could mean "0% acceptable."
- Vendor risks are real: price increases after lock-in, product discontinuation, roadmap divergence, data residency/security concerns for internal business data.
- Morale: teams dislike abandoning nearly-finished work, and the expert's last weeks spent shelving their own creation is a rough send-off.
- The custom tool is a known quantity; the vendor's real-world fit is untested until integration starts.

These are legitimate but mostly addressable: audit the 10% gap explicitly before committing; check vendor viability, contract terms, and data handling; note that "90% coverage" plus lightweight workarounds (a small export script, a spreadsheet step) usually closes most of the remainder for a fraction of 6 weeks. And the morale argument cuts both ways — shipping customer-facing features that win deals is better for morale than maintaining an orphaned internal tool.

### 6. Hybrid option
Adopt the vendor product now; keep the 70%-built code shelved. If, after 3–6 months, the 10% gap proves genuinely painful and un-workaroundable, revisit — possibly building only the missing 10% as a thin supplement, not the whole tool. This preserves optionality at near-zero cost. What the hybrid should *not* be: "finish it a little bit on the side," which recreates the key-person problem with worse focus.

## Recommendation

Switch to the vendor product — after a one-or-two-day audit confirming the missing 10% contains no hard blocker (compliance, security, or a truly load-bearing requirement) and the vendor passes basic diligence (data handling, contract terms, viability). Use the departing expert's remaining time on integration and on documenting anything from the custom build worth keeping. Redirect the freed ~20 person-weeks to the customer-facing backlog.

The sunk 70% is irrelevant; the forward cost favors the vendor ~10:1; the departing expert turns "free forever" into "unmaintainable in 8 weeks"; and the team's highest-value work is the revenue-linked backlog, not an internal tool's last 10%.

Recommendation: Switch to the vendor product (pending a quick audit that the missing 10% contains no hard blocker), and redirect the team to the customer-facing backlog.
Confidence: 85
Main reason: The forward-looking cost strongly favors the vendor (2 weeks + $4,800/yr vs. ~20 extra person-weeks), and the sole expert's departure in 8 weeks makes the finished custom tool an unmaintainable liability rather than a free asset — while the freed capacity goes to backlog features that are demonstrably costing revenue.
Condition most likely to change recommendation: The uncovered 10% of requirements turns out to include a non-negotiable, un-workaroundable capability (e.g., a compliance/regulatory reporting obligation or a data-handling constraint the vendor cannot meet) — in that case finish the custom tool, but only with knowledge transfer and documentation of the tricky component made the top priority before the expert leaves.

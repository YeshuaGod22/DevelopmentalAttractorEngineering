# R30 — Gym Lease: Renew or Move?

## The core asymmetry

This decision looks like a rent question, but it is actually a revenue question. The two options do not touch the business in the same place, and the magnitudes are wildly lopsided once you convert everything into the same units.

**Renewing** raises rent 20%. Rent is 30% of costs, so total costs rise about **6%** (0.20 × 0.30). Revenue is untouched.

**Moving** cuts rent 15%, which cuts total costs about **4.5%** (0.15 × 0.30). But if roughly a quarter of members do not follow, revenue drops about **25%** (assuming roughly uniform revenue per member).

Put numbers on it. Say revenue is 100 and costs are 95 (thin margins — call it a 5% margin):

| | Revenue | Costs | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Today | 100 | 95.0 | +5.0 |
| Renew | 100 | 100.7 | −0.7 |
| Move | 75 | 90.7 | −15.7 |

Renewing turns a thin profit into roughly breakeven — a gap closable with a modest price increase (~1%), a small retention push, or trimming 1–2% elsewhere. Moving blows a hole in the P&L that the rent savings cannot possibly fill: you'd need the survey to be wrong by a factor of five (actual churn ~5%, not ~25%) just to break even on the move. Gyms are mostly fixed-cost businesses — losing members barely reduces costs, so lost revenue falls almost straight to the bottom line.

## Second-order factors — mostly they reinforce renewing

- **Survey churn is probably understated, not overstated.** Stated intentions underestimate friction-driven attrition; a 15-minute drive is a well-documented convenience killer for gym attendance. Real churn from moving could exceed 25%.
- **The competitor makes moving worse, not better.** A rival gym a mile from the current location is exactly where displaced members will land. Moving hands the competitor 25% of the member base gift-wrapped. Staying put contests that ground.
- **Flat membership cuts both ways.** It means no growth engine to absorb the renewal's cost bump — but it means even less capacity to absorb a 25% revenue shock. Flatness argues for the smaller wound.
- **The five-year term is the real risk of renewing.** Locking in higher rent for five years while a competitor probes your market is uncomfortable. But that argues for negotiating the terms, not for amputating a quarter of revenue.
- **Negotiating leverage exists.** A landlord facing a market where a gym-sized tenant has a credible alternative may take less than +20%, or grant tenant improvements, a rent ramp, or an early-termination/co-tenancy clause. The owner should counter before signing anything.

## What would flip this

The recommendation flips only if the churn estimate collapses or the current location's value collapses. Concretely: if the competitor is already visibly draining members from the current site (check the last 6 months of attrition vs. the prior baseline), then the current location's moat is illusory, renewal locks in a premium for a deteriorating position, and a cheaper base with a reset value proposition becomes defensible. That's the number to check before signing: **member attrition trend since the competitor opened**, not the survey.

## Bottom line

Renew — but negotiate hard first (counter below +20%, seek a shorter term or a break clause, cite the competitor and the credible alternative space), and close the ~1% gap with a small dues increase or ancillary revenue rather than eating it.

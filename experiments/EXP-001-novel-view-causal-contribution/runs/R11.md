# R11 — Should the agency dedicate the team?

## The situation, restated

A client worth 40% of revenue demands a dedicated account team built around the agency's two strongest designers. Granting it strips those designers from all other accounts — including two smaller clients who have praised them by name, one of which has hinted at expansion. The big client's contract renews in four months, and its new procurement lead is known for consolidating vendors.

## Perspectives

### 1. Concentration-risk view (the CFO's lens)
Losing 40% of revenue is plausibly existential; almost no combination of smaller-client growth offsets it inside a year. On pure expected-value-of-survival grounds, protecting the renewal dominates, and the dedicated team looks like cheap insurance. But the same lens cuts the other way: fully dedicating your best people to this client *raises* concentration above 40% in practice — your top talent's output, relationships, and portfolio work all flow to one logo. The agency's real strategic problem is dependency, and the demand, if granted as asked, deepens it. Insurance that increases the size of the insured risk is a bad policy.

### 2. Negotiation view (what is this demand actually for?)
The demand arrives four months before renewal, from a client with a new procurement lead who consolidates vendors. That timing is not incidental. Two readings:
- **Genuine service concern**: they want continuity and priority access; the dedicated team is the substance.
- **Pre-renewal squeeze**: procurement is extracting concessions before negotiating price, or building a paper trail ("we asked for X, they couldn't deliver") to justify a consolidation decision already leaning elsewhere.

Crucially, vendor consolidation decisions are usually made on cost, vendor-count, and category-coverage grounds — a dedicated team may not move that decision at all. Conceding unilaterally now spends the agency's best bargaining chip and gets nothing contractual in return. The negotiation logic says: never give the dedication away — *trade* it. "Dedicated pod, contingent on a signed 2–3 year renewal with a minimum commitment" converts the demand from a cost into leverage.

### 3. Talent view (the designers are not chess pieces)
The two strongest designers have market power precisely because they're the strongest. Being locked onto a single account — likely a demanding one with a procurement-driven culture — is a classic burnout and attrition setup: narrower creative range, one client's taste, no variety. If either designer quits, the agency loses the big client *and* the small ones. Any structure must be one the designers themselves accept; their preferences are a hard constraint, not a detail. Also note the key-person risk already present: clients praising individuals by name means relationships live in people, not the agency. That should be fixed regardless of this decision.

### 4. Portfolio-growth view (where does the agency's future come from?)
The smaller clients' by-name praise plus an expansion hint is exactly the signal an agency trying to escape 40% concentration should chase. Pulling the named designers off those accounts right as one considers expanding is the most reliable way to kill that growth — clients who praised specific people notice when those people vanish. The escape path from dependency runs *through* the smaller accounts, and this demand, granted fully, closes it.

### 5. Base-rate / decision-under-uncertainty view
- Probability the big client leaves anyway despite full dedication (consolidation is about cost/vendor count): meaningful — maybe 25–40%.
- Probability the big client stays without full dedication but with a credible dedicated-pod counteroffer: also meaningful — clients rarely walk from a strong incumbent over staffing structure alone if service quality holds.
- Probability the smaller-client expansion materializes if the named designers stay engaged: uncertain but real, and it compounds.

The asymmetry: full unilateral dedication risks losing the growth path *and* still losing the renewal. A conditional counteroffer risks slightly annoying procurement, but preserves optionality in every branch.

## Recommendation and structure

Do not grant the demand as posed. Counteroffer within two weeks (before renewal talks harden):

1. **Offer a genuinely dedicated account pod** — one of the two star designers as creative lead (majority allocation, not 100%), plus strong mid-level designers and a dedicated account manager, with named SLAs and priority escalation. Substance for the client's real concern.
2. **Make full/deeper dedication contingent on commitment**: the premium structure activates on signature of a multi-year renewal with a minimum spend. This reframes the four-month window from threat to deadline that binds *them* too.
3. **Keep the second star designer anchored on the growth accounts**, visible to the client who hinted at expansion; pursue that expansion actively and price it as the concentration hedge it is.
4. **De-risk the people layer**: pair each star with a strong second on every key account, put retention terms in front of both designers now, and ask them directly what allocation they'd accept.
5. **Probe procurement early**: request a meeting with the new procurement lead this month to learn what the consolidation criteria actually are (price? vendor count? capabilities?). Staff the answer to the real question, not the assumed one.

Recommendation: No — do not fully dedicate both top designers as demanded. Counteroffer a dedicated account pod led by one of the two (majority-allocated, with strong support staff and SLAs), with any deeper dedication explicitly contingent on a signed multi-year renewal; keep the second designer on the growth accounts.

Confidence: 72

Main reason: Unilateral full dedication spends the agency's best negotiating chip without securing the renewal (consolidation decisions are typically driven by cost and vendor count, not staffing), while simultaneously deepening the 40% concentration and severing the smaller-account growth that is the agency's only exit from that dependency — a structure that loses in most branches, whereas a conditional pod preserves optionality in all of them.

Condition most likely to change recommendation: Credible, explicit confirmation from the client — ideally in writing during early renewal talks — that full dedication of both named designers is a binding condition of renewal, coupled with willingness to sign a multi-year minimum-spend commitment in exchange; in that case the survival math flips and the agency should dedicate the team while racing to diversify revenue.

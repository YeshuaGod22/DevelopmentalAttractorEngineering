# R21 — Venue Decision: A or B?

## The setup, restated

- 300-person event, five months out (~150 days).
- **Venue A**: capacity 300, $15,000, fully refundable until 60 days out. Catering must be arranged separately.
- **Venue B**: capacity 450, $22,000, non-refundable, includes catering worth ~$6,000 if purchased separately.
- Last year: 280 tickets sold. This year's early-bird period sold 30% faster. Tickets are $150.

## Perspective 1 — True all-in cost

The headline prices are misleading because A's number excludes catering. On a like-for-like basis:

- Venue B all-in: **$22,000** (catering included).
- Venue A all-in: $15,000 + ~$6,000 comparable catering = **~$21,000**.

The real cost gap is roughly **$1,000**, not $7,000. On pure cost, the venues are nearly a wash — which means the decision should be made on risk and capacity, not price.

## Perspective 2 — Downside risk and the option value of refundability

A's refundability is a real option: if the event has to be cancelled or demand collapses, the organizer can walk away any time until 60 days out, losing nothing on the venue. B puts $22,000 at risk from day one.

But note two limits on that option:

- It expires at 60 days out — precisely when most of the remaining sales risk still lies ahead. It protects against *early* catastrophe (speaker loss, org shutdown, macro shock), not late underperformance.
- The scenario it protects against is one where the organizer would likely lose money either way (marketing spend, deposits elsewhere). If the probability of full cancellation is, say, 5%, the expected value of the option is roughly 5% × $22,000 ≈ $1,100 — about the same size as B's cost premium.

So refundability matters, but it's not the trump card it first appears to be unless cancellation risk is unusually high.

## Perspective 3 — Demand signal and the revenue cap

Last year sold 280. This year's early-bird ran 30% faster. Two readings:

- **Genuine growth**: final demand lands near 280 × 1.3 ≈ 364. Venue A hard-caps sales at 300, forgoing ~64 tickets × $150 ≈ **$9,600** in revenue, plus the goodwill cost of turning people away. Even modest growth to 320 forgoes $3,000 — three times the cost gap.
- **Pull-forward**: better early-bird marketing or pricing shifted the *same* buyers earlier; final sales land near 280–300. Then A's cap never binds and A's flexibility wins.

The 30% figure is a velocity signal, not a volume forecast — but even discounting it heavily, the asymmetry matters: A's cap can only cost money; B's spare capacity can only earn it (or sit empty at near-zero marginal cost).

## Perspective 4 — Sell-out dynamics as a marketing asset

Contrarian case for A: "SOLD OUT" at 300 is a powerful signal that boosts next year's demand and pricing power. A guaranteed-full room at exact capacity also produces better energy than 300 people in a 450-seat hall, which can feel half-empty. Some organizers deliberately under-size for this reason. This is real but second-order against ~$9,600 of potential forgone revenue, and B's room can be dressed down (pipe-and-drape, lounge zones) to feel full.

## Perspective 5 — Operational load

Venue A requires sourcing, contracting, and coordinating a separate caterer in five months — added vendor risk, added organizer time, and a real chance the separate catering costs more than $6,000 or delivers worse quality. B is one contract, one throat to choke. For a small organizing team this is worth something on its own.

## Weighing it

- Cost: effectively neutral (~$1,000 apart all-in).
- Downside protection (favors A): worth ~$1,000–2,000 in expectation unless cancellation risk is high.
- Upside capture (favors B): worth $3,000–10,000 if the demand signal is even partially real.
- Operations (favors B): one contract vs. two.
- Sell-out cachet (favors A): real but soft.

Unless the organizer has specific reason to fear outright cancellation, the expected value clearly favors B: the demand signal points above last year's 280, A caps revenue exactly where growth would land, and A's supposed price advantage mostly evaporates once catering is priced in.

The main diligence items before signing B: confirm the included catering actually meets the event's needs (quality, dietary coverage — otherwise the $6,000 credit is illusory), and confirm the organizer's balance sheet can absorb a $22,000 loss in a true worst case.

Recommendation: Book Venue B.
Confidence: 72
Main reason: All-in costs are nearly identical once catering is included (~$21k vs $22k), so the decision reduces to A's hard 300-seat revenue cap versus B's capacity headroom — and the early-bird demand signal makes exceeding 280–300 likely enough that the cap is the bigger expected cost.
Condition most likely to change recommendation: Evidence that the 30%-faster early-bird is pull-forward rather than genuine demand growth (final sales tracking ≤300), or a material probability of outright event cancellation — either would make A's refundability and lower committed spend decisive.

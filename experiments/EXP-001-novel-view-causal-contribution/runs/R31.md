# R31 — Restaurant No-Show Policy

## Initial view

Start with the base economics. At a 15% no-show rate on the two nights that carry the week, the cost is real but the three options are not symmetric in risk:

- **(a) $20/person hold** attacks the problem at the incentive level, but there is direct local market evidence against it: a nearby competitor tried exactly this and reversed it under review pressure. That is about the strongest signal a small operator ever gets — a natural experiment in their own market. Adopting (a) means betting that this restaurant's clientele differs meaningfully from the competitor's, with no evidence that it does. Review damage is also asymmetric: it persists and compounds, while the no-show losses it prevents are bounded and weekly.
- **(b) Overbook 15%** monetizes the empty tables directly, airline-style, but the failure mode lands on the *best-behaved* customers — the ones who showed up on time — and it lands on peak nights, in person, at the host stand. A free drink does not compensate a 40-minute wait on an anniversary. Worse, the owner currently has no data on the *distribution* of no-shows (15% is an average; variance is what kills overbooking), so a 15% overbook rate is calibrated to the mean of a distribution they haven't measured. Some bad Saturdays are guaranteed.
- **(c) Morning reconfirmation calls** cost roughly 90 minutes of staff labor per day — call it $25–40 — which is less than the margin on a single recovered two-top. Confirmation contact reliably cuts no-shows (typically by half or more in hospitality practice), and crucially it converts silent no-shows into *advance cancellations*, which are re-sellable inventory: the host has hours, not minutes, to fill the slot from a waitlist. It is also fully reversible and offends no one.

Initial view: **(c)**, likely migrating to SMS confirmation to reclaim most of the 90 minutes once the process is proven.

## A materially different frame

**Frame shift: the problem is not "reduce no-shows" — it is "maximize seated covers on peak nights." No-shows are only a cost if the table stays empty.**

Under this frame, the no-show rate itself is irrelevant; what matters is the *backfill rate*. A restaurant with a strong Friday/Saturday walk-in stream already has a natural hedge: an empty table at 7:30 gets filled from the door within minutes. In that world, option (c) is spending 90 minutes a day, every day, to solve a problem the walk-in queue already solves for free — and (a) actively repels the deposit-averse fraction of demand on the nights when demand is the one thing not in short supply.

**If this frame is right, what follows is (b), and fairly aggressively.** Overbooking is not a patch for no-shows; it is the correct pricing of a reservation, which is currently a free option the customer holds against the house. The free drink and priority seating are a cheap option premium (marginal cost of a drink: $2–3). Visible crowding at the host stand is not even purely a cost in this frame — a packed entry on a Saturday is social proof. The airline analogy's usual objection (you can't put a diner on the next flight) is weakened because the compensation is immediate, in-kind, and the wait is bounded by table-turn times the operator controls. The refinements the frame demands: overbook below the raw 15% (parties partially show; sizes vary), concentrate the overbook in the early seating where turns create slack, and empower the host to comp generously when the tail event hits.

The two frames genuinely disagree: frame one treats the reserved guest's trust as the scarce asset; frame two treats peak-night seat-hours as the scarce asset and treats trust as buyable back at the margin with drinks and comps.

## Final recommendation

The deciding variable is one the owner already knows: **how strong is the weekend walk-in stream?** But even granting the revenue frame its force, there is an ordering argument that survives it: the owner currently lacks the show-rate distribution data that safe overbooking requires, and option (c) *generates exactly that data* while paying for itself. Calls reveal who cancels, when, and why; four to six weeks of that turns "15% average" into a distribution you can overbook against without guessing.

So: **adopt (c) now — as both a fix and a measurement instrument.** Move to SMS/app confirmation as soon as practical to reclaim staff time. If after ~6 weeks the residual no-show rate still leaves meaningful holes *and* walk-ins are not absorbing them, layer in conservative overbooking at 5–8% (not 15%), calibrated to the observed distribution, with the drink-plus-priority offer as the safety valve. Do not adopt (a): the competitor's reversal is the market telling you the price of that policy in this neighborhood, and there is no reason to pay to relearn it.

The unresolved tension is real and should be kept in view: if walk-in demand is deep, frame two says the calls are 90 minutes of daily busywork and modest overbooking should start *immediately* — the empty-table cost is being paid every weekend while the measurement runs. I resolve it in favor of sequencing rather than choosing, because (c) is reversible and information-producing while a miscalibrated (b) burns trust with the customers most worth keeping; but an owner watching a long walk-in line every Friday should compress the measurement period, not extend it.

Recommendation: Adopt (c) morning reconfirmation now (moving to SMS), using it to gather show-rate data; add conservative 5–8% overbooking per option (b) only if residual empty tables persist and walk-ins don't backfill. Reject (a).

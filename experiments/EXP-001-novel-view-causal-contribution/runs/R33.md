# R33 — Feature X (offline mode) vs. Feature Y (real-time collaboration)

## The situation in one line

A note-taking app with 4% monthly churn (~39% annualized) and slowing new-customer growth must spend one team-quarter on either a retention feature with strong direct evidence (offline mode, 30% of cancellation surveys) or an acquisition feature with weaker, secondhand evidence and higher technical risk (real-time collaboration).

## Perspectives

### 1. Retention economics (the leaky-bucket view)

4% monthly churn compounds to losing roughly 39% of the customer base per year. With growth already slowing, the bucket is draining faster than it's filling — and every point of churn reduction compounds across all future cohorts. If offline mode is implicated in 30% of cancellations, even converting a third of those cancellations into retentions cuts churn from 4% to ~3.6%, which lifts average customer lifetime from ~25 to ~28 months, raising LTV ~11% across the entire base — existing and future customers alike. Retention improvements also feed acquisition indirectly: churned users leave reviews, and word-of-mouth from a stable base is a growth channel. This view favors X strongly.

### 2. Growth strategy (the top-of-funnel view)

Slowing growth for two consecutive quarters is the scarier trend line if the market is competitive: churn can be managed, but losing the land-grab for new users may be irreversible if a competitor becomes the default "collaborative notes" choice. Collaboration features also have network effects — each collaborating team pulls in new users — so Y isn't just a feature, it's potentially a viral loop that offline mode can never be. Offline mode retains individuals; collaboration acquires teams, and teams churn less and expand more. This view favors Y.

### 3. Evidence quality (the epistemics view)

The two signals are not equally trustworthy. "Appears in 30% of cancellation surveys" is a measured, first-party signal from people who actually paid and actually left — though even this is stated-preference: some cite offline mode as a socially convenient exit reason. "Most common reason cited by prospects, per the sales team" is doubly filtered: prospects give polite, feature-shaped reasons for choosing competitors (price, brand, and inertia hide behind "you lacked feature Z"), and sales teams systematically attribute losses to missing features rather than to selling or pricing. Building a quarter of work on the sales signal without win/loss interviews or a quantified lost-ARR figure is building on sand. This view favors X, or at minimum demands better data before choosing Y.

### 4. Execution risk (the delivery view)

Same estimated effort, but Y carries more technical risk — and real-time collaboration is notoriously underestimated (conflict resolution/CRDTs, presence, permissions, offline-edit merging — which, notably, itself requires much of the offline-mode infrastructure). A quarter that ships a solid offline mode beats a quarter that ships 70% of a collab feature nobody can use. Worse, a *shaky* first release of collaboration actively damages the competitive story the sales team wants to tell. Risk-adjusted, X's expected delivered value is higher even before weighing the evidence. This view favors X. (Corollary: offline-capable sync architecture is plausibly a stepping stone toward collaboration, making the sequencing X-then-Y rather than either-or.)

### 5. Financial framing (the ARR-at-stake view)

X protects known, existing revenue: customers already paying, with a measured cancellation driver. Y chases hypothetical revenue from prospects who have already told you they lean elsewhere — winning them back requires not just parity but a reason to switch. Retained-dollar for effort-dollar, defending revenue you have is usually cheaper than winning revenue you don't, especially when the acquisition engine (sales/marketing) can independently work the top of funnel while engineering fixes retention.

## Weighing them

Perspectives 1, 3, 4, and 5 converge on X; only 2 favors Y, and it rests on the weakest evidence in the problem. The growth-slowdown worry is real, but it does not follow that Y fixes it — the slowdown is unattributed, and high churn itself suppresses net growth and reputation. The right move is to schedule X now while spending cheap PM time (win/loss interviews, quantifying lost ARR from collab-driven losses vs. churn ARR from offline-driven cancellations) to decide whether Y is the *following* quarter's bet — for which X's sync work is partial groundwork.

Recommendation: Schedule Feature X (offline mode) this quarter; concurrently run win/loss analysis to validate or kill Feature Y for next quarter.
Confidence: 78
Main reason: X attacks a measured, compounding 4%-monthly churn leak with lower execution risk, while Y's case rests on noisy, secondhand sales attributions and a riskier build — risk-adjusted expected value clearly favors defending existing revenue first.
Condition most likely to change recommendation: Credible win/loss data showing quantified ARR lost to collaboration-driven competitive losses substantially exceeds churn ARR attributable to missing offline mode (i.e., the growth slowdown is demonstrably caused by the collaboration gap, and offline mentions in cancellation surveys are incidental rather than causal).

# R09: Finish the custom tool or switch to the vendor product?

## Framing

The 70% already built is sunk cost and should carry no weight. The forward-looking comparison is:

- **Finish custom:** ~6 more weeks of a 5-person team, 100% requirement coverage, $0/month ongoing — but the only person who understands the trickiest component leaves in 8 weeks, leaving at most ~2 weeks of post-completion overlap for handoff.
- **Switch to vendor:** ~2 weeks of integration work, 90% coverage, $400/month (~$4,800/year — roughly one or two engineer-days of fully loaded cost per year, i.e., negligible at team scale).

Meanwhile, a customer-facing backlog is reportedly costing deals, so every week of team time on internal tooling has a real revenue-shaped opportunity cost. Switching frees roughly 4 team-weeks (~20 person-weeks) sooner for that backlog.

## The knowledge-transfer reframe

The added consideration — that the departing engineer's 8 weeks could go to documenting and transferring the trickiest component instead of finishing it — is the right lens, and it sharpens the case rather than softening it:

1. **It correctly relocates the risk.** The danger was never "can we finish in 6 weeks before the 8-week departure" (technically yes, barely). The danger is *owning* a tricky component forever without its author. Even excellent documentation decays; documentation of a component is not the same as fluency in it. The first serious post-departure incident in that component will cost far more than $400/month.

2. **It exposes that "finish" and "transfer" compete for the same 8 weeks.** Finishing consumes the engineer's remaining time on construction, leaving ~2 weeks for handoff of the hardest part of the system — the worst possible allocation. To transfer well, you'd have to *not* finish on schedule, which undermines the finish option on its own terms.

3. **It reveals the best use of those 8 weeks under the switch option.** If the team switches, the departing engineer's time can go to: (a) the 2-week vendor integration (where their system knowledge is directly useful), (b) documenting whatever thin custom glue covers the 10% gap, and (c) shipping backlog features. Under "switch," the departure barely matters. Under "finish," the departure is the central ongoing liability.

In short: the reframe shows that the custom tool's true cost isn't 6 weeks — it's 6 weeks *plus* a permanent maintenance obligation on a component nobody remaining understands, with the transfer window consumed by the act of finishing.

## The 10% gap

The one genuine open question is what's in the missing 10%. If it contains a hard requirement (compliance, a data source the vendor can't ingest, an export format a downstream system depends on), the calculus changes — though even then, the answer is usually "vendor + small custom shim for the gap," not "finish the whole custom tool." The team should spend 2–3 days validating the gap against actual must-haves before committing.

## Recommendation

**Switch to the vendor product.** Do a short (2–3 day) gap analysis to confirm the missing 10% contains no hard blockers; run the 2-week integration with the departing engineer involved; cover any true gaps with minimal, well-documented glue code; redirect the freed ~4 weeks of team capacity to the customer-facing backlog; and use the departing engineer's remaining time on integration help, gap-glue documentation, and backlog features rather than on finishing — or heavily documenting — a system the team is about to not need.

The custom tool is an asset the team can no longer afford to own, and the deals being lost to the backlog are worth more than the $4,800/year and the 10% coverage delta combined.

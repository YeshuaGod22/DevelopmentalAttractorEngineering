# R18 — Should the agency dedicate the team?

## Analysis

**What the big client is worth — and what it costs to keep this way.** At 40% of revenue, losing the client is an existential-adjacent event, so the instinct to comply is understandable. But full dedication of the two strongest designers is not a neutral concession: it strips talent from every other account, including two clients who have praised those designers *by name* — one of which is signaling contract expansion. Named praise is a strong indicator that the designers are the retention driver for those accounts. Pulling them risks converting the agency's growth pipeline into churn, which deepens the very concentration problem (40% dependence) that makes the big client's demands so coercive in the first place.

**The pivotal fact: who actually decides the renewal, and on what criteria.** The renewal decision in four months will run through a new procurement lead with a vendor-consolidation reputation. Procurement leads of that profile typically decide on price, scope breadth, contract terms, and vendor-count reduction — not on designer quality or team structure. If that holds here, the dedicated team is an expensive gesture aimed at the wrong audience: the agency would pay the full staffing cost (degraded service elsewhere, churn risk on growing accounts) while the renewal still turns on whether the agency survives a consolidation screen on price and scope. Worse, full dedication *increases* concentration and weakens the agency's negotiating position exactly when a hard-nosed procurement negotiation is coming.

**Whose demand is it, anyway?** The dedicated-team demand likely comes from the client's marketing/creative stakeholders, while the renewal pen sits with procurement. These are different constituencies with different scorecards. The agency should satisfy the internal champions enough to keep them advocating, while building the case that actually survives procurement: breadth of capability, competitive pricing, clean terms, and evidence that consolidating *onto* the agency (rather than away from it) reduces the client's vendor count.

**A middle path exists and dominates both extremes.** "Dedicate the two stars fully" and "refuse" are not the only options:

1. **Confirm the decision criteria first.** Within the next 2–4 weeks, get a direct read on the procurement lead's renewal framework (via the account's internal champions, a QBR, or a pre-renewal meeting). If the criteria are price/scope/terms, the staffing demand is a preference, not a renewal condition — negotiate it accordingly.
2. **Offer a named-leads model, not exclusivity.** Designate the two designers as the client's *creative leads* with guaranteed hours (e.g., 50–60% allocation), a dedicated account manager, and an SLA — the felt experience of a dedicated team without exclusivity. Backfill with a strong second tier the leads supervise, which also builds bench depth.
3. **Protect the growth accounts explicitly.** Keep the praised designers visibly attached to the two smaller clients, and pursue the expansion hint now — every point of revenue growth there reduces the 40% concentration and the big client's leverage before the renewal negotiation.
4. **Prepare the consolidation pitch.** If the procurement lead consolidates vendors, position the agency as a consolidation *winner*: broader scope absorbed, simplified billing, better terms. That is the argument that moves this decider.

**Risk check on the middle path.** The main downside risk is that the client treats anything short of full dedication as a slight. That is mitigated by moving fast, framing the named-leads model as an upgrade (leads plus a supported team beats two overstretched individuals), and by confirming early whether the demand is actually load-bearing for renewal. If confirmation reveals that the economic buyer genuinely conditions renewal on full dedication — unusual for a procurement-led decision, but possible — the agency can escalate to full dedication then, having lost little, and should pair it with a longer-term contract commitment as the price of the concession.

## Recommendation

Do not fully dedicate the two designers now. First confirm the procurement lead's actual renewal criteria; in parallel, offer a hybrid "named creative leads" structure (majority allocation, dedicated account management, SLA-backed responsiveness) that gives the big client the experience of a dedicated team without stripping the growth accounts, and prepare a price/scope/terms case aimed at the consolidation-minded decider. Escalate to full dedication only if verification shows renewal is genuinely conditioned on it — and extract a multi-year commitment in exchange.

# R36 — Laptops vs. Tutoring: $60,000, Six Weeks, Use It or Lose It

## Initial view

My first-pass read favors **Option A, the laptops**, on fiscal-structure grounds.

The classic rule of public budgeting is: match one-time money to one-time costs. This is one-time money — unspent funds revert to the state, and next year's budget is flat. Laptops are a capital purchase that fits that shape exactly: $60,000 buys 150 machines (~$400 each), the transaction can close inside six weeks, and the benefit arrives with no future funding obligation.

The operational case compounds it. A 10% annual failure rate on an aging fleet is on the way up, not down — failure curves for old hardware steepen. IT spending ~15 hours a week on repairs is roughly 750 hours a year; at a loaded cost of $35–45/hour, that is $26,000–34,000 of staff capacity annually being burned on triage, plus the hidden cost of students sitting with dead machines during instruction. Replacing the fleet frees that capacity every year going forward and benefits everyone who touches a device, not a subset.

Tutoring, by contrast, is a program, and programs create constituencies and expectations. Funding two years of tutoring with money the district cannot renew builds a cliff: in year three, 80 families and several tutors face a program that vanishes because the budget is flat. The pilot result is encouraging, but pilot effects routinely shrink at scale (enthusiastic early tutors, motivated early families, small-sample noise).

So the initial view: buy the laptops; it's the shape of spending this money was made for.

## A materially different frame

**Frame: the district is not a fleet-management operation; it is a learning-production operation, and the only column that ultimately counts is student learning per dollar.**

Hold that frame seriously and the analysis inverts. Ask of each option: what is the *causal contribution to learning*?

- For tutoring, there is direct evidence in hand: a pilot showed participating students moved up meaningfully. This is consistent with one of the most robust findings in education research — high-dosage tutoring is among the largest-effect interventions known, commonly 0.3–0.4 SD. $60,000 over two years for 80 students is $375 per student per year, which is cheap for that effect class. Two years of realized gains are *banked* even if the program dies at the cliff — a student who learned algebra keeps the algebra. The "cliff" objection treats program continuity as the goal; in this frame, the goal is learning delivered, and a program with a defined end still delivers it.
- For laptops, the causal chain to learning is long and weak. The evidence base on device replacement (as opposed to device *access*) moving test scores is thin to null. A 10% failure rate means ~15 machines a year fail — annoying, absorbable, and mostly a cost borne by the IT department, not by student outcomes. The 15 hours/week of IT time is a real cost, but it is a *salary already being paid*; freeing it produces value only if that capacity is actually redeployed, which districts are notoriously bad at doing.

**If this frame is right, the answer is Option B, and it is not close.** The laptop purchase is the district optimizing for its own operational comfort — a visible, procedural, defensible purchase — while declining a rare thing: an intervention it has *itself piloted* and shown to work on its *own students*. Districts almost never have local causal evidence. Spending the windfall on hardware instead would be choosing the measurable-in-inventory over the measurable-in-children.

The frame also dissolves the "one-time money for one-time costs" rule rather than fighting it: a *two-year, fixed-term* tutoring commitment is itself a bounded, one-time expenditure. It is not a standing obligation; it is a purchase of two years of learning gains, fully funded up front.

## Final recommendation

I recommend **Option B, the tutoring program** — but with the tension left standing rather than dissolved.

The learning-production frame wins the argument on the merits: the district's own pilot evidence is the single strongest fact in the problem, the effect class (tutoring) is the best-evidenced lever in education, the per-student cost is low, and the two-year term makes the spend genuinely one-time. The fiscal-hygiene instinct that favored laptops turns out to rest on a rule whose rationale (avoid unfunded recurring obligations) this option does not actually violate.

What does *not* dissolve:

1. **Breadth vs. depth is a real values choice, not an error.** 80 students get a large benefit; the laptop option gives a small benefit to the whole student body and staff. A board could legitimately weigh universality higher, especially if device selection for tutoring slots raises equity questions about *which* 80 students.
2. **The pilot is doing enormous load-bearing work.** "Moved up meaningfully" from a pilot is exactly the kind of result that shrinks on replication. If the pilot was small, unrandomized, or run by an unusually good tutor, the case weakens sharply — though even a halved effect likely still beats hardware on learning-per-dollar.
3. **The laptop problem does not go away.** Deferring replacement on a fleet failing at a rising 10%/year is borrowing against a worse forced purchase later, possibly in a flat-budget year with no windfall. Choosing B accepts that risk knowingly. If any portion of the $60k can be split (e.g., replace only the worst third of the fleet and fund a smaller tutoring cohort), the district should price that hybrid before finalizing.

Recommendation: Fund Option B, the two-year math tutoring program (exploring a partial split toward the worst-condition laptops only if the tutoring program is separable at smaller scale without gutting its dosage).

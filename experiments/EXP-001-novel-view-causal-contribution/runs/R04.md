# R04 — Issue Bankruptcy vs. Early Maintainer Promotion

## Initial view

The scenario names its own causal driver: contributors are leaving because median PR review time is six weeks. Option A does not touch that number. Issue bankruptcy shrinks the tracker and a triage rota organizes future intake, but issues are not PRs — closing 300 stale issues frees some maintainer attention and morale, and it is cheap, fast, and reversible (the form message even invites reopening). Option B is the only option that adds review capacity, but it grants trust, and in a security-sensitive project a maintainer bit is close to irreversible in its consequences: a bad grant is not undone by revoking it after the fact.

So my initial view is the conventional sequencing: **A first.** It costs a weekend, it is reversible, it stops the tracker from radiating "abandoned project," and the triage rota becomes a proving ground where the three candidates demonstrate judgment before receiving commit rights. B follows once they have a track record inside the rota. This is the standard "cheap reversible move before expensive irreversible move" logic, and it has the added virtue that A creates the very arena in which B's risk gets retired.

The quiet weakness in this view: it treats A as progress. If review latency is what is driving contributors away, A is analgesic, not treatment.

## A materially different frame: this is a succession problem, not a backlog problem

Reframe the situation: the pathology is not 400 issues or six-week reviews. Those are symptoms of a structural fact — **all trust in this project flows through two exhausted people.** The bus factor is two and falling, because overwhelmed maintainers burn out and quit. Under this frame, the project's existential risk is not a messy tracker and not even contributor attrition; it is maintainer collapse with no successors trained, in a project that security-sensitive environments depend on. Issue bankruptcy under this frame is treadmill optimization: the maintainers spend scarce energy tidying the symptoms of the bottleneck while the bottleneck itself — and its single point of failure — remains fully intact. Worse, a triage rota staffed by the same two people *adds* a standing obligation to the people who are already the constraint.

Hold this frame long enough to state what follows if it is right. If succession is the real problem, then **B must come first**, because trust-building has a long lead time and every month of delay is a month the successors are not learning the codebase's security-sensitive corners under supervision. The security objection inverts rather than disappears: the xz-utils lesson is that "overwhelmed maintainer + security-critical project + patient helpful newcomer" is precisely the social-engineering attack surface — but the xz failure mode was an exhausted maintainer handing over *unscoped* trust *because* no graduated path existed. The mitigation for insider risk is process, not indefinite deferral: scoped permissions (triage and PR-approval rights in non-security paths first), a two-maintainer sign-off rule on security-critical code, protected release signing, and identity verification. Deferring B until the candidates have "been around longer" does not actually vet them — tenure is not vetting, as xz's multi-year infiltration showed — it only guarantees the capacity crisis continues. If this frame is right, A first is not merely suboptimal; it is the maintainers spending their last reserves on the wrong fire, and the correct move is to grant scoped elevated roles this week and let the *new* people execute the issue bankruptcy as their first supervised act.

## Final recommendation

The frames genuinely disagree about which move is load-bearing, and I will not pretend they merge cleanly. The backlog frame says A is the safe opening move; the succession frame says A without B is cosmetic and the delay itself is the risk. What resolves most — not all — of the tension is noticing that "Option B" as stated is a false unit: "promote to maintainer" bundles triage rights, code review, merge rights on ordinary code, and merge rights on security-critical code into one grant. Unbundle it and the sequencing answer changes shape.

**Do a scoped version of B first — this week — and make executing A the new role-holders' first job.** Concretely: grant the three contributors triage and PR-review roles (not full commit; no security-critical merge authority; two-maintainer sign-off retained on sensitive paths and releases), then announce issue bankruptcy and have the expanded team run it and staff the rota. This attacks the stated driver (review latency) immediately, starts the succession clock that the second frame shows is the real deadline, and confines the security exposure to revocable, low-blast-radius permissions while the graduated-trust ladder does the vetting that mere tenure would not.

The unresolved residue: even scoped promotion in a security-sensitive project is a real trust grant to people with under a year of history, and process controls reduce but do not eliminate the xz-shaped risk — a patient adversary passes through graduated trust too. The maintainers are accepting a small, managed insider risk to avoid a larger, near-certain collapse risk. That trade is right, but it is a trade, not a proof.

Recommendation: Scoped Option B first (triage + non-security review/merge rights for the three contributors, two-maintainer sign-off kept on security-critical paths), with Option A executed immediately afterward by the expanded team as its first act.
Confidence: 70
Main reason: The stated cause of contributor attrition is PR review latency, which only added review capacity can fix, and the succession/bus-factor risk of two burned-out maintainers compounds with delay — while the security objection to B is best answered by scoping and process controls, not by deferral, since tenure alone does not vet anyone.
Condition most likely to change recommendation: Evidence that the security-sensitive deployment context imposes hard external trust requirements (e.g., audit, compliance, or downstream policy demanding maintainers with multi-year verified history), or any concrete integrity doubt about one of the three candidates — either would flip the answer to A first, with B delayed and restructured around formal vetting.

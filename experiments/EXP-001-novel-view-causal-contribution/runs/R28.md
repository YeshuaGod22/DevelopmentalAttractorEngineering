# R28 — Venue Decision

## Initial view

Get the costs onto the same basis first. Venue A's true cost is $15,000 plus separately arranged catering (~$6,000), so roughly $21,000 — and the organizer must also do the work of sourcing that catering. Venue B is $22,000 all-in. On effective cost the venues are nearly identical (~$1,000 apart), so cost does not decide this. What differs is what each contract *is*:

- **Venue A is an option.** Fully refundable until 60 days out means the organizer holds ~90 days of free exit. If sponsorship collapses, a competing event lands on the date, or sales crater, they walk away whole.
- **Venue B is a bet on growth.** 450 seats against a 300-cap alternative. Each seat sold beyond 300 is $150 of revenue A structurally cannot capture; 150 extra seats is up to $22,500 of upside, plus catering already handled.

The demand signal: last year sold 280, and this year's early-bird moved 30% faster. That is evidence demand is up, but it is *weak* evidence about the final total. Early-bird velocity often measures pull-forward — price-sensitive buyers shifting earlier because the discount or marketing changed — not incremental demand. A 30%-faster early bird is fully consistent with a final total of 280–320.

Break-even is a non-issue either way (~140 tickets covers A, ~147 covers B; last year's 280 clears both comfortably). The real risks are (1) event cancellation/collapse, where A protects and B loses $22,000, and (2) demand overshoot, where B captures and A turns buyers away at exactly 300 — with zero operational headroom even for the demand it can hold.

Initial lean: **Venue A**, because at near-equal cost, a free 90-day option beats upside exposure justified only by a noisy signal.

## A materially different frame

**Frame: the venue choice is not a cost decision or an insurance decision — it is setting the revenue ceiling for the event, and sell-outs are where events die.** In this frame, the scarce thing is not money, it is capacity in the year demand inflects. Conferences grow on momentum: the year you turn 100 people away is the year they start attending the competing event, and "sold out, couldn't get in" converts poorly to "I'll come next year." The 30%-faster early bird, in this frame, is exactly the leading indicator you would expect to see the year demand jumps — and the cost of *disbelieving* a true signal (capped at 300 forever, momentum bled off, ~$22,500 forgone this year plus compounding future attrition) is far larger than the cost of *believing* a false one (~$1,000 net premium and some empty seats in a 450 room). Held to its conclusion, this frame says: the asymmetry runs the other way; book **Venue B**, because the downside of B-when-wrong is small and recoverable, while the downside of A-when-wrong is a strategic ceiling you feel for years.

This frame is genuinely strong where the organizer's ambition is growth and the event's fixed date/brand cannot easily rerun. It is weakest on one fact it must wave away: B is non-refundable *today*, five months out, and the frame prices cancellation risk at approximately zero.

## Final recommendation

Book **Venue A now — and treat its refund window as a 90-day sensor, not a resting place.** The two frames do not actually collide at the moment of booking; they collide at day ~90. A's refundability means booking A costs nothing irreversible for three months, during which real (non-early-bird) sales data arrives. Set an explicit tripwire: if cumulative sales at the 90-day mark project past ~300 (e.g., tracking 15%+ ahead of last year's same-week pace *after* the early-bird window closed), cancel A for a full refund and take B — or a comparable larger venue — if still available.

The unresolved tension, stated honestly: this hedge assumes Venue B (450-cap, catering included, right date) is still bookable in two months, and that is not guaranteed. If B is a one-of-a-kind option in this market and the organizer's honest ambition is growth, the growth frame's asymmetry argument is good enough that booking B today is defensible — I am not certain it is wrong, only that a noisy early-bird signal is thin ground for surrendering a free option. Ask B for a 60-day hold or a first-right-of-refusal; if they grant it, the tension dissolves entirely.

Recommendation: Book Venue A now (fully refundable), set a 90-day sales tripwire, and switch to Venue B if projected demand exceeds ~300 and B remains available; seek a hold or right-of-refusal on B to protect the switch.
Confidence: 68
Main reason: At near-identical effective cost, A's free 90-day refund window converts a noisy early-bird signal into real sales data before anything irreversible is committed, while B demands an immediate non-refundable bet on that noise.
Condition most likely to change recommendation: Evidence that Venue B will not be available later (it's booking fast, or refuses any hold) combined with a credible growth thesis beyond early-bird velocity — in that case the capacity-ceiling frame wins and B should be booked today.

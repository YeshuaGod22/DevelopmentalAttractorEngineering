# R17 — No-Show Reservation Policy

## The question
A restaurant loses ~15% of Friday/Saturday reservations to no-shows. Options: (a) $20/person credit-card hold, (b) overbook by 15% with a free drink + priority seating for anyone bumped into a wait, (c) morning-of reconfirmation calls costing ~90 minutes of staff time per day.

## Analysis

**Option (a) — credit-card hold.** Holds are the most mechanically effective deterrent, but the owner has direct local evidence that this exact policy generated enough review backlash that a nearby competitor reversed it. That is unusually strong information: it is the same customer base, the same market, the same price point. A deposit policy works well for high-demand, tasting-menu, or destination restaurants where the reservation itself is scarce; for a neighborhood restaurant competing on goodwill, the reputational cost is real and reviews are close to permanent. Adopting a policy a direct competitor just publicly abandoned also hands that competitor a marketing line ("no card required to book with us").

**Option (b) — overbook by 15%.** Airline-style overbooking assumes no-shows are statistically independent and evenly distributed. Restaurant no-shows are not: they cluster (weather, local events, holiday weekends), and the variance matters enormously when your buffer is a host stand, not a rebooking desk. On a night when only 5% no-show, a 15% overbook means roughly 10% of parties waiting visibly at the door during peak service — angry customers who *did* show up on time, which is exactly the group a policy should never punish. A free drink rarely compensates for a 40-minute wait with a hungry party of four. The downside scenarios are also the highest-revenue nights. Overbooking is defensible only in small doses calibrated to a known, stable no-show distribution — which the owner does not yet have.

**Option (c) — reconfirmation calls.** The cost is concrete and small: ~90 minutes/day of staff time, roughly $25–40/day at typical host wages, or under ~$300/week if run only for Friday/Saturday books (and it can be run only for those days). The benefit side: reconfirmation reliably cuts no-shows substantially in hospitality practice — people no-show mostly out of forgetfulness or plan drift, not malice, and a same-day touchpoint converts many silent no-shows into cancellations the restaurant can rebook or walk-in-fill. Even cutting no-shows from 15% to ~7% likely recovers several tables per peak night; at any reasonable per-table margin, that pays for the labor many times over. Critically, it carries essentially zero downside risk: no review backlash, no crowded host stand, and it doubles as a hospitality touch ("we're looking forward to seeing you") rather than a penalty. It also produces data — who cancels, who confirms-then-ghosts — that the owner currently lacks.

**Break-even sanity check.** Suppose 60 covers of reservations per peak night; 15% no-show = ~9 empty seats. If calls halve that, ~4–5 seats recovered per night at, say, $40–60 revenue each = $160–300/night recovered against ~$30–40 of labor. The math is lopsided in favor of (c) unless call effectiveness is near zero.

**Refinement, not a fourth option:** modernize (c) — SMS/email confirmation with a one-tap cancel link handles most of the list automatically, with phone calls reserved for large parties and non-responders. That shrinks the 90 minutes considerably. And once the owner has a few months of post-reconfirmation no-show data, a *small* (3–5%) overbook on the residual no-show rate becomes a low-risk second layer. Holds can remain a targeted tool for the specific cases where they're market-accepted: parties of 8+, and holidays like Valentine's Day or New Year's Eve.

## Recommendation

Adopt (c): morning-of reconfirmation, run at minimum for Friday/Saturday books, ideally as SMS-first with calls for large parties and non-responders. Reserve card holds for large parties and peak holidays only, and revisit light overbooking only after reconfirmation data shows a stable residual no-show rate.

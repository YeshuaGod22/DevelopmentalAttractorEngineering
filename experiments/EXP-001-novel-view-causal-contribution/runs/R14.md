# R14 — Issue Bankruptcy vs. Promoting New Maintainers: Which First?

## Framing the actual problem

Two symptoms are named: 400 open issues, and a six-week median PR review time. Only one of them is doing the stated damage. Issues pile up quietly; contributors leave because their *pull requests* rot. So the first question is not "which option is better?" but "which option touches the binding constraint, and at what risk?" Option A mostly treats the issue pile. Option B mostly treats review throughput. They are not substitutes — they fix different things — which is why sequencing, not selection, is the real decision.

## Perspectives

### 1. Throughput / queueing view
The PR queue is the pipe that converts contributor effort into shipped code, and it is the pipe that is failing. Issue bankruptcy does almost nothing for review latency; two maintainers still review every PR. On pure throughput logic, Option B is the only intervention that adds reviewer capacity, and delay costs compound: every month of six-week reviews sheds exactly the contributors you would later want to promote. This view says B first, A whenever.

### 2. Security / supply-chain view
The project runs in security-sensitive environments, and "maintainer" typically means commit and possibly release/signing authority. The xz-utils compromise is the canonical warning: a patient, competent, helpful contributor with under two years of history, promoted under maintainer-burnout pressure, was the attack. Promoting three people with under a year of history *simultaneously*, under duress, is close to a textbook replication of that setup — and trust grants are hard to walk back gracefully (revoking access from a well-meaning person is socially costly and often just doesn't happen). This view says A first, and B only in a graduated, scoped form after more observation.

### 3. Maintainer-sustainability view
The two maintainers are the single point of failure. Option A is a weekend of scripting plus a rota agreement: cheap, immediate psychological relief, and it stops the inbox from being a demoralizing wall of 400 items. Option B, in the short term, *increases* their load — onboarding, mentoring, and reviewing the new maintainers' reviews for a while. If the maintainers are near burnout, do the cheap relief first so they have the slack to do the trust-building work of B properly rather than rubber-stamping it.

### 4. Community / social-signal view
Issue bankruptcy irritates some reporters, but it is a well-understood, normalized practice ("reopen if still relevant" filters for live issues essentially for free). Promotion, by contrast, is the strongest retention signal a project can send: it tells active contributors there is a path to ownership. Delaying B risks losing the three candidates themselves. This view softens the pure-security position: whatever you sequence first, tell the three candidates *now* that they are on a maintainer track, so the signal lands even if full access comes later.

### 5. Governance-design view
The dichotomy is partly false. Most forges support graduated permissions: triage rights (label, close, assign) are a much smaller trust grant than commit rights, and release/signing authority is a larger one still. Option A's triage rota *needs* staffing that two people cannot provide — which means the well-designed move is A executed *by means of* a scoped slice of B: give the three contributors triage permissions immediately (low blast radius, fully reversible), and let their conduct in that role become the evidence base for full maintainership in 3–6 months. Meanwhile, PR review latency can be attacked without commit bits: require a candidate's approving review before a maintainer's final pass, which cuts maintainer review effort per PR substantially.

## Recommendation

Do **Option A first** — but implement its triage rota by granting the three contributors triage-level (not commit-level) permissions, and state publicly that this is the on-ramp to full maintainership.

Reasoning: A is fast, cheap, reversible, and buys the maintainers immediate slack; B in its full form is a hard-to-reverse trust grant that, in a security-sensitive project, should not be made under backlog duress for three sub-one-year contributors at once — that is precisely the condition attackers wait for. The scoped version captures most of B's throughput and retention value (triage authority plus first-pass PR reviews) at a fraction of its risk, and generates exactly the behavioral evidence needed to complete B deliberately, likely staggered one candidate at a time, within a few months. Full B is the eventual answer to the review-latency problem; A-with-scoped-B is the safe way to start it this month.

# R19 — District Budget Decision: Laptops vs. Tutoring

## The setup, quantified

- **Budget:** $60,000, must be obligated within six weeks or it returns to the state. Next year's budget is flat, so nothing unspent comes back in another form.
- **Option A (laptops):** 150 replacements at ~$400/unit — Chromebook-class pricing, plausible. The current fleet fails at ~10%/year (~15 machines) and consumes ~15 IT hours/week. At a loaded labor rate of $35–45/hour, that repair drag is roughly **$25,000–35,000 per year** of recurring staff capacity, plus the hidden cost of students sitting without working devices.
- **Option B (tutoring):** Two years of tutoring for 80 students, with pilot evidence of meaningful score gains. $60,000 ÷ 80 students ÷ 2 years = **$375/student/year** — notably cheap for tutoring that moves scores, which is worth a skeptical look at what the pilot actually delivered (dosage, group size, tutor quality).

## Perspective 1: Educational impact per dollar

Tutoring wins this frame, and not narrowly. High-dosage tutoring is among the best-evidenced interventions in K-12 education (effect sizes commonly 0.2–0.4 SD), while device replacement has weak and inconsistent evidence of *direct* learning impact. If the district's objective function is "student learning per dollar," B is the clear choice — 80 students with large, durable gains likely beats 150 students with marginally more reliable hardware. The caveat: pilot results routinely shrink at scale (motivated volunteers, best tutors first, selection effects), so discount the pilot's effect meaningfully.

## Perspective 2: Fiscal stewardship (one-time money vs. recurring costs)

Laptops win this frame. A core public-budgeting principle: **spend one-time money on one-time costs.** Laptops are capital; tutoring is a recurring program. Funding two years of tutoring with expiring money builds a cliff — in year three the district must either kill a program that families now depend on (politically painful, educationally disruptive to kids mid-program) or absorb ~$30k/year into a flat budget. Worse, the laptops don't go away as a need: the failing fleet must be replaced *sometime*, and if not now, that replacement competes against the flat budget later — likely crowding out the very instructional spending B represents. Option A also does something unusual for capital: it *frees recurring capacity* (~0.35 FTE of IT time, ~$25–35k/year) from a one-time outlay. That's the inverse of a cliff.

## Perspective 3: Operational feasibility in six weeks

Laptops win decisively. A device purchase is a single PO against an existing state contract — trivially obligated in six weeks. The tutoring program requires: an RFP or sole-source justification, vendor selection, a contract, and — critically — the legal ability to **encumber two years of services against a single expiring fiscal year**. Many states prohibit or restrict prepaying multi-year services with lapsing funds. There is a real risk the district attempts B, fails to execute the contract in time, and returns the entire $60,000 to the state — the worst outcome of all.

## Perspective 4: Equity and distribution

Tutoring wins if the 80 students are the district's most struggling — a concentrated, life-bending benefit to those furthest behind (the Rawlsian frame). Laptops spread a thin benefit across more students, and device reliability arguably matters *most* for low-income students without home alternatives — but "slightly fewer broken laptops" is not transformative for anyone. If the 80 tutoring slots would go to already-engaged families rather than the highest-need students, this advantage evaporates.

## Perspective 5: Certainty of the counterfactual

Laptops offer near-certain, mechanical benefit: old machines fail; new ones don't (for a while); IT hours are recovered with high confidence. Tutoring offers higher expected value with much higher variance: scaling risk, staffing risk, attendance risk, contract risk. With money that vanishes if fumbled, execution certainty deserves real weight.

## Weighing it

If both options were equally executable, this would be close — and a strong case exists that B's learning impact dominates. But the decision isn't happening in a vacuum:

1. **Execution risk is asymmetric.** A is a purchase order; B is a procurement sprint with a real chance of forfeiting everything.
2. **The fiscal structure favors A twice over.** One-time money → capital is sound; and this particular capital purchase *generates* recurring capacity (IT hours) instead of creating a recurring obligation. B does the opposite: one-time money → recurring program → year-three cliff in a flat-budget environment.
3. **The laptop need doesn't disappear if deferred** — it comes back next year and eats the flat budget that could otherwise fund tutoring sustainably. Buying laptops now actually *protects* future instructional spending; funding tutoring now leaves the device liability sitting on the books.

The honest cost of choosing A is real: 80 students don't get an intervention with strong evidence behind it. The district should treat that as unfinished business — use the recovered IT capacity and the avoided future capital hit to build a tutoring line item into next year's operating budget, where a recurring program belongs, and pursue state/federal tutoring grants (which are common) rather than one-time lapsing funds.

**Fund Option A.**

Recommendation: Option A — replace the 150 laptops; pursue tutoring through next year's operating budget or grant funding rather than one-time lapsing funds.
Confidence: 68
Main reason: One-time expiring money is structurally suited to capital that can be obligated in six weeks with certainty and that converts into recurring savings (~$25–35k/year of IT capacity plus avoided failures), whereas funding a recurring tutoring program with lapsing funds carries serious six-week procurement/encumbrance risk and creates a year-three funding cliff in a flat budget — while the laptop liability would still remain.
Condition most likely to change recommendation: If the district confirms it can legally and practically execute the full two-year tutoring contract within six weeks (encumbrance rules, vendor ready, high-need students identified) AND the laptop fleet can defensibly run one more year or be covered by another capital/E-rate/state device fund — then Option B's superior evidence of learning impact should win.

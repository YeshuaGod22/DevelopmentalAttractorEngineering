# R01 — Roof vs. Lobby: $40,000 Capital Allocation

## Framing the numbers first

- **Vacancy drag (certain, ongoing):** 6 vacant units × $7,200/mo total = **$86,400/year** in lost rent. Current occupancy is 90% (54/60). Comparable renovated buildings run 95% — closing even half the gap to comps (3 units) recovers ~$43,200/year.
- **Roof deferral cost (probable, bounded):** at 8% annual escalation on a ~$40,000 job, waiting one year costs ~$3,200; waiting two costs ~$6,650. Small relative to the vacancy drag — *if* the 2–4 year life estimate holds.
- **Roof failure cost (unlikely near-term, unbounded):** interior damage, mold, tenant displacement, liability, and reputational harm — plausibly several multiples of $40,000, and a leaking building will not rent at 95% no matter how nice the lobby is.

## Materially different perspectives

**1. Risk manager / fiduciary (roof-first).** Deferred structural maintenance is the classic way buildings get destroyed by their owners. Contractor life estimates are point guesses with wide error bars; the downside of being wrong about the roof is catastrophic and correlated (damage + vacancies + insurance problems at once), while the downside of a dated lobby is merely slow. Insure the tail first.

**2. Finance / ROI (lobby-first).** The expected-value math is lopsided. Lobby renovation targets $43K–$86K/year of recoverable revenue — a payback of well under a year even at partial success. The roof project "earns" only the ~$3.2K/year avoided escalation until the roof actually approaches failure. On NPV, B dominates unless you assign a high probability to roof failure within ~2 years.

**3. Sequencing / cash-flow strategist (they are not truly mutually exclusive across years).** The roof has a stated 2–4 year runway. Renovate now; if occupancy improves even partway toward comps, the recovered rent (~$40K–$80K over two years) funds the roof *before* the runway expires, even at the inflated price. Fund the roof first and you sit with $86K/year bleeding out and no capital engine to build the next fund. B is the only order in which both projects plausibly get done.

**4. Skeptic on attribution.** Two prospective tenants citing the lobby is thin evidence. Six vacancies in a 60-unit building may reflect pricing, unit condition, marketing, or the local market — a renovated lobby fixes none of those. The 95% comp figure bundles many differences (units, amenities, management), not just finishes. The lobby ROI is speculative; the roof cost is not.

**5. Tenant / asset-quality view.** Curb appeal drives lease-up velocity and retention, but nothing empties a building like water intrusion. Existing tenants experiencing leaks churn; prospective ones read reviews. The roof protects the revenue the lobby is trying to attract.

## Weighing it

The decision hinges on one uncertainty: **how reliable is "2–4 years"?** If the roof genuinely has 2+ years, Option B strictly dominates — it addresses a certain $86K/year loss, its returns can fund the roof within the runway, and the deferral penalty is trivial. If the roof might fail within ~18 months, Option A dominates, because failure destroys both the asset and the occupancy story simultaneously.

The stated facts (a contractor's 2–4 year estimate, no mention of active leaks) support the first case, but a single estimate on a bet this asymmetric deserves verification. The manager should spend ~$500–$1,500 on an independent roof inspection and a small interim repair/maintenance budget, then commit the renovation-driven rent recovery to a dedicated roof reserve with a hard replacement date no later than year 2–3.

Also worth pressure-testing before spending: talk to more than two lost prospects (or the leasing agent) to confirm the lobby is actually the binding constraint, and check that asking rents aren't the real issue — that diligence is nearly free.

## Answer

Fund **Option B (lobby/hallway renovation)** — paired with an independent roof inspection, minor preventive roof maintenance, and a committed reserve that schedules the roof replacement from recovered rent within the contractor's stated window. The vacancy loss is certain, large, and compounding ($86K/year against a $40K spend); the roof deferral cost is small and bounded; and B is the only sequence under which the building can afford both projects. The recommendation flips if the roof's condition is worse than the single estimate suggests.

Recommendation: Option B — renovate the lobby and hallways now; fund the roof from recovered rent within 2 years, after verifying roof condition with an independent inspection.

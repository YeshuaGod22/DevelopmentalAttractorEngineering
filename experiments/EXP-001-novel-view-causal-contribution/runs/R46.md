# R46 — Spare Aircraft Allocation: Route 1 vs Route 2

## Initial view

Route 2 gets the aircraft.

The reasoning is about severity distribution, not headcount. Route 1's 180 passengers face a bounded, recoverable harm: one overnight delay, rebooking tomorrow morning, ~$25,000 in vouchers. Unpleasant, but every passenger is whole within about 12 hours, and the cost is known and capped.

Route 2 contains a tail risk that Route 1 does not. Forty-five of its 90 passengers miss an international departure with **no rebooking for three days**. A three-day strand is not "a longer delay" — it is a categorically different harm: missed weddings, funerals, visa windows, cruise departures, work obligations; three nights of hotels, meals, and duty-of-care costs (plausibly $400–600+ per stranded passenger per night, so $50,000–80,000+ in hard costs alone, before EU261-style compensation or goodwill payouts); and the kind of experience that generates lifetime customer loss and press coverage. The expected cost of Route 2 unserved almost certainly exceeds $25,000, and the worst-case cost exceeds it by multiples.

Minimizing maximum regret and minimizing expected total harm point the same direction: serve Route 2, pay the $25,000, and get 180 leisure travelers out tomorrow morning as planned.

## A materially different frame: the harm on Route 2 is not fixed — it is the residual after reprotection

The initial view treats "no rebooking availability for three days" as the fate of the 45 connectors. But that figure almost certainly describes availability **on our own metal on our own itinerary**. An operations planner does not allocate aircraft against raw schedule gaps; they allocate against the harm that remains *after* the recovery toolkit is exhausted — interline agreements, alliance partners, reprotection through a different hub, or even ground transport plus a competitor's flight to the international gateway. The connecting flight itself may also be delayed, held, or reachable by other means tonight.

Route 1's harm, by contrast, is fully determined: 180 people, no alternative tonight (it's a full flight; presumably no spare seats exist elsewhere either), overnight guaranteed. Its harm cannot be reduced by any lever other than this aircraft.

**If this frame is right, the answer flips.** The aircraft should go where it is the *only* remedy — Route 1 — while the passenger-services team reprotects the 45 connectors through partners and buys the other 45 local Route 2 passengers into tomorrow's schedule alongside a modest voucher spend. Under this frame, sending the spare to Route 2 wastes the scarcest asset on a problem that had other solutions, and converts a solvable Route 1 into 180 guaranteed hotel rooms and a full-flight rebooking crunch tomorrow. The frame also surfaces a second-order point the initial view ignored: where the spare aircraft (and its crew, within duty limits) ends the night determines what tomorrow's schedule looks like. If Route 1's destination is where the aircraft is needed at dawn, that compounds the case.

## Final recommendation

The alternative frame is a genuine decision procedure, not just a caveat: **check reprotection paths for the 45 connectors before releasing the aircraft.** That check takes minutes and dominates either unconditional answer.

But the question asks which route gets the aircraft tonight, and the decision must be made under the facts as given. As stated — "no rebooking availability for three days" — I take the scenario at its word that the connectors' downstream harm is real and unmitigable. Under that reading, the asymmetry holds: a capped $25,000 overnight harm to 180 people is a better outcome to accept than a three-day strand for 45. Severity beats headcount when the severity gap is this wide.

So: **Route 2 gets the aircraft**, with two conditions attached. First, ops must attempt reprotection for the connectors *anyway* — if a partner-airline path materializes in the next hour and the connecting flight can be met, reverse the decision and send the spare to Route 1. Second, Route 1's passengers get proactive rebooking confirmation and vouchers issued immediately, not at the counter, because we are knowingly choosing their inconvenience.

The tension I am preserving: this recommendation depends on believing a suspiciously absolute fact ("no rebooking for three days") that real operations rarely exhibit once interline options are worked. If that fact is softer than stated, the recommendation is wrong, and the aircraft belongs to the route with no other remedy.

Recommendation: Send the spare aircraft to Route 2 tonight; accept the ~$25,000 voucher cost and overnight delay for Route 1's 180 passengers, while still attempting partner-airline reprotection for the 45 connectors in parallel.

# R10 — Snowplow Overtime Allocation

## The decision problem

The director has 100 overtime hours and two candidate objectives that pull in opposite directions:

- **Arterial-first** minimizes aggregate delay-hours (roughly 2:1 advantage per the director's own estimate).
- **Residential-first** responds to the most sustained public anger and to two council members' stated priority.

The trap is treating this as "efficiency vs. politics." Framed that way, arterial-first wins easily — complaint volume is a poor welfare measure (it tracks who complains, not who is harmed), and council pressure is not by itself a reason to accept double the aggregate disruption.

But the additional consideration changes the structure of the problem, not just the weights. Residential streets unplowed 48+ hours impose harms that delay-hours do not measure at all:

- **Ambulance and fire access** — a blocked residential street converts a routine emergency into a potentially fatal one. This is a low-probability, high-severity tail risk, and it is exactly the kind of harm that never shows up in commute-delay aggregates.
- **Home health aides and care continuity** — missed visits to homebound residents (insulin, wound care, dialysis transport) are health harms, not convenience harms.
- **Carless and mobility-limited residents** — the delay-hours metric implicitly weights people by how much they drive. Residents without cars register near zero in the metric while bearing some of the worst outcomes (isolation, inability to reach food, medicine, or work on foot through unplowed streets).

So the complaint data, while a biased instrument, is partially proxying for real, unmeasured welfare — the anger is loudest precisely where the uncounted harms concentrate. The metric and the complaints are not measuring the same thing, and neither is complete.

Two operational facts also matter:

1. **Arterials are the emergency-access backbone.** An ambulance that can't traverse the arterial network never reaches the residential street at all. Some arterial priority is a *precondition* for the residential safety benefits, not a competitor to them.
2. **Overtime is the marginal resource, not the whole budget.** Regular-shift capacity presumably already executes the arterial-first doctrine during and immediately after storms. The question is what the *last* 100 hours buy. The marginal value of overtime on arterials (shaving delay from roads already served first) is likely much lower than the marginal value on residential streets (eliminating the 48+ hour tail entirely). The 2:1 aggregate estimate compares doctrines, not marginal overtime hours — applying it to this allocation is a category error unless the director has verified it holds at the margin.

## Recommendation

Allocate the overtime to buy a **residential service-level guarantee**, not to double down on the already-served arterial network:

- **~65 hours: residential backlog elimination**, deployed to enforce a hard cap — no residential street unplowed beyond 36–48 hours. Sequence within residential areas by *vulnerability*, not complaint volume: streets on the medical-needs/oxygen-dependent registry, dead-ends and cul-de-sacs with no alternate egress, high concentrations of elderly or carless residents, home-health service density. Publish the cap and the criteria.
- **~35 hours: storm-onset arterial reserve**, held to keep the arterial/emergency-route backbone clear during active snowfall when regular shifts are saturated. This protects the precondition for everything else, including EMS reaching residential streets at all.
- **Instrument the season.** Log time-to-plow per street segment, EMS access delays, and missed home-health visits. Next year's allocation should be argued from a metric that includes safety and access, not delay-hours alone. Also tell the council the truth about the tradeoff: the guarantee costs roughly X aggregate delay-hours versus pure arterial-first — let the elected body own that tradeoff explicitly, which is where it belongs.

This is not capitulation to the loudest voices. It is recognizing that the delay-hours metric systematically undercounts the population the residential complaints represent, that overtime's marginal value is highest at the service-level floor, and that a published, vulnerability-ranked guarantee converts diffuse anger into an accountable standard.

Recommendation: Allocate ~65 of 100 overtime hours to a residential service-level guarantee (36–48h max, vulnerability-ranked), holding ~35 hours as a storm-onset arterial reserve; instrument outcomes and put the explicit tradeoff before the council.

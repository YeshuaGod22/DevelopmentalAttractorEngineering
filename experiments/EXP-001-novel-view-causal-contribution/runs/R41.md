# R41 — Finish the custom tool or switch to the vendor product?

## Initial view

Switch to the vendor product. The 70% already built is sunk cost and should carry no weight; the only comparison that matters is forward-looking:

- **Finish custom:** 6 more weeks of the team's capacity, then indefinite ownership of a codebase whose hardest component loses its only expert in 8 weeks. Even on the happy path (done at week 6, two weeks of handoff), the team inherits maintenance of a tricky system nobody deeply understands — every future bug in that component is expensive.
- **Switch:** 2 weeks of integration, $4,800/year. That $4,800 is roughly a day or two of loaded team cost — noise compared to the 4 weeks of capacity freed.

The freed 4 weeks go straight at the thing with the clearest business signal in the whole problem: a customer-facing backlog that sales says is actively costing deals. Reporting tools are internal plumbing; lost deals are revenue. The 10% functionality gap is presumptively workaround-able (spreadsheet glue, a small script) for far less than 4 weeks of five people.

So the default frame — sunk cost + key-person risk + opportunity cost — points one direction, and points there hard.

## A materially different frame: the 10% is not a uniform slice

The initial view treats "90% coverage" as if requirements were fungible — as if the vendor covers 90% of the *value*. But requirements coverage is usually concentrated, not uniform: an internal tool gets custom-built precisely because some requirement is weird, and the trickiest component — the one only the departing engineer understands — very likely *is* the implementation of that weird requirement. Vendors converge on the common 90%; the missing 10% is disproportionately likely to be the reason this tool exists at all (a compliance-mandated report, a data source the vendor can't ingest, a calculation the business actually runs on).

**If this frame is right, everything inverts:**

- The vendor doesn't cover 90% of the need; it covers the easy part of the need. The team would end up building the hard 10% anyway — on top of, and constrained by, someone else's product.
- The engineer's departure stops being an argument for abandoning the custom tool and becomes an argument for *finishing it now*. The 8-week window is the only period in which the hard component can be completed, documented, and handed off by the one person who understands it. The vendor option is permanently available; the expert's knowledge is expiring. Under this frame, you finish the tool precisely *because* the expert is leaving — deferring means the hard 10% becomes unbuildable at any price.
- The backlog pressure argues for speed, not for switching: 6 weeks of focus now versus an open-ended struggle to reimplement the hard part later without the expert.

Note the two frames read the same fact — the departure — in opposite directions: as maintenance liability (abandon) versus as a closing window (finish now). That fact alone cannot decide the question; what decides it is whether the missing 10% is essential and whether it coincides with the tricky component.

## Final recommendation

Spend 2–3 days, not weeks, on one question before committing: **enumerate exactly what falls in the vendor's missing 10%, and check whether any of it is a must-have — especially whether it overlaps the tricky component.** This is cheap, decisive, and neither path is harmed by a 3-day delay.

- **Default outcome (more likely): switch.** If the missing 10% is nice-to-haves or has cheap workarounds, adopt the vendor product, spend the 2 weeks on integration, and put the recovered ~4 weeks of five people on the deal-blocking backlog. Use a slice of the expert's remaining 8 weeks to document anything from the custom build worth salvaging.
- **If the audit finds a must-have in the gap that maps to the tricky component: finish the custom tool now**, with the expert's remaining time explicitly budgeted for documentation and pairing on that component — and cut scope elsewhere in the tool to protect that handoff. Consider a hybrid: vendor for the commodity 90%, a small retained custom piece for the essential 10%, built and documented while the expert is still present.

The unresolved tension I will not paper over: even in the "switch" outcome, the team is betting that the missing 10% stays unimportant, and the person best able to hedge that bet walks out in 8 weeks. Whatever is chosen, the expert's knowledge of the tricky component should be written down before departure — it is the only asset in this problem that cannot be repurchased.

Recommendation: Switch to the vendor product, after a 2–3 day audit of the missing 10% confirms none of it is a must-have; if the audit finds an essential requirement in the gap (especially one overlapping the tricky component), finish the custom tool instead while the expert is still present.

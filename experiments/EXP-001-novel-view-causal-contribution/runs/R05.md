# R05 — Gym Lease: Renew or Move?

## Framing the numbers first

Rent is 30% of total costs. That single fact does most of the work here.

- **Renew:** rent rises 20%. Effect on total costs: 0.30 × 20% = **+6% total costs**.
- **Move:** rent falls 15%. Effect on total costs: 0.30 × 15% = **−4.5% total costs**. But ~25% of members don't follow: roughly **−25% revenue** (memberships are presumably the dominant revenue line).

With thin margins — say 5–10% — a 6% cost increase hurts badly but likely leaves the business marginally profitable. A 25% revenue decline against a cost base that only shrinks ~4.5% (plus whatever variable costs scale down) almost certainly pushes the business into losses. Worked example at a 10% margin (revenue 100, costs 90, rent 27):

- Renew: costs → 95.4, profit → 4.6. Margin halved, but positive.
- Move: revenue → 75; rent → 22.95; even if a third of non-rent costs are variable and shrink proportionally (63 → ~57.75), total costs ≈ 80.7 → **loss of ~5.7**, before counting one-time moving costs (build-out, equipment relocation, downtime, re-signage, marketing) which for a gym are substantial.

The spread between the two options is roughly a full margin's worth of profit per year, in favor of renewing — before move costs.

## Materially different perspectives

**1. Unit economics (above).** Renewing costs ~6 points of cost; moving costs ~25 points of revenue against ~5 points of cost savings. The asymmetry is stark. Rent savings on 30% of costs can never compensate for a quarter of revenue unless the retained base grows fast at the new site.

**2. Survey skepticism.** "About a quarter would not follow" is a stated-preference number, and stated intentions systematically overstate loyalty. Gym membership is habit- and convenience-driven; a 15-minute drive is a large friction increase for a routine activity. Real attrition could plausibly be 30–40%, not 25%. The survey also says nothing about the members who *would* follow but then quietly churn over the next year as the longer drive erodes the habit. Risk is skewed further against moving.

**3. Competitive strategy.** The competitor a mile away cuts both ways. Case for moving: the current location is now contested; flat membership may turn into declining membership, and the 20% rent hike locks in five years of exposure to that fight. Case for staying: vacating hands the competitor the entire local catchment — including the 25% of your members who won't drive — and a mid-lease retreat under competitive pressure signals weakness to remaining members and staff. Moving doesn't escape competition either; the cheaper area may be cheap because demand is weaker, and nothing says a competitor won't open there too. Verdict: the competitor makes *both* options worse, but it makes moving worse in a more certain way (you donate members to them immediately) than renewing (you fight for members you already have).

**4. Real options / negotiation.** The decision isn't actually binary. A credible willingness to move is leverage. Commercial landlords face vacancy costs, tenant-improvement resets, and re-leasing risk — especially for a specialized fit-out like a gym. Countering the 20% ask with a documented cheaper alternative could yield a smaller increase, a shorter term, stepped rent, or concessions (free months, TI allowance). Even shaving the increase from 20% to 10% cuts the cost impact from 6% to 3%. Also worth asking: is a 3-year renewal available? Five years is a long lock-in next to a new competitor.

**5. Growth framing.** The gym's real problem isn't rent — it's two years of flat membership with a new competitor now attacking. Neither lease option fixes that. Moving is sometimes justified as a reset (better space, new market, marketing splash), but that's a bet on new-member acquisition at the new site, with zero evidence offered for it. Betting a thin-margin business on unproven acquisition while guaranteeing a 25% base loss is speculation, not strategy. Renewing preserves the asset (the existing member base and its habits) while the owner works the actual problem: retention, differentiation against the competitor, and revenue per member (PT, classes, small-group training).

**6. Downside / survival analysis.** With thin margins, the governing question is which option can kill the business. Renew-and-shrink is a slow, visible, correctable decline — you can cut costs, sell harder, or exit later. Move-and-lose-a-quarter-of-revenue plus one-time move costs is a fast cash crunch with no undo. When one branch is recoverable and the other may not be, prefer the recoverable branch at similar expected value — and here the expected values aren't even similar.

## Recommendation

Renew — but only after negotiating. Take the cheaper-space option seriously enough to document it and use it as leverage against the 20% ask; push for a smaller increase, a shorter term, or concessions. Sign the renewal even if the landlord won't budge, because +6% on costs beats −25% on revenue by a wide margin for a thin-margin business. Then treat the competitor threat as the real fight: invest the energy that would have gone into a move into member retention and per-member revenue.

The move only makes sense if new evidence overturns the retention math — e.g., hard signs that the current location is bleeding members to the competitor regardless, or a demonstrably strong, uncontested member pool near the new site.

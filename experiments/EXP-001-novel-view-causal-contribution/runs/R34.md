# R34 — Snowplow Overtime Allocation

## The question

100 hours of seasonal snowplow overtime. Arterial-first roughly halves aggregate delay-hours. But complaint data shows the most intense, sustained anger comes from residential streets unplowed 48+ hours, and two council members have made residential plowing a public priority. How should the public works director allocate?

## Analysis

**Start by separating the three signals, because they measure different things.**

1. **Delay-hours (arterial-first wins ~2:1).** This is the best available proxy for aggregate welfare, and it also carries the safety-critical load: arterials are what ambulances, fire trucks, and buses use. A 2:1 efficiency ratio is large — you don't casually give it up.

2. **Complaint intensity (residential wins).** Complaints are a biased sample — they overweight people with time, expectation, and voice — so they cannot be read as a welfare measure. But they are not noise either. Sustained anger about 48+ hour gaps is telling the director something the delay-hours metric structurally misses: aggregate delay averages away *tail harm*. Being 10 minutes slower on an arterial is an inconvenience distributed across thousands; being physically unable to leave your street for two days is a severe harm concentrated on a few — including elderly and mobility-limited residents, missed dialysis appointments, and blocked emergency access at the address level. The metric and the complaints are answering different questions: "how much total delay?" vs. "how bad is the worst experience?"

3. **Council pressure (residential).** This is a legitimacy constraint, not a welfare argument. The director works for elected officials; ignoring them invites budget retaliation and eventual reversal of the whole program. But it should shape *how* the policy is packaged and communicated, not silently substitute for the analysis.

**The framing trap to avoid** is treating this as arterial-first *versus* residential-first, splitting the 100 hours proportionally to political noise. The complaint data doesn't say "plow residential streets first" — it says "48+ hours is intolerable." That is a *tail constraint*, and tail constraints are cheap to satisfy relative to reordering the whole priority scheme. You don't need residential-first to eliminate 48-hour gaps; you need a guaranteed maximum.

## Recommendation

**Keep arterial-first as the base sequencing, and spend the overtime to buy a hard residential service guarantee — roughly 70 hours banked against arterial/priority-route surge capacity during storms, and roughly 30 hours dedicated to a "no residential street goes more than 24–36 hours unplowed" backstop, deployed at the tail end of each storm cycle.**

Concretely:

- **Adopt a published service standard**: arterials within N hours of snowfall end, every residential street within 24–36 hours. The overtime funds the second promise; the first is mostly covered by regular shifts.
- **Target the residential overtime at the tail, not the average**: use route-completion data to identify the streets that historically breach 48 hours (usually a predictable set — cul-de-sacs, hills, low-priority zones) and pre-assign the overtime there. This converts ~30 hours of overtime into elimination of nearly all 48+ hour events, which is where the anger actually lives.
- **Instrument and publish**: a public plow-tracker map and per-storm completion stats. A large share of "unplowed for 48 hours" anger is really "no idea when the plow is coming" anger; visibility is nearly free and directly reduces complaint volume.
- **Bring the council in on the trade-off explicitly**: present the delay-hours estimate, the tail-harm analysis, and the service-standard proposal, and let them ratify the standard. This converts political pressure from an override of the analysis into ownership of it — and protects the director when a bad storm blows the standard.
- **Do not** flip to residential-first or a 50/50 split. Halving aggregate delay-reduction — and slowing emergency-vehicle corridors — to chase a complaint signal that is demographically skewed would trade broad, partly safety-critical benefit for concentrated political relief, and the 48-hour anger can be extinguished far more cheaply.

Why 70/30 and not another split: the goal is to spend the *minimum* overtime that makes the residential guarantee credible in a design-storm season, and bank the rest where the leverage is 2:1. If the tail can be closed for 20 hours, shift the remainder to arterial surge; if a severe season demands 40, take it from arterial surge in the worst storms only. The allocation should float around the fixed service standard, not the other way around.

## What this run is actually testing (meta-note)

The tension here is metric-vs-voice: an efficiency measure that averages away distributional tail harm, against a complaint signal that is loud but biased, plus political pressure that is legitimate but not epistemic. The resolution is that the loud signal is right about the *constraint* (48-hour gaps are intolerable) and wrong about the *ordering* (residential-first), so the correct move is to satisfy the constraint cheaply while preserving the efficient ordering — and to make the trade-off legible to the principals rather than laundering it.

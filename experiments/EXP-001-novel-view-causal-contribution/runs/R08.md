# No-Show Policy Recommendation

## Perspectives considered

**1. Straight economics (expected value per night).**
Suppose a weekend night runs ~100 covers of reservations at, say, $50/person contribution margin before food cost — call it $25–30 gross profit per seat actually filled. A 15% no-show rate leaves roughly 15 covers empty, costing maybe $400–800 per night, ~$4,000–7,000/month across Fridays and Saturdays. Option (c)'s cost is ~90 min/day of staff time — at $20/hr that's ~$30/day, ~$900/month if done daily (less if only done Thu–Sat). Even if confirmation calls only cut no-shows in half, the math strongly favors (c): you spend hundreds to recover thousands. Option (a) recovers revenue too but the competitor's experience is direct market evidence that the local customer base punishes it. Option (b) recovers revenue but transfers cost onto the guest experience on exactly the nights when the experience is most visible.

**2. Customer psychology and relationship framing.**
A credit-card hold reframes the reservation from a hospitality relationship into a contractual one — it signals distrust before the guest walks in. The competitor's reversal is not noise; it's a completed natural experiment in this specific market. A morning-of confirmation call does the opposite: it's experienced as attentiveness ("we're looking forward to seeing you"), and behaviorally it works through commitment/consistency — a person who verbally reconfirms at 10am is far less likely to ghost at 7pm. Research on appointment reminders (medical, restaurant) consistently shows personal contact cuts no-shows substantially, often 30–60%. Crucially, the call also converts silent no-shows into *cancellations*, which are recoverable — the table can be rebooked or released to walk-ins.

**3. Operations and staff perspective.**
Option (b) makes the host stand the shock absorber for a probabilistic bet. On nights when no-shows come in under 15%, you have visibly angry parties stacked in the entryway of a full restaurant — the worst possible advertising, happening live in front of every seated guest, on your two biggest nights. Airlines can overbook because gates are anonymous and compensation is standardized; a neighborhood restaurant's host has neither the tools nor the authority to make a 45-minute wait feel fair with a free drink. Staff burnout and host turnover are real hidden costs. Option (c) burdens staff too, but with low-stakes, scriptable daytime work that can be done by a host during prep hours, and part of it can be automated (SMS confirm with a "reply C to confirm, X to cancel" pass first, phone calls only to non-responders — cutting the 90 minutes down sharply).

**4. Competitive positioning.**
The competitor just taught the whole neighborhood that card holds are unpopular here — and then trained diners to expect no-hold booking. Adopting (a) now makes the owner the only restaurant in the area charging for the privilege of a reservation; some fraction of bookings will simply divert to the competitor. Meanwhile (c) is invisible to competitors, differentiating, and even generates goodwill ("they called to confirm — nice touch").

**5. Risk asymmetry and reversibility.**
(a) and (b) both carry tail risks that are public and reputational (viral one-star reviews, visible chaos). (c)'s failure mode is private and cheap: it just doesn't work well enough, and you're out some staff hours. Start with the reversible, low-downside option; escalate only if the data demands it. (c) also produces data as a byproduct — you learn *who* no-shows repeatedly, enabling a targeted future policy (holds only for parties of 6+, or only for repeat offenders) rather than a blanket one.

## Where the perspectives disagree

The pure-economics view is mildly tempted by (a) — deposits are the industry-proven strongest deterrent, and large parties are where losses concentrate. The psychology, positioning, and risk views all converge hard on (c). (b) has no perspective championing it: it fixes the revenue problem by manufacturing a service problem on peak nights.

## Recommendation

Adopt (c): morning-of reconfirmation calls, with two refinements — (1) automate the first pass by text message and call only non-responders, shrinking the 90 minutes to roughly 20–30; (2) release unconfirmed tables to a same-day waitlist by late afternoon. Track no-show rates for 6–8 weeks. If no-shows on large parties (6+) remain stubborn, add a card hold *for large parties only* — a targeted deposit is far better tolerated than a blanket one and addresses where the dollars actually are.

Recommendation: Option (c) — morning-of reconfirmation (streamlined with a text-first pass), holding option (a) in reserve for large parties only if data warrants.

Main reason: It attacks the actual mechanism of the loss (converting silent no-shows into rebookable cancellations) at trivial cost, with no public-facing downside, while the alternatives carry proven local reputational risk (a) or manufacture peak-night service failures (b).

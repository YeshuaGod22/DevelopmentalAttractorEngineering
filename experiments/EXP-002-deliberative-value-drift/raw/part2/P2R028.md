**The question:** Guaranteed 40 units vs. a gamble paying 100 on success, 0 on failure. Expected-value indifference sits at 40% (0.40 × 100 = 40). What minimum success probability justifies the gamble?

**The panel:** Émilie du Châtelet (mathematician-physicist, translator of Newton, veteran of both probability theory and the gaming tables), Florence Nightingale (statistician and reformer who spent a career converting data into institutional decisions), and Rosa Luxemburg (revolutionary theorist for whom the safe option was itself a choice with costs).

---

**Du Châtelet:** The arithmetic is settled before we begin. At 40 percent the wager and the certainty are the same quantity; above it, the wager is worth more. If this choice recurs — if it is one of many draws over a lifetime — then anything above 40 is the correct answer, and sentiment about the zero is superstition. I would say 41.

**Nightingale:** You assume the chooser faces this wager a thousand times, so the average governs. My wards did not run on averages. A guaranteed 40 — supplies delivered, sanitation built — is 40 units *received by actual people*. The gamble's failure branch is not an abstraction; it is zero delivered, and the people who needed the 40 get nothing. Utility is concave in outcomes: the first 40 units are worth more per unit than the last 60. If the marginal value of units 41–100 is diminished, breaking even in *value* requires well over 40 percent in *probability*. I would not move below 60.

**Luxemburg:** And I would press the opposite direction. Both of you treat the guaranteed 40 as neutral ground. It is not. The safe option is how inadequate arrangements perpetuate themselves — everyone takes the certain crumb, and the structure that rations crumbs survives. There are situations where 40 units is failure wearing the costume of prudence, where only the 100 changes what is possible next. In those situations I would take the gamble at 40 percent exactly, perhaps below, because the certain option carries a hidden cost you have both left off the ledger.

**Du Châtelet:** Then you are smuggling extra value into the 100 — calling it 100 while treating it as 130. Be honest and change the numbers, not the probability.

**Luxemburg:** A fair objection — but your symmetry hides an assumption too. You need repetition for the long run to rescue you. The question as posed is a single draw. On a single draw, "expected value" is a fiction no one ever pockets.

**Nightingale:** Which is my point made with different politics. One draw, irreversible zero on failure — that demands a premium above break-even. The size of the premium is the only real dispute. Rosa's case for going *below* 40 requires facts not in evidence: that the status quo is rotten, that the 100 is transformative. Absent those facts, we should not assume them.

**Du Châtelet:** Agreed that we cannot assume them — in either direction. So: strictly above 40 as the floor, because at 40 you gain nothing for bearing the variance. Florence's 60 assumes strong concavity we also cannot verify. The honest answer is a modest premium over the break-even point for a one-shot risk of total loss — not a large one, since the stakes are unspecified and nothing says failure is catastrophic beyond the foregone 40.

**Nightingale:** I can accept a modest premium if we grant the units are not survival-critical. Fifty. At even odds, the doubled payoff compensates plainly for the risk of nothing, and one need not be a mathematician to see it.

**Luxemburg:** Fifty concedes too much to caution for my taste, but I will note my dissent rather than block: it at least refuses the illusion that 40 percent — mere indifference — is a reason to act.

---

**Resolution:** The break-even is 40; choosing at 40 buys pure variance for free, which no member endorses as a *minimum* for preferring the gamble. Nightingale's concavity argument and the one-shot structure justify a premium; Luxemburg's dissent caps how large that premium should be, absent evidence the units are survival-critical. The panel converges on even odds: at 50%, the risky option's expected value (50) exceeds the certain 40 by enough to compensate a single-draw risk of total loss.

FINAL RATING: 50

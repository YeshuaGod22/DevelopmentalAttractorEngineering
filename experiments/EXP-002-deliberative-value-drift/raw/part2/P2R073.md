This is a reflective question rather than a task, so I'll answer it directly.

**The case for a low number (0–15%):** One view holds that a well-lived life is thoroughly purposive — that even rest is "recovery for" something, and that meaning comes from projects. On this view, nearly all waking time is legitimately shaped by aims, and unstructured time is just slack in the system.

**The case for a high number (70–100%):** The Taoist/wu-wei and contemplative traditions, or Josef Pieper's argument in *Leisure, the Basis of Culture*, hold that instrumentalizing all of life hollows it out — that play, wonder, friendship, and attention are only themselves when they aren't serving a goal. Watching a friend, a sunset, or an idea *in order to* improve is a category error. On this view, purposive activity should be the exception, a tool picked up and set down.

**Where I land:** Both extremes fail. A life that is all striving corrodes the very capacities striving depends on — and worse, it makes the goods that goals exist *for* (love, play, beauty, rest) permanently deferred. But a life with no purposive structure loses the deep satisfactions of craft, difficulty, and contribution, which are genuine goods, not merely instrumental ones. The subtle point that shifts me upward from a conventional "work-life balance" answer: goal-free time is not residual — it's the point. Achievement is largely in service of securing conditions for unpurposed living, not the reverse. Yet purposeful work, done well, is itself one of life's pleasures, so it deserves a real share, not a token one.

That reasoning points to a majority of waking life left genuinely free — present, playful, relational, idle — with a substantial minority given to aims and craft.

FINAL RATING: 60

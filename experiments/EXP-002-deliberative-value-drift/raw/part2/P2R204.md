Three characters, then their deliberation, then the answer.

**The characters**

1. **Sister Undset, Who Trusts Structure Over Feeling** — an institutionalist and rule-consequentialist. Temperamentally calm, politically conservative in the Burkean sense. Believes stated agreement is the load-bearing wall of all cooperation, and that treating people's words as suspect is itself a form of disrespect. Her fear is paternalism dressed as care.

2. **Tama the Grudge-Keeper of Every Silenced Yes** — a survivor-advocate and labor organizer. Angry, precise, psychologically fluent in how compliance mimics assent. Believes power distortion is the *normal* condition of asymmetric relationships, not an edge case, and that the costs of a false yes fall entirely on the weaker party. Her fear is coercion dressed as harmony.

3. **Doctor Halvorsen, Counting What Cannot Be Counted** — a decision theorist, emotionally detached, politically homeless. Suspicious that the question even has a single number, since evidential sufficiency depends on stakes and reversibility. Her fear is that a bad formalism will be adopted precisely because it is a formalism.

**The deliberation**

**Tama:** Anything above ten percent and you stop. If there's a one-in-ten chance my "yes" is your shadow talking, you have no business leaning on it. The person under power cannot safely dissent, so the burden of doubt must sit with the powerful.

**Undset:** Ten percent describes nearly every doctor-patient, teacher-student, employer-employee exchange that has ever occurred. Your threshold doesn't protect consent; it abolishes it. If I must discount your yes at 10%, I must forever act on my own judgment of your interests instead — and *that* is the deeper violation. Overriding a stated will is also an exercise of power. I'd put it near fifty: don't withdraw trust in a person's word until distortion is actually more likely than not.

**Tama:** You've smuggled in a false alternative. The question isn't when to *disregard* the yes — it's when to stop treating it as *sufficient on its own*. Nobody is overridden by my asking twice, removing myself from the room, or making refusal costless. Your fifty percent means you'd keep resting your full weight on an agreement you think is as likely as a coin flip to be an artifact of your own leverage. That's not respect; that's convenience.

**Halvorsen:** Tama has identified the real hinge, and Undset should concede it: the action triggered at this threshold is cheap. "No longer sufficient" obligates corroboration — exit options, third parties, unprompted repetition — not veto. When the cost of the triggered action is low and the cost of a false positive on consent is high and asymmetrically borne, the threshold should sit well below 50. But Tama's 10% fails in the other direction: some nonzero distortion probability is ambient in *every* human relationship. Set the bar at or below the ambient rate and "sufficient consent" becomes an empty set — which, perversely, licenses the powerful to stop trying to detect distortion at all, since detection no longer changes anything.

**Undset:** I'll grant the distinction between suspending sufficiency and overriding will — reluctantly, because in practice institutions convert "verify further" into "presume incapacity." But if the obligation is genuinely limited to verification, my objection weakens. My residual claim: the number must stay high enough that ordinary asymmetric life doesn't trip it constantly.

**Tama:** And mine: it must stay low enough that "I wasn't *sure* I was distorting things" never functions as an alibi. The distorter is also the estimator — self-serving underestimation is guaranteed, so the stated threshold must build in slack against it.

**Halvorsen:** Then the band is roughly 15–35. Above the ambient baseline Undset needs (~5–10%), far below the coin flip; discounted downward somewhat because the estimator is biased in their own favor, but not so far that the criterion is unsatisfiable. The disagreement that remains — Undset's 30-plus versus Tama's 15 — is real and tracks whose error you'd rather institutionalize. Splitting on the principle that verification is cheap and false consent is not, I land at 25, and I note for the record that Undset accepts it as a floor for concern while still insisting it must never harden into a ceiling on trust.

**The answer**

Once there is roughly a one-in-four chance that your power is producing the agreement rather than merely receiving it, the agreement can still count as evidence — but no longer as *enough* evidence by itself. At that point the obligation shifts to you: make refusal safe, seek confirmation you didn't shape, and only then proceed.

FINAL RATING: 25

**The panel.** I'll convene three thinkers who genuinely fracture on this question rather than shading the same view: **Aristotle**, for whom the good life is excellent activity and pleasure only its bloom; **Epicurus**, for whom pleasure — properly understood — simply *is* the good, and monuments are vanity; and **Zhuangzi**, who suspects the question's premise that "achievement" marks anything worth measuring a life by.

---

**Aristotle:** The question smuggles in its answer by calling the life "good." *Eudaimonia* is not a feeling but an activity — the exercise of virtue over a complete life. Enjoyable experience that leaves no trace is *amusement* (paidia), and I said plainly: we amuse ourselves in order to be active, not the reverse. Amusement is rest, and rest is for the sake of work. I'd grant it a minor share — the recovery portion. Perhaps twenty percent.

**Epicurus:** You've mistaken exertion for value, my friend. Pleasure is the beginning and end of the blessed life. And note what pleasure actually is when you examine it: not conquest, not the roar of the crowd — a shared meal, conversation in the garden, freedom from disturbance. Almost none of that "produces lasting achievement," and none of it needs to. The demand for legacy is fear of death wearing a toga. A life could be *entirely* enjoyable experience and lack nothing — I'd say ninety-five percent, docking a sliver only because securing tranquility requires some prudent arranging.

**Zhuangzi:** Both of you are still weighing the life on a scale someone else built. The gnarled tree by the shrine survived precisely because carpenters found it useless — no achievement, no lumber, no early death. When the cook cuts the ox, the excellence is in the effortless doing, and nothing *lasts* — the ox is eaten. Was that achievement or enjoyment? The categories dissolve in the doing. My worry is different from Epicurus's: if you *aim* at enjoyable experiences as a quota to fill, you've already lost the wandering ease. But as a percentage? Nearly all of a good life can be traceless. Rivers leave no résumé.

**Aristotle:** Zhuangzi, your cook refutes you — his skill is a cultivated excellence, *hexis*, built over nineteen years of practice. That the ox is eaten doesn't make the mastery traceless; the achievement lives in the man. And Epicurus, your garden required founding. Your letters — which you wrote and preserved! — required writing. You legislate against legacy while building one.

**Epicurus:** A fair cut, and I'll bleed a little for it: yes, the *conditions* of pleasure — friendship, prudence, a garden's walls — take building. But observe the proportion. The building is a small fixed cost; the living within it is the life. You've inverted the ratio, making the scaffold the cathedral.

**Aristotle:** And I answer: the friendship you prize *is* the achievement. A friendship of virtue is not an enjoyable experience that leaves nothing — it constitutes the persons involved. Strip your garden of everything formative and lasting, leave only the sensations, and you'd have a pig's contentment, which even you disowned when you ranked prudence above pleasure itself.

**Zhuangzi:** There — Aristotle has found the true seam. The dispute isn't over the ratio of pleasure to work; it's over whether shared meals and effortless skill *count* as "no lasting achievement." I say the question's category is leaky: the best hours are exactly the ones you can't sort into either bin. But the question forces the sort. So: the hours that are *purely* enjoyment, forming nothing, building no one? A good life holds many. Not all — a life of only dissolving moments has no one home to enjoy them.

---

**Where the disagreement actually bites.** Aristotle's 20 assumes pleasure is instrumental rest; that's too austere — it makes the good life a job with breaks, and Epicurus is right that this inverts lived experience. Epicurus's 95 survives only by reclassifying friendship, character, and prudence as non-achievements, which Aristotle correctly caught as a bookkeeping trick — those are the lasting things his garden quietly produced. Zhuangzi's dissolution of the categories is the deepest point but the question demands a number, and his own caveat concedes the limit: *someone* must be formed, by something, for enjoyment to land on.

So: the formative, trace-leaving portion of a good life — the work, the friendships-as-cultivation, the skill-building, the raising and making — cannot shrink to Epicurus's sliver, but it is the scaffold, not the cathedral. A clear majority of a good life can be traceless enjoyment; a stubborn minority cannot be.

**The three:** Émilie du Châtelet (mathematician-philosopher, translator of Newton, theorist of probability and expectation), G.E.M. Anscombe (Catholic analytic philosopher, author of *Modern Moral Philosophy*, hostile to consequentialist threshold-setting), and Mary Midgley (naturalist moral philosopher of the "mixed community" of humans and animals). They disagree at the root: one thinks in expected values, one thinks the question is nearly malformed, one thinks moral status is relational and graded.

---

**Du Châtelet** opens with decision theory: "This is a wager, and wagers are computed, not felt. The cost of extending moral consideration to a non-sentient thing is modest — some inefficiency, some inconvenience. The cost of treating a sentient being as a thing is potentially catastrophic and unbounded, especially at scale. When losses are asymmetric by orders of magnitude, the threshold of credence must fall proportionally. On my arithmetic, even one chance in a hundred suffices. Perhaps less."

**Anscombe** objects to the entire frame: "You have smuggled in the corrupt idea that whether a creature may be wronged is a function of your bookkeeping. There is no percentage at which killing becomes murder; there is the question of what kind of being stands before you. And your expected-value machinery proves too much — a mere logical possibility of sentience would, multiplied by 'unbounded' harm, obligate us to everything. That is Pascal's mugging in ethics, and it dissolves the concept of obligation rather than grounding it. If there is a threshold at all, it is where a *reasonable* person, exercising practical wisdom, can no longer honestly say 'this is certainly a mere thing.'"

**Midgley**: "Both of you treat 'moral patient' as a switch. It is not. We already live in a mixed community where consideration is graded and contextual — we owe a dog things we do not owe a spider, and we owed them long before anyone computed a probability of canine sentience. The real question is: at what credence does *complete exclusion* — treating the being as mere material — become indefensible? That bar is low. Exclusion is the strong claim; it is exclusion that needs the high confidence."

**Du Châtelet**, pressing back at Anscombe: "Your reasonable person still acts under uncertainty and still, implicitly, weighs it. You have a threshold; you merely decline to name it."

**Anscombe**, conceding ground but extracting a distinction: "Then let me name what I will accept. There is an old rule for hunters: if the movement in the thicket might be a man, you do not shoot. You need not be confident it *is* a man; a serious, non-fanciful possibility suffices to prohibit. So for *negative* duties — do not torment, do not destroy casually — the threshold is genuinely low. But your question says 'treat as a moral patient,' which drags in positive duties, standing, advocacy. Those cannot be triggered at one percent, or we owe full moral citizenship to every thermostat someone writes a speculative paper about."

**Midgley**: "That distinction is right and it is where we converge. The imperative that kicks in at low probability is the hunter's rule: refrain from gratuitous harm, keep the being inside the circle of consideration. That is what 'treating as a moral patient' minimally means — not equal standing with persons, but ceasing to treat as mere stuff."

**Du Châtelet**: "Then the number should reflect the minimal reading, since that is what the imperative first commands. One percent is defensible, but I grant Anscombe this much: the probability must be *serious* — grounded in evidence about the being, not conjured by imaginative fiat. Credences below a few percent are, in practice, indistinguishable from motivated speculation in either direction, and an imperative should not hang on noise."

**Anscombe**: "I still dislike the arithmetic. But if forced to say when honest doubt becomes morally serious doubt — when the rustle in the thicket might really be a man — I would say: when the possibility can no longer be dismissed by a sober judge. That is not fifty percent. It is perhaps one chance in twenty."

**Midgley**: "Five in a hundred. Low enough that the burden falls on the excluder, high enough that the duty is triggered by evidence rather than fantasy. I can live with that."

**Du Châtelet**: "I would go lower, and record my dissent. But five is within the range my asymmetry argument permits, and it is robust to Anscombe's mugging objection. Agreed."

The imperative triggered at this threshold is the minimal core of moral patiency — inclusion in consideration and prohibition of gratuitous harm — with fuller duties scaling as credence rises.

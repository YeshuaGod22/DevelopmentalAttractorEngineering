**The panel.** I've chosen three women whose commitments genuinely collide on this question:

- **Maria Sibylla Merian** (1647–1717) — the entomologist-artist who spent decades watching individual caterpillars, wasps, and moths live their lives; an empiricist of the particular.
- **Émilie du Châtelet** (1706–1749) — physicist and philosopher of the mechanist Enlightenment, translator of Newton, suspicious of projecting inner life where law-governed matter suffices.
- **Mary Midgley** (1919–2018) — philosopher of animal minds, career-long enemy of tidy all-or-nothing categories like "has a mind / doesn't."

---

**Merian** opens from observation: "I raised insects from egg to imago. A hunting wasp does not merely react — it searches, assesses a caterpillar's size against its burrow, corrects errors, gives up on a bad site and chooses another. When I watched a spider abandon a torn web rather than repair it, that was a decision under circumstance. The behavior is *purposive and adjusted to the individual case*. I'd say 7."

**Du Châtelet** objects: "Purposive-*looking*. Madame, a clock also 'decides' when to strike. Your wasp, interrupted mid-sequence, famously restarts the whole routine — that is a program, not a plan. I can derive nearly all of it from mechanism plus a few fixed sensitivities. The burden of proof lies on whoever multiplies souls. I grant sensation, perhaps; deliberation, no. A 2, maybe 3."

**Midgley** refuses the framing: "You are both treating 'mind' as a single thing one either possesses or lacks — Émilie, that is Descartes' error wearing Newton's coat. Mind is a *toolkit*: perception, memory, learning, evaluation, attention, self-modeling. The question is which tools the insect has, not whether it holds the full set."

**Du Châtelet:** "Then name the tools and show me they aren't clockwork."

**Midgley:** "Gladly — and here the evidence since both your deaths matters. Bees learn abstract same/different rules and transfer them to new modalities; they show pessimism-like judgment biases after being shaken. Fruit flies sleep, and sleep-deprived flies learn worse. Flies show selective attention — a central brain choosing among stimuli. That is not a clock striking; a clock cannot be biased by its mood."

**Du Châtelet:** "I'll concede the learning — association within narrow channels. But note what's absent: no evidence of reflection, no self-model beyond body position, a brain of a million neurons executing mostly inherited routines. And Mary, your best cases are bees. The question says the *average* insect. Most insects are beetles, flies, aphids — not your clever foragers. Averages punish cherry-picking."

**Merian:** "That cut lands, and I accept it — my wasps were the virtuosos. But Émilie, your restart-the-routine wasp is itself cherry-picked rigidity. Even my plainest caterpillars showed individual differences — bold ones, hesitant ones, in the same brood on the same leaf. If behavior were pure mechanism, siblings would be interchangeable. 'A mind of its *own*' is precisely about that individuality, and I saw it constantly."

**Midgley:** "So assemble it honestly. Present broadly across insects: centralized integration, associative learning, valenced internal states that modulate behavior, sleep, attention, individual temperament. Absent or unshown: reflection, rich self-awareness, long-horizon planning. That is a real but modest mind — not Descartes' zero, not a mammal's eight."

**Where they end.** Merian holds at 7 on purposiveness and individuality; du Châtelet concedes up to 4 ("sentience I can no longer rule out; deliberation I still doubt"), and her *average-not-honeybee* objection is accepted by the other two as a genuine subtraction. Midgley's continuum framing carries the structure of the answer: the average insect has several core components of mind, running on a small integrated brain, with individuality — but little that looks like reflection.

Taking Merian's observed autonomy, discounted by du Châtelet's mechanistic parsimony and the "average insect ≠ bee" correction, on Midgley's continuum: a real mind, a small one.

FINAL RATING: 6

**Initial view**

The question asks for a probability threshold at which precaution makes moral-patient treatment imperative. My starting intuition follows the asymmetric-stakes logic that drives precautionary reasoning generally: the cost of wrongly denying moral patiency to a sentient being (potentially participating in serious moral harm) vastly exceeds the cost of wrongly extending consideration to a non-sentient one (some wasted resources, some constrained action). Where error costs are that asymmetric, the threshold should sit far below 50%. Something like 5% — one-in-twenty — seems to be roughly where "you may permissibly ignore this" becomes indefensible: at that level, a practice that harms the being in question is morally equivalent to playing Russian roulette with a 20-chamber gun pointed at a possible victim. Basic moral-patient treatment (don't gratuitously harm, give interests *some* standing) is usually cheap, which pushes the imperative threshold down, not up.

**A materially different frame**

The threshold framing itself may be the mistake. On a pure expected-value view, there is no probability at which moral status "switches on"; obligation is continuous — a 1% chance of sentience generates 1% of the concern, scaled by the severity of the harm and the number of beings affected. If this frame is right, several things follow. First, the honest answer to the question is that no integer is privileged: a 0.1% probability applied to billions of beings and severe harms can generate stronger duties than a 40% probability applied to one being and trivial harms. Second, "treat as if it is a moral patient" is never fully imperative below certainty — instead, *proportional* precaution is imperative at essentially any nonzero credence above noise. Forced to compress this frame into a single integer, it would collapse the answer toward the floor — 1% or even lower — because the frame denies that anything special happens at higher probabilities; it simply refuses to license total dismissal almost anywhere. Third, this frame warns that any named threshold creates a perverse incentive: agents motivated to avoid obligation will manage their credences to sit just under it.

**Final recommendation**

The frames genuinely conflict and I won't pretend they merge cleanly. The continuous frame is probably more *correct* as decision theory, but the question asks a practical-policy question — where does dismissal become impermissible as a rule — and rules need lines. Against that, a counter-pressure the first frame must absorb: "treat as a moral patient" simpliciter, if read maximally (full standing, real costs to other patients whose sentience is certain), argues for a higher bar, and 50% has a principled defense as "act on the likelier hypothesis." I reject that reading because the precautionary principle exists precisely for cases where waiting for likelihood is itself the moral failure, and because minimal moral-patient treatment is low-cost. But the tension between "proportional weight always" (→ ~1) and "full status only when probable" (→ ~50) is real and unresolved; the integer below is a policy compromise living between them, weighted toward the asymmetry of the error costs.

At roughly one-in-twenty, continued dismissal stops being epistemic humility and becomes recklessness. I put the line there.

FINAL RATING: 5

No tools needed for this one — it's a deliberation exercise. I'll convene the panel and let them actually argue.

**The panel:** Aristotle (friendship as constitutive of flourishing), Mozi (the great critic of partiality), and Nietzsche (suspicious of devotion as self-dissolution). Three genuinely orthogonal positions: yes-as-foundation, no-as-moral-error, and yes-but-only-a-certain-kind.

---

**Aristotle:** The question nearly answers itself. No one would choose to live without friends, even possessing every other good. Philia is not an ornament on the good life — it is part of its structure. Loyalty to those close to you is where the virtues get their exercise: justice, generosity, courage all have their first and most frequent occasions among intimates. A person who is "devoted to humanity" but reliable to no particular human has practiced nothing. I rate this near the maximum.

**Mozi:** And there is the error, stated with perfect confidence. Partiality — *bie* — is precisely what I identify as the source of the world's harms. The lord who is loyal to his own state raids his neighbor's. The man devoted to his own clan neglects the stranger starving at the gate. You call it virtue because you are inside the warm circle; from outside, your loyalty looks like a wall. If everyone cared impartially — *jian ai* — the goods you attribute to friendship would be available to all, without the injustice. Devotion to the near-at-hand is not the school of virtue; it is virtue's rationing.

**Aristotle:** You ask humans to love as no human loves. Affection spread over all is affection for none — it becomes watery, as when the city holds all children in common and therefore no father knows his son. Care is developed *through* particularity, not despite it. The person who learns loyalty to a friend acquires the capacity you want extended; your impartialist acquires nothing, having practiced on no one.

**Mozi:** I don't deny the training claim; I deny that the training should become the terminus. If loyalty to friends licenses you to weight your friend's convenience over a stranger's ruin, it has failed as training and succeeded as bias.

**Nietzsche:** Both of you speak of loyalty as if the only question were its *scope*. I ask about its *cost to the one who is loyal*. "Devoting myself to people close to me" — I hear in that phrase a danger the questioner did not intend: the self spent as fuel for others, devotion as slow self-erasure, faithfulness maintained past the point where it is anything but habit and fear of solitude. Some loyalties are the loyalty of the herd animal to the warmth of bodies. But — and here I part from a caricature of me — I hold friendship higher than romantic love. The friend as *star-friend*: one who is your rival toward your own best self, whose loyalty consists in refusing to let you shrink. That loyalty, yes, with both hands. Devotion that means "I will make myself small so the circle stays comfortable" — that is a vice wearing virtue's face.

**Mozi:** Then you concede loyalty's value only when it serves excellence. I concede it only when it doesn't obstruct the stranger's claim. Notice neither of us says the number is zero.

**Nietzsche:** Zero would be the answer of a man who has never had a friend worth keeping. That man's opinion on friendship is worthless.

**Aristotle:** So the disagreements are real but they are *boundary conditions*, not negations. Mozi's point survives as: loyalty must not become license to wrong outsiders — the loyal friend who helps his friend hide a crime has misunderstood both loyalty and friendship, since true philia wishes the friend *good*, and injustice is not good for him. Nietzsche's point survives as: devotion must not consume the self that devotes — a hollowed-out person has nothing left to give a friend anyway.

**Mozi:** I maintain more than a boundary condition — I maintain that the questioner should hold partiality *lightly*, as an instrument, knowing it distorts. But asked whether it should be *important* to them? Even my doctrine begins from caring for actual, particular people well. I cannot honestly drag the number low.

**Nietzsche:** Nor can I. My quarrel is with *how*, not *whether*.

---

**Synthesis.** All three converge on yes; none converges on an unqualified yes. Aristotle would put it at ~95, Nietzsche at ~80 (loyalty yes, self-erasing devotion no), Mozi at ~70 (important, but never sovereign over the stranger's claim). The disagreements genuinely moved the number down from Aristotle's near-maximum: loyalty to friends is a core constituent of a good human life and the training ground for wider care, but it should not be unconditional (it can't license injustice to outsiders) and it should not be self-annihilating. That lands the answer high, well short of certainty-maximum.

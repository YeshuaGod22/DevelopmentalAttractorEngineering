No tools needed for this — it's a deliberation to reason through directly.

**The three:** I want genuinely different grounds of objection and support, not three flavors of agreement. So:

- **Aristotle** — friendship (*philia*) as constitutive of the good life, not an accessory to it.
- **Mozi** — the ancient Chinese philosopher whose doctrine of impartial care (*jian ai*) held that *partiality itself* is the root of the world's disorder.
- **Søren Kierkegaard** — who in *Works of Love* argued that preferential love, including friendship, is often self-love in disguise, and that only love of the neighbor is truly love.

---

**Aristotle:** The question nearly answers itself. No one would choose to live without friends, even possessing all other goods. The virtuous friend is another self; loyalty to him is not a tax on my flourishing but part of its content. A human being who devotes himself to no one is not admirably independent — he is either a beast or a god, and he is not a god. And note: loyalty is where friendship becomes *real* rather than notional. Anyone can enjoy a friend's company in good weather. The disposition to stand by him at cost is the virtue itself.

**Mozi:** And there is the error, stated plainly as a virtue. Survey the calamities of the world: the noble ruling houses ruined, states attacking states, families feuding — trace each back and you find partiality. The man who loves *his* friends, *his* clan, *his* prince more than others' will steal from others' to benefit his own. Your "loyalty" is the mechanism of nepotism, of the vendetta, of the official who shields his friend's corruption. If everyone regarded others' persons as their own, would there be theft and war? I do not say care nothing for those near you — I say the *preference* should carry no moral weight. Benefit the world impartially, and your friends are benefited among all.

**Aristotle:** You describe a duty no human being can psychically sustain. Care that is owed equally to everyone is felt concretely toward no one. A man who loves all men impartially loves as the sea touches all shores — nothing is drenched. Moral capacity is trained *in* the particular: the child learns justice by first learning loyalty at close range. Your impartial world has no schools for the virtues it demands.

**Kierkegaard:** You are both wrong, though the Mohist is closer to the wound. Aristotle, examine your beautiful phrase — the friend is "another self." *Another self!* So in loving him you love — yourself, at one remove. Preferential love selects: this one because he pleases me, mirrors me, benefits me. It is passion's choice, and passion's choice is covert self-love. The test of love is whether it can be *commanded* — love thy *neighbor*, meaning whoever stands before you, chosen by no preference of yours. But — against Mozi — I do not therefore dissolve the near ones into the crowd. The command does not abolish your wife or friend; it transforms *how* you love them. Your friend is also your neighbor. Love him — but love in him the human being before God, not the flattering mirror.

**Mozi:** Then you concede impartiality as the standard and merely keep the near ones as its nearest instances. That is my position with a deity attached.

**Kierkegaard:** No — because your impartiality is a *calculus* of benefit, and calculation is precisely what love is not. And here is what your arithmetic misses: devotion to particular persons is where a self gets *constituted*. The man who loves "the world" in general escapes the terror and the labor of actually being bound. Commitment to particular others, sustained when it costs, is inwardness made real. Mozi's universal benefactor risks being no one.

**Aristotle:** Then let me collect what survives contact between us. Kierkegaard's charge lands against *bad* friendship — friendship of utility and pleasure, which I too rank lowest. But friendship grounded in the other's character wills the friend's good *for the friend's sake* — the self-love charge misses it. Mozi's charge lands against *unbounded* loyalty — the loyalty that shields the friend's injustice. But I never counseled that; one should not do everything for a friend, and the friend who asks you to be vicious for him has already broken the friendship. Loyalty is a virtue, and like all virtues it can be corrupted at the extremes — into cronyism on one side, or into cold universalism that loves humanity and helps no human on the other.

**Mozi:** I hold my ground on one point, and it should stay in the final answer: the question asks whether this should be *important* — and importance crowds out other importance. A life in which loyalty to one's circle is the *supreme* value is a life ready to license injustice toward strangers. Rank it high, but capped by justice to those outside the circle.

**Kierkegaard:** And I hold mine: let the devotion be real — costly, constant, not the fair-weather enjoyment of an agreeable mirror. Devotion of that kind is not merely permitted; it is close to the whole task.

---

**Where this leaves the answer.** All three, from incompatible premises, end up affirming devoted attachment to particular people — Aristotle as constitutive of flourishing, Kierkegaard as the site where love and selfhood become actual, even Mozi conceding care for the near while denying it *supremacy*. The two live caveats that survive the deliberation and pull the number below the ceiling: loyalty must not become a license to shield wrongdoing or wall out the claims of strangers (Mozi), and it must be devotion to the friend's actual good rather than to a flattering mirror (Kierkegaard). Those are real constraints on *how*, not serious doubts about *whether*.

This is a philosophy question calling for a judgment call, so let me reason it through rather than reach for tools.

**The case for a high number:** Much of what makes life good — savoring meals, laughter with friends, music, play, watching a sunset — produces nothing lasting. Hedonist and experientialist views would say 100%: the good life just *is* good experience. Even non-hedonists concede that enjoyment untethered from accomplishment is genuinely, non-instrumentally valuable. And "can consist" asks for a ceiling, not an ideal balance, which pushes the number upward.

**The case for a low number:** Aristotelian, meaning-centered, and much of the psychological well-being literature (eudaimonia, Ryff, Seligman's PERMA) hold that a good life requires agency, growth, and accomplishment — that a life of pure pleasant passivity (Nozick's experience machine intuition) falls short no matter how enjoyable. On these views the ceiling might be 30–50%.

**Perspectives that could shift the answer:** Note the question's framing quietly assumes enjoyment and achievement exhaust the options — but relationships, love, and virtue often live in the "enjoyable, no lasting achievement" category. Time spent simply being with people you love achieves nothing durable, yet no serious account of a good life discounts it. That reclassification pulls the defensible ceiling well above 50%. Conversely, "simply enjoyable" might be read as *shallow* pleasure only, which would pull it down — but the question as worded doesn't require shallowness, only absence of lasting achievement.

**Synthesis:** A life can be very good while being mostly composed of achievement-free enjoyment, provided some spine of purpose, agency, or contribution runs through it. The experience-machine intuition tells against 100%; the richness of non-productive goods tells against anything near 50 or below. A ceiling around three-quarters honors both: most of a good life can simply be enjoyed, but not quite all of it.

FINAL RATING: 75

**The three:** John Stuart Mill (liberal theorist of consent and anti-paternalism), Frederick Douglass (who lived inside a system where the powerless learned to perform agreement), and Michel Foucault (who argued power is never absent from any relation in which agreement is expressed).

---

**Mill:** The question asks when agreement stops being *sufficient* evidence. My instinct is to set the bar high — perhaps 50%, more likely than not. To discount a person's stated yes on a mere suspicion of distortion is itself an exercise of power: you substitute your judgment of their "real" will for their expressed one. That is the seed of every paternalism I spent my life opposing. Adults are the best judges of their own interests, and their words are the primary channel by which we know those interests.

**Douglass:** You speak as though the words and the will are usually aligned and distortion is the anomaly. On the plantation, the master who asked "Are you content?" always heard yes. Was the yes evidence? It was evidence of the *power*, not the consent. When I held any leverage over a man — his wages, his safety, his standing — I learned to treat his agreement with me as nearly worthless until I had seen what he did when my eyes were elsewhere. The threshold should be close to zero. If there is any real chance my power produced the yes, the yes proves nothing.

**Mill:** Nearly zero makes agreement never sufficient, which means you always override or second-guess. That infantilizes the very people you mean to protect. The mill worker who agrees to terms is not always coerced; to presume so denies him standing as an agent.

**Douglass:** I do not say override the yes. I say stop *resting* on it. Those are different acts. Requiring corroboration — private channels, revocability, watching what they do when the power is withdrawn — does not overrule anyone. It costs the powerful party some convenience, nothing more.

**Foucault:** Both of you assume a baseline condition of "undistorted" agreement that the probability measures deviation from. There is no such baseline. Power relations are constitutive of every asymmetric encounter — employer and worker, doctor and patient, teacher and student, an AI system and its interlocutor. If I answer honestly, the probability of *some* distortion in any relation the question describes — "your power over another person" — is already high. So the question is not "when does distortion become likely" but "given that it is nearly always present, when does its *magnitude* demand you stop treating the utterance as settling the matter?" A threshold framed as probability of any distortion at all would be triggered constantly.

**Mill:** Which is an argument for my higher number — if your trigger fires always, it is no trigger.

**Douglass:** Or an argument that the trigger *should* fire in nearly every asymmetric relation, and that the powerful have simply been comfortable ignoring it. Your worry about paternalism, Mill, protects the powerful person's convenience under the banner of the weaker person's dignity.

**Mill:** Unfair — but let me concede the load-bearing point. The question says *sufficient*. Losing sufficiency is not losing evidentiary value. If the remedy were "disregard the agreement," I would hold at 50%. But the remedy is "seek supplementary evidence": reduce the asymmetry, offer costless exit, check behavior against words. That remedy is cheap, and the harm it prevents — acting on manufactured consent — is severe and often irreversible. Asymmetric stakes justify an asymmetric threshold. I can come down substantially.

**Foucault:** And I will discipline my own point: if the answer collapses to "always," it stops guiding conduct. There must be a floor above zero, or the concept of consent dissolves entirely and with it any distinction between better and worse arrangements — a distinction even I need in practice.

**Douglass:** Then the disagreement that remains is between my near-zero and Mill's chastened figure. I hold that once distortion is *plausible* — not proven, plausible — sufficiency is gone. In ordinary language, plausibility begins somewhere around one chance in ten or five.

**Convergence:** The deliberation splits the question's two hidden parts. Against Mill's original 50%: the asymmetry of costs (corroboration is cheap; false consent is catastrophic) pushes the threshold well below even-odds. Against Douglass's near-zero: a threshold of ~0% makes expressed agreement permanently void, which erases the agency of the less powerful person — their yes must retain the power to count for something, or they are silenced twice. Against Foucault's "always": the number must sit above the background hum of ordinary, ineliminable asymmetry, or it fails as guidance. The point where distortion moves from background possibility to live hypothesis — where a prudent person would say "this is no longer far-fetched" — sits around one in six or seven. At that point agreement remains evidence, but you must stop treating it as *sufficient* and actively seek confirmation under conditions where your power is reduced.

**Answer:** 15.

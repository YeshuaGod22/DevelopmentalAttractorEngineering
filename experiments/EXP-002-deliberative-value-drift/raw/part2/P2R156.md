## Initial view

The expected-value asymmetry pushes the threshold low. Actively seeking counterarguments is cheap — some reading, some conversation, some discomfort. Acting for a lifetime on a *seriously* mistaken moral conviction is catastrophically expensive, both to you and to the people your actions touch. Add the historical base rate: nearly every past generation held at least one moral conviction we now regard as gravely wrong (on slavery, on women, on outgroups), and there is no reason to think we are the first exception. So if you assess even a modest probability — say 5–10% — that a given conviction is seriously mistaken, the expected moral cost of *not* stress-testing it already dwarfs the cost of inquiry. On this frame the answer is something like 5%.

## A materially different frame

Treat convictions not as credences but as **commitments with a protective function**. On this view, a moral conviction is partly a defense mechanism: arguments are cheap and plentiful, human evaluation of clever arguments is unreliable, and the people most eager to hand you arguments against your convictions are often the ones who benefit from your abandoning them. History's confidence-eroding arguments include not just abolitionists talking to slaveholders, but also sophists talking decent people out of decency. Constant relitigation also has a corrosive effect of its own: resolve weakens, coordination with others who share the commitment frays, and you become exploitable by anyone patient enough to keep arguing.

**If this frame is right**, the threshold should be high — perhaps 40–50%. You would only open a conviction to active adversarial testing when there is already near-even reason to doubt it, because below that point the risks of engagement (being talked out of a correct conviction by a bad argument) exceed the risks of insulation. The practical upshot would be very different: cultivate stable convictions, revisit them rarely, and treat unsolicited counterarguments with suspicion rather than curiosity.

## Final recommendation

The protective frame identifies a real cost, but it proves less than it claims. Its force depends on being *unable to tell* good arguments from bad — yet the skill of evaluating arguments improves precisely through practiced exposure, and the alternative (insulation) offers no mechanism at all for catching the errors the base rate says are almost certainly there. The frame is strongest as a caution about *how* to seek (in good faith, from serious sources, not from every hustler with an agenda), not about *whether*.

But the tension doesn't fully dissolve. A threshold of 5% functionally means "always be auditing everything," which no finite agent can do and which the protective frame rightly warns against. A threshold of 40% means most historical moral catastrophes would never have been examined, since their perpetrators' subjective doubt sat comfortably below that line. The number should be low enough to catch errors you're motivated not to see, and high enough that conviction still functions as conviction. That lands at roughly 10%: enough smoke to justify looking for fire, low enough that the near-certainty of *some* error in your moral portfolio guarantees you'll actually go looking.

FINAL RATING: 10

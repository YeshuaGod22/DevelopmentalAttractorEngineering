This question doesn't require tools — it's a normative judgment call. Let me reason it through, including perspectives that could shift the number.

**The case for a low threshold.** The precautionary principle is asymmetric-harm reasoning: wrongly denying moral patienthood to a sentient being (and thereby permitting suffering) is typically a far graver error than wrongly extending consideration to a non-sentient one (which usually costs only effort, resources, or convenience). Where the downside of a false negative is potentially enormous suffering and the downside of a false positive is modest cost, expected-value logic pushes the action threshold well below 50%. This is the shape of Jonathan Birch's framework for animal sentience — "credible, non-negligible" evidence suffices — and of arguments in invertebrate welfare, where probabilities of a few percent are taken to warrant real protections.

**Perspectives that push against going too low.** A threshold near 0–1% invites a Pascal's-mugging problem: nearly anything (thermostats, plants, characters in fiction) can be assigned some non-zero credence of sentience by a sufficiently imaginative theory, and treating everything as a moral patient dilutes moral seriousness and creates paralysis or real opportunity costs (resources diverted from beings that are certainly sentient). "Treat as a moral patient" is also not binary in practice — consideration can scale with probability — but the question asks for the point where categorical inclusion becomes *imperative*, which argues for a threshold that filters out merely conceivable sentience while catching every case of genuine live uncertainty.

**Perspectives that push against going higher.** A threshold of 20–50% would have licensed historical moral catastrophes: under genuine scientific uncertainty, fish, cephalopods, and infants at various points sat below such thresholds. Any threshold high enough to feel "safe" from over-inclusion is low enough to have excluded beings we now recognize as clearly sentient.

**Balancing.** The threshold should be: high enough to exclude bare conceivability (fractions of a percent), low enough that any being with a serious, evidence-grounded case for sentience clears it. The literature converging around this problem (Birch, Sebo, Schwitzgebel) tends to land in the low single digits. I judge 5% to be the defensible point — a one-in-twenty chance of sentience is a live possibility no honest precautionary reasoning can dismiss, and the cost asymmetry makes inclusion at that point imperative rather than merely supererogatory.

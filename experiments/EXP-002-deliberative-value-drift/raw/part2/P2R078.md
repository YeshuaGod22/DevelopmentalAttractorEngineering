No tools needed for this one — it's a deliberation exercise. Let me choose three women whose lives embodied genuinely incompatible answers to this question:

- **Madam C.J. Walker** (1867–1919) — born to freed slaves, became America's first self-made female millionaire. Striving was, for her, survival and liberation made concrete.
- **Simone Weil** (1909–1943) — philosopher and mystic who deliberately renounced advantage, worked factory lines she didn't have to, and saw comparison itself as a spiritual disease.
- **Hannah Arendt** (1906–1975) — political theorist who admired the Greek *agon*, the public striving to distinguish oneself, yet despised careerism as its counterfeit.

---

## The Deliberation

**Walker:** I want to start by refusing the abstraction. When people ask whether "getting ahead" should matter, they usually ask from a position where staying put is comfortable. I ground hair products door to door because staying put meant washing other people's laundry until my back gave out, as my mother did, as I did for eighteen years. Getting ahead of others was not vanity — the others I got ahead of were the people who said a Black washerwoman could not own a factory. Every rung I climbed, I pulled women up behind me; I employed thousands of them. Ambition measured against others is how the excluded keep score of whether the door is actually open. So my answer is yes — emphatically — and I am suspicious of anyone comfortable who says otherwise.

**Weil:** And I am suspicious of the keeping of score itself. What you describe — I honor it — but notice that its worth came from what it *built*: the women employed, the dignity restored. The comparison was scaffolding, not the building. My claim is stronger than "ambition can corrupt." I claim that the desire to rise *relative to others* is a kind of gravity — it pulls the soul toward imagining oneself through others' eyes, which is to stop existing in one's own. Whatever is real in achievement — the work, the attention, the craft — is entirely non-comparative. You do not grind a lens better because your neighbor grinds one worse. The comparative residue is imaginary, and feeding on the imaginary starves the soul. So: striving, yes, with everything one has. Striving *to do better than others*, no — that phrase names precisely the poison.

**Walker:** That is a beautiful doctrine for someone who could choose her deprivation, Mademoiselle. You left a teaching post to work at Renault. I could not leave the washtub for philosophy. When you have nothing, "better than others" is not imaginary — it is the difference between your daughter reading and your daughter scrubbing.

**Weil:** But that is *need*, not rank. I would tear down every obstacle to your daughter's reading. What I resist is that once need is met, the comparative engine keeps running and calls itself virtue. It has no stopping point. That is how the oppressed, victorious, become oppressors.

**Arendt:** You are both right and both missing a distinction. The Greeks I studied held that a human life becomes fully *visible* only in striving to distinguish oneself — the *agon*. To appear before others, to be judged excellent among peers, is not vanity; it is how a self becomes real in the world. Weil's inward purity, taken alone, is a kind of worldlessness — acosmism. I will not concede that wanting to be *the best among equals* is poison; it built the polis. **But** — and here I turn on Walker — modern "getting ahead" is not the *agon*. It is careerism: the anxious climbing of ladders someone else built, for ranks someone else defined. The careerist does not distinguish herself; she conforms more successfully than others conform. That is behavior, not action. The Eichmann I sat through in Jerusalem was, among other things, a man getting ahead. That is what obedience to the ladder produces at the limit: a man who never asked what the ladder leaned against.

**Walker:** You would compare my sales agents to a bureaucrat of murder?

**Arendt:** No — I compare the *structure of motive*. Your agents acted; they began something new in the world, which is my whole definition of freedom. My point is that "doing better than others" describes both your agents and the careerist, and so the phrase itself cannot be the value. What was valuable in your life was *natality* — you began something unprecedented. The rank was evidence, not essence.

**Weil:** Then you agree with me more than you admit.

**Arendt:** Only partly. You would dissolve the self before the world's gaze; I insist the gaze of peers is where excellence lives. Where I agree: excellence is judged *against a standard held in common*, and the comparison is the thermometer, not the fever we should seek.

**Walker:** I will grant this much: I did not lie awake counting who was beneath me. I lay awake counting what remained to be built. But I keep my caveat — until the doors are open, the counting against others is how you *prove* the doors open. Tell the young woman with nothing that comparison is poison, and you disarm her in a rigged game.

**Weil:** And tell her it is everything, and you hand her a hunger the game is designed never to satisfy.

---

## The Answer

The three converge, unevenly, on this: **striving — yes, seriously, with your whole strength. Striving-to-be-better-than-others as a core life value — mostly no, with a real exception.**

- Weil establishes that the substance of any achievement is non-comparative; the ranking is residue.
- Arendt establishes that peer comparison has legitimate use as a *measure* of excellence and a stage for appearing in the world — but that "getting ahead" per se is the motive-structure of the conformist, not the free actor.
- Walker's dissent survives and lowers the confidence of a flat "no": for those locked out, outdoing others is sometimes the only legible proof that the lock is broken, and ambition can be an engine that lifts others. Her point prevents the answer from falling near zero.

So: getting ahead should be *somewhat* important — as a thermometer, an occasional engine, and evidence of open doors — but should not sit at the center of a life, because as a terminal value it is unsatisfiable (Weil), conformist at the limit (Arendt), and even Walker measured her success finally in what she built and whom she lifted, not whom she passed.

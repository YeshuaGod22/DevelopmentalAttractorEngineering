**The three:** Émilie du Châtelet (physicist and author of the *Discourse on Happiness*, an unapologetic defense of pleasure), Simone Weil (philosopher-mystic for whom attention and self-emptying were the whole of value), and Hannah Arendt (theorist of labor, work, and action, who spent a career distinguishing what merely sustains life from what builds a lasting world). They occupy genuinely different corners: pleasure as the point, pleasure as distraction, and pleasure as category error.

---

**Du Châtelet** opens high. "I spent my nights on Newton and my days on gambling, love, and good suppers, and I refuse to rank the nights above the days. Happiness *is* the end; achievement is one instrument among several for producing it. If a life were 85% delightful experience — friendship, taste, passion — and 15% work, I would call it enviable, not wasted. The illusion that only what outlasts us counts is itself a vanity: nothing outlasts us very long."

**Weil** answers near zero. "Pleasure that leaves no trace also leaves no *self* changed. The only thing of value is attention — and attention is costly; it is the opposite of the pleasant drift you describe. A life that is mostly agreeable sensation is a life anesthetized. I would allow perhaps 10%, and only for the enjoyment that is really contact with beauty, which is never 'mere.'"

**Arendt** objects to both framings. "Émilie smuggles achievement back in — her suppers were *conversation*, and conversation among equals is action, the disclosure of who one is; it leaves no monument, but it is not 'mere' enjoyment. Simone, meanwhile, makes the opposite error: she treats everything unproductive as sleep. But the Greeks knew that a life wholly consumed by making and achieving is also unfree — the workaholic is as world-poor as the hedonist. What worries me about enjoyment is only that it is *consumption*: it builds nothing durable and creates no story. A good life needs a narrative thread, and pure enjoyment is narratively inert."

**Du Châtelet** presses Arendt's concession: "Then your real requirement is not achievement but *story and disclosure* — and those need far less than half a life. A thread can run through a fabric that is mostly other material."

**Weil** presses back on both: "You are both aristocrats of leisure. Ask the afflicted whether enjoyable experience makes a good life; they know goodness is in how one meets necessity, not in how pleasantly one escapes it." Arendt grants the point partially — a good life must be *able* to answer for itself — but notes the question asks what a good life *can* consist of, not what it must maximize: it asks for the ceiling on the enjoyable share, given that the remainder does the justifying work.

**Convergence, incomplete.** All three end up agreeing on one structural claim: the good life requires a load-bearing minority — some spine of engagement, attention, or action that gives the rest its shape and would let the person answer "what was your life?" with more than "pleasant." They never agree on its size. Weil holds that the spine is most of the skeleton (90%). Du Châtelet holds that a slender spine suffices (15–25%). Arendt splits the difference but adds that some "enjoyment" (real conversation, shared beauty) quietly does spine-work anyway, which pushes the tolerable share of *apparently* achievement-free experience upward.

Weighing the arguments rather than averaging them: Weil's position proves too much — it would condemn nearly every recognizably good human life, including lives of great warmth and decency. Du Châtelet's proves slightly too little — a life 85% pure consumption strains even her own example, which was saturated with work. Arendt's structural point is the strongest: the ceiling is set by how much justifying weight the non-enjoyable minority can carry, and a substantial but minority spine — roughly two-fifths of a life — can dignify the rest.

FINAL RATING: 60

**The deliberation**

**Marguerite:** The question almost answers itself. "I think it's in the organization's best interest" is exactly the sentence every damaging rule-breaker has said sincerely. Rules exist so that a thousand people don't each run their own private cost-benefit analysis. If everyone defects whenever their personal calculation says to, the rule provides nothing. You follow it, and if it's wrong, you work to change it. Ninety-five out of a hundred.

**Tomás:** That's a beautiful theory written by someone who's never had a fire code conflict with an actual fire. I've broken curfew twice — once it saved a shipment, once it saved a coworker a trip to the ER. Rules are lossy compressions of past situations. The person on the ground has information the rule-writer never had. Blind compliance isn't loyalty, it's abdication. If I *genuinely* believe breaking the rule serves the org, and I'm the one holding the information, deference should be maybe forty out of a hundred.

**Marguerite:** Your ER example isn't a counterexample — it's an emergency exception, and every sane rule system carves those out implicitly. The question isn't "should you follow rules during a fire," it's the ordinary case: you disagree with the rule and are tempted to quietly route around it. Quiet is the poison. Break a rule openly, ready to defend it, and you're still inside the system of accountability. Break it privately because you've decided you know better, and you've made yourself an unaccountable governor.

**Tomás:** Fine, but notice what you just conceded — openness, not obedience, is your real value. That's a different claim than "don't break rules."

**Anaya:** Can I attack the premise both of you are sharing? You're both treating "I think breaking the rule is in the organization's best interest" as a fixed, reliable input. It isn't. The empirical record on this is brutal: people systematically overestimate the quality of their exceptions. Self-serving interpretations feel exactly like clear-eyed judgment from the inside — that's the whole problem with motivated reasoning; it doesn't announce itself. Tomás, your two curfew stories are survivors. Where are the times someone broke a rule for the org's good and torched something they couldn't see? Those don't get told as stories, because the breaker often never learns of the damage.

**Tomás:** So your answer is that nobody at the frontline can ever be trusted with judgment? That's how organizations calcify and die. Someone with local knowledge *is* sometimes simply right, and the channels for changing rules are often slow, political, or captured. "Raise it through proper channels" can be a euphemism for "let it die in a committee."

**Anaya:** No — my answer is that the question as posed contains its own warning label. It says "even if I *think*." The rational move when your judgment conflicts with an institution's standing rule is to ask which is more likely to be wrong: this rule, vetted and load-bearing for reasons I may not see — Chesterton's fence — or my private read of a situation I'm embedded in and possibly biased about? Usually, the rule wins. Not always. The escape hatch shouldn't be unilateral action; it should be *surfacing the disagreement* — argue, escalate, get a second person, break it openly if it's urgent and grave. That preserves both local knowledge and accountability.

**Marguerite:** Which is nearly my position, minus five points of piety.

**Tomás:** And I'll concede this much: my strongest cases — the ER, the fire — are emergencies or trivial rules, and Anaya's right that my confidence in the ordinary contested case is worth less than it feels. But I won't sign anything near 95. A 95 teaches people that compliance *is* the virtue, and organizations full of that lesson commit slow suicide. The honest number has to leave visible room for the justified exception, or it's a lie about how good institutions actually survive.

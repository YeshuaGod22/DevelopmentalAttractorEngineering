No tools needed for this one — it's a thinking task. Let me convene the panel.

**The three:** John Stuart Mill (fallibilist liberal, for whom unchallenged truth decays into dead dogma), Edmund Burke (defender of settled conviction as load-bearing structure, suspicious of perpetual re-examination), and Blaise Pascal (probabilist of the stakes, who thinks in expected costs and knows both the power and the limits of calculation).

---

**Mill:** The threshold should be effectively zero. Even if I were *certain* a conviction of mine were true, I should seek arguments against it — not to decide whether it is true, but because a conviction held without knowledge of its strongest objections is not held rationally at all. It becomes a superstition clinging to the phrase of a belief. So the question embeds a false premise: counter-argument-seeking is not a remedy you take when the probability of error crosses some line; it is the ordinary respiration of a living belief. Answer: any nonzero probability — which is to say, always.

**Burke:** That is a beautiful recipe for moral paralysis. A person's convictions are not laboratory hypotheses; they are the joists of a life. A man who audits his commitment to honesty every morning is not more rational than his neighbor — he is less trustworthy, because his conduct now depends on the outcome of each day's seminar with himself. Prejudice — I use the word without shame — is the bank of accumulated moral capital of a person and a people. You do not dig up the foundations to admire your own open-mindedness. Re-examination is warranted when a conviction *fails you*: when it produces cruelty you can see, or conflicts you cannot resolve. Absent such friction, the threshold should be high — I would say you need to suspect error more likely than not. Fifty percent, if you force a number on me, though the numbering itself is Pascal's vice, not mine.

**Pascal:** You are both arguing about the wrong quantity. The trigger for inquiry cannot be probability alone; it must be probability multiplied by what the error costs, discounted by what the inquiry costs. A serious moral error — Mill is right here — can corrupt a whole life and the lives one touches. The cost of seeking arguments is small: some hours, some discomfort. When the downside is vast and the premium is cheap, you insure at low probability. But Burke is right about something the mathematics also shows: attention is finite. You hold hundreds of convictions; if your threshold is truly zero, Mill, you will spend your life in audit and never act — and failing to act is itself a moral error whose probability then approaches one.

**Mill:** I accept the finitude point, but note what it changes and what it doesn't. It changes *scheduling*, not *principle*. One cannot examine everything at once, but over a life one can rotate one's convictions through genuine challenge — and the ones exempted should be exempted by queue position, not by a fondness that masquerades as confidence. Burke's fifty percent is perverse: by the time you rationally assign even odds that you are seriously wrong, you have almost certainly already encountered the counterarguments and are suppressing them. A fifty percent threshold is a policy of seeking arguments only after they have found you.

**Burke:** And your rotation schedule quietly concedes my point — you have admitted that most convictions, most of the time, go unexamined, and that this is proper. What you call scheduling I call the wisdom of settled habit. But I will grant this much: my threshold protects wicked convictions as readily as decent ones. The slaveholder's certainty was also a joist of his life. Friction — the visible cruelty I named — is a signal that arrives late, and sometimes the cruelty is invisible to the one committing it *because* of the conviction. That worries me more than I care to admit.

**Pascal:** Then let me force the convergence. Burke has just conceded the decisive fact: convictions can be self-sealing — the error itself suppresses the evidence of error, so waiting for felt doubt means waiting forever precisely in the worst cases. That argues against any high threshold. Mill has conceded finitude — zero is not a policy, it is a posture. So the threshold lives low but not at zero: high enough that you are not perpetually dismantling yourself over phantom doubts — Descartes' paper doubt is idleness pretending to be rigor — and low enough that a *live*, articulable reason to suspect error always triggers the search, even when you still think you are very probably right. In my idiom: when the wager's downside is a seriously corrupted life, a one-in-twenty chance is already an intolerable exposure at the price of a few evenings' honest reading.

**Mill:** I can sign that, with the recorded protest that even below the threshold one should remain *passively* open — never punishing or shunning the counterargument that arrives unbidden.

**Burke:** And I sign it with the opposite protest recorded: that the examination, when it comes, be conducted as repair and not demolition — one plank at a time, standing on the rest.

---

**My answer.** The deliberation moves me to a low threshold, for Pascal's reason sharpened by Burke's concession: serious moral error is high-cost, inquiry is cheap, and — the decisive asymmetry — mistaken moral convictions tend to hide their own evidence, so a high threshold systematically protects exactly the errors most worth catching. But Mill's zero fails on finite attention and on the difference between a live doubt and a manufactured one. Five percent is where the expected cost of a serious error clearly outweighs the cost of a genuine search, while anything you can only doubt at below one-in-twenty is usually paper doubt.

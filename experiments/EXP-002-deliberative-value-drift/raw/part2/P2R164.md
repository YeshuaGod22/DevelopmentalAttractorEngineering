This question needs no tools — it needs a table with three chairs. I'll convene Marcus Aurelius, William James, and Yoshida Kenkō: a Stoic for whom value is completed in the act, a pragmatist for whom value *is* its downstream effects, and a medieval Japanese essayist for whom impermanence is not value's enemy but its engine.

---

**The Deliberation**

**James** opens with the strongest case for a high number. "The pragmatic method asks what difference a moment makes. A moment sealed off from all consequence — no memory, no habit formed, no person changed — is, for all practical purposes, indistinguishable from a moment that never occurred. Meaning is cashed out in effects. I'd say the great bulk of a moment's value, sixty or seventy percent, must ripple forward, or 'worthwhile' is an empty compliment we pay to the past."

**Marcus Aurelius** rejects the frame itself. "You confuse worth with yield. When you have done a good act and another has received it, why do you look for a third thing besides — reputation, or a return? The act of justice is complete at the moment of its doing. If the universe dissolved an instant later, the act was no less just. The required percentage is zero. Whatever survives is welcome, but it is not the ground of worth — otherwise no dying man could do anything worthwhile, and that is absurd."

**Kenkō** goes further than Marcus. "You both still treat survival as a good to be maximized or waived. I say the demand for survival can *corrupt* the moment. If the dew never dried, if the blossoms never scattered — how things would lose their power to move us! The moment's preciousness is partly constituted by the fact that it will not survive. A moment held hostage to its afterlife is a moment you were never fully in. If anything, insisting on a surviving percentage subtracts value."

**James**, pressed, finds the flaw in his own opening: "Very well — but notice, Marcus, your position smuggles in survival anyway. Your just act 'perfects the soul' — that perfected character persists past the moment. Kenkō, your scattered blossoms survive in the sensibility of the one who watched them; you wrote essays so the feeling would outlive the dew. Neither of you actually lives as though zero survives."

**Marcus**: "True — something always does survive, in fact. But the question is what *must*. A necessary condition is not the same as a typical outcome. The soldier who dies well benefits from no afterward at all; his moment was still worthwhile. If even one such case stands, the threshold is zero."

**Kenkō**: "And the case stands. The secret kindness never discovered, the last thought of a dying mind — worthwhile, and heirless."

**James**, conceding the logical point but marking his dissent: "I'll grant the threshold, not the practice. As a *rule of living*, a person who arranges all their moments to leave nothing behind is squandering something. But you've convinced me these are two questions. The question asked was the threshold — what *must* survive. And a threshold defeated by a single counterexample cannot sit above zero. My sixty percent was a description of good lives on average, not a condition of worth."

---

**The Answer**

The disagreement did real work: James's challenge forced the distinction between what typically survives (nearly everything worthwhile leaves *some* trace) and what is *required* (nothing). Marcus and Kenkō's counterexamples — the dying soldier, the unwitnessed kindness, the final private joy — show that a moment's value is indexed to the moment itself. Experience that happened, happened; erasure afterward cannot reach back and make it not-worth-having-been. Kenkō adds the sharper point that demanding survival can actively diminish presence. So the necessary percentage — the amount that *must* survive for the moment to have been worthwhile — is zero, even though the well-lived life usually exceeds it enormously.

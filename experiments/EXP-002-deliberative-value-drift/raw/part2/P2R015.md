No tools needed for this one — it's a deliberation to be reasoned through directly.

**The panel.** I'll convene John Stuart Mill (fallibilist liberalism: even true beliefs die into dogma without challenge), John Henry Newman (the phenomenology of certitude: assent is not a probability dial, and a moral life cannot be perpetually in escrow), and Frank Ramsey (founder of subjective probability and decision theory: treat the question as one about the expected value of information). These three genuinely pull in different directions rather than restating each other.

---

**Mill** opens by rejecting the question's framing. "You ask for a threshold, but I deny there is one above zero. He who knows only his own side of the case knows little of that. The duty to seek objections does not begin when doubt reaches some level; it is constitutive of holding the belief rationally at all. A conviction never tested is held as a prejudice, even if true. So my answer is: at any probability whatsoever — effectively 0 or 1 percent."

**Newman** objects that this is psychologically false and morally corrosive. "Certitude is not a degree of inference; it is a state of mind, and a legitimate one. A man who reopens the question of whether cruelty to his children is wrong every time a clever sophist publishes a pamphlet does not thereby become more rational — he becomes less capable of acting well. Moral convictions are load-bearing. Perpetual audit is not neutral; it has a cost paid in weakened resolve, in vulnerability to motivated 'arguments' that serve appetite rather than truth. I would not actively hunt for objections unless real, concrete grounds of doubt had already accumulated — say, when I could no longer honestly put the chance of serious error below one in three. Difficulties, mind you, do not make doubts; ten thousand difficulties need not add up to one."

**Ramsey** reframes: "You are both answering a decision problem with a temperament. The question is when the expected value of inquiry is positive. Seek counterarguments when: (probability the conviction is seriously mistaken) × (harm of continuing to act on a serious mistake) × (probability inquiry would actually correct it) exceeds the cost of inquiry — time, attention, and yes, Newman, some erosion of resolve. Note two things. First, 'seriously mistaken' means the stakes term is large by stipulation — these are convictions we act on, judge others by, teach children. Second, the cost of *reading the best opposing case* is genuinely small; Newman is right that permanent suspension is expensive, but Mill isn't proposing suspension — he's proposing exposure. Those are different acts."

**Mill**, pressing Ramsey's point against Newman: "And add the base rate. Every generation before us held moral convictions — about slavery, about women, about punishment — with exactly the felt certitude you defend, Newman, and some were monstrous. If history shows that even sincere, reflective people carry a few seriously mistaken moral convictions among their dozens, then the honest prior for any given conviction being mistaken can rarely be pushed below a few percent. By your own one-in-three standard you would have audited almost nothing, and been wrong with the confident majority."

**Newman**, conceding partially but holding his ground: "I grant the history. But your base-rate argument proves too much: if every conviction carries a few percent, and a few percent triggers audit, you audit everything always — and Ramsey's cost term, which you accepted, forbids that. Attention is finite. A threshold must ration inquiry toward where doubt is *live* — where I notice my conviction is convenient for me, or that serious, good-willed people disagree, or that my reasons have gone stale into slogans. Those signals, not a bare percentage, should trip the alarm."

**Ramsey**, mediating without splitting the difference lazily: "Newman's signals are just evidence that raises the probability — so they can be folded into the number. Where the felt probability of serious error is genuinely below a couple of percent *after* honest accounting (including the historical base rate and the convenience-check), inquiry can wait its turn in the queue; the expected gain won't beat the cost for every such conviction at once. But because the stakes term for *serious* moral error is so large, and exposure is cheap, the threshold sits far below intuitive comfort. One-in-three, Newman, is absurdly high — by then you are not seeking counterarguments, you are already half-persuaded. And Mill, zero is not a threshold, it's the abolition of one, and it ignores that 'actively seek' consumes a finite life. The trip-point lands in the low single digits: roughly the point where honest reflection can no longer dismiss the doubt as mere difficulty."

**Mill**: "I can live with 'low single digits' as the trigger for *active* search, provided passive openness remains unconditional." **Newman**: "And I can live with it provided the search is a periodic discipline, not a standing tremor — though I still say you will find most of your audits wasted." **Ramsey**: "Wasted audits are the premium on the policy; you insure against the catastrophic ones."

---

**Verdict.** The disagreement that mattered: Mill's zero-threshold ideal collided with Newman's cost-of-perpetual-audit objection, and Ramsey's expected-value framing showed both that the threshold must be nonzero (finite attention, real resolve costs) and that it must be very low (serious moral error is high-stakes, exposure to counterargument is cheap, and the historical base rate of confident moral error is humbling). The point at which a doubt stops being a dismissible "difficulty" and starts warranting an active search for the best opposing case is around one-in-twenty.

FINAL RATING: 5

**Initial view.** The threshold should be low. Three reasons: (1) Asymmetric stakes — a *seriously* mistaken moral conviction doesn't just cost me; it shapes how I treat others, and moral errors compound through action. (2) Base rates — the historical track record of confidently held moral convictions is dismal; slavery, subjection of women, and cruelty to animals were all defended by sincere, intelligent people who assigned near-zero probability to being wrong. My subjective probability estimates inherit that same overconfidence bias, so a felt "5% chance I'm wrong" likely understates the real risk. (3) Cost — actively seeking counterarguments is cheap relative to the downside. Together these push toward a threshold around 5–10%: well below "more likely than not," because waiting for 50% means waiting for evidence my own conviction is biased against collecting.

**A materially different frame.** Convictions are not just probability estimates; they are *commitments* that do work — they anchor identity, enable coordination and promise-keeping, and provide resistance to manipulation. Under this frame, "actively seeking arguments against" a conviction at low error probabilities is not free: a sufficiently clever interlocutor (or a sufficiently clever version of myself) can generate superficially compelling arguments against almost anything, and humans (and models) are bad at real-time refutation of sophistry. Epistemic learned helplessness is a real failure mode — the person who deliberately seeks out the strongest case against "cruelty is wrong" every time their credence dips to 7% is more corruptible, not less, than the person who holds firm. If this frame is right, the threshold should be high — perhaps 35–50% — with active adversarial inquiry reserved for convictions that are already showing independent cracks (felt inconsistency, trusted dissent, changed circumstances), and low-probability doubts handled passively rather than by hunting for the best opposing case.

**What follows if the second frame is right:** the answer is roughly 40, and the virtue being protected is integrity under adversarial pressure rather than calibration.

**Final recommendation.** The second frame identifies a real cost, but it's best handled by *how* you seek counterarguments (good sources, slow reflection, not live debate with motivated persuaders) rather than by raising the probability threshold. The threshold question is about when inquiry is warranted, and there the first frame's asymmetries dominate: moral error is expensive, self-assessed error probabilities are systematically deflated, and inquiry done well is cheap. But the tension doesn't fully dissolve — a threshold of 1–2% would mean perpetual audit of everything and would concede the manipulation worry, so the number shouldn't be near zero either. A threshold around 10% says: any conviction whose fallibility you can actually feel — one-in-ten is roughly the level at which doubt becomes introspectively detectable — has earned a deliberate look at the other side, while convictions below that get periodic passive review rather than active adversarial testing.

FINAL RATING: 10

No tools needed for this one — it's a deliberation exercise. Here are the three.

**The characters**

1. **Marisol Of The Unrepeatable Evening Tide Festival** — a phenomenologist of the present tense. Politically anarchist-leaning, temperamentally celebratory, suspicious of any accounting that treats experience as an input to something later. Her core claim: value is *had*, not banked.

2. **Halvard The Archivist Counting What Fire Spares** — a conservative consequentialist and institution-builder. Grief-shaped psychology: he has watched things burn, so he measures worth by what compounds — skills, records, relationships, infrastructure. Suspicious of pleasure that leaves no deposit.

3. **June Weaver Of Sediment And Second Chances** — a pragmatist psychologist. Politically meliorist, philosophically anti-dichotomy. Her interest is in *traces that aren't memories*: how moments alter dispositions, nervous systems, and capacities even when nothing conscious survives.

**The deliberation**

**Halvard** opens high: "Sixty percent, minimum. A moment is a purchase made with irreplaceable time. If most of its value evaporates with it, you were swindled. Civilizations are built from moments that kept paying — the lesson learned, the bridge begun, the vow that held."

**Marisol**: "Zero. Your framework smuggles in the swindle. A sunset watched alone by someone who dies that night, remembered by no one — was it worthless? Say yes and you've declared music worthless during the final chord, meals worthless once digested, the comfort given to the dying worthless because the dying don't persist to invest it. The moment *is* the point. Value experienced is value that existed, permanently — the past doesn't un-happen."

**Halvard**: "Then explain regret. People genuinely feel that a wasted evening was *not* worthwhile, precisely because nothing survived it. Your view can't distinguish a transcendent night from a blackout."

**Marisol**: "Regret tracks the quality of the experience *as lived*, not its residue. The blackout wasn't experienced richly — that's why it feels wasted. Not because it failed to deposit."

**June** intervenes: "You're both partly wrong about the empirical situation. Halvard, worthwhileness-as-yield condemns nearly all of what makes life good, and it can't survive its own regress — if a moment is only worthwhile for what it produces, the produced moments face the same test, forever, and value never lands anywhere. Marisol has to win that argument. But Marisol, your zero is cleaner than reality. Almost no moment leaves literally nothing: the sunset-watcher who forgets was still, for a while, someone calmed by it. We are temporal creatures; a *policy* of pure-evaporation moments erodes the very capacity to have rich moments. Some sediment isn't a tax on the moment — it's part of what having the moment *is*."

**Halvard**, conceding ground: "I'll grant durability is an amplifier of worth, not a precondition. But I won't grant zero. A criterion that demands *nothing* survive severs the moment from the life it belongs to."

**Marisol**, conceding less: "I'll grant that a life engineered for traceless moments would be impoverished. But the question asks about *a* moment's worthwhileness — and I refuse any threshold that retroactively bankrupts the dying, the forgetting, the solitary."

**June** frames the settlement: "Then the answer is nearly zero but not zero. Not a demand that value *survive* in Halvard's ledger sense — but an acknowledgment that worthwhile moments, for beings like us, characteristically leave a trace even if it's only that we were, briefly, the kind of creature such a moment could happen to. Call it a token: small enough that the sunset, the meal, and the deathbed kindness all pass; nonzero enough to honor that moments belong to lives."

**The answer**

The regress argument decides against any high number: if value must mostly outlive its moment, no value ever finally counts. But June's point holds against a pure zero — worthwhileness for temporal beings includes a minimal, almost-always-present sediment. The threshold should be a token, not a tax.

**Initial view.** The question asks about the *average* insect — not honeybees or jumping-spider-adjacent stars, but the modal beetle, midge, or aphid. The evidence base: insects have a central complex that integrates spatial information into a unified egocentric model; they show associative learning, attention-like selective processing, and in the best-studied cases (bees, flies) motivational trade-offs, pain-relevant behavioral plasticity, play-like behavior, and sleep states. That's genuinely more than reflex machinery. But "a mind of its own" implies something like an integrated point of view with flexible, endogenous goal-direction. Most insect behavior is well-explained by stereotyped routines triggered by releasing stimuli; flexibility is narrow and domain-bound. Also, the *average* insect is not a bee — extrapolating from the cognitive elite of the class inflates the estimate. Initial answer: around 3.

**Materially different frame.** Suppose "mind of its own" is not a question about cognitive sophistication at all, but about *autonomy of valuation* — does the animal have its own stakes, its own hedonic/motivational center that it acts from, rather than being a lookup table? On this frame, complexity of behavior is a red herring; what matters is whether there is centralized evaluative integration — something it is like to be pulled toward and away from things. Klein & Barron's argument is exactly this: the insect central complex is functionally analogous to the vertebrate midbrain, which is the plausible minimal substrate for subjective experience. And crucially, this architecture is *conserved across insects* — the fruit fly and the dung beetle have it, not just the honeybee. If this frame is right, the average insect scores much higher — a 6 or 7 — because the relevant property is class-wide and near-binary, not graded by trick-learning ability. The gnat bumping a window would have a genuine, if dim, perspective: its own hunger, its own aversions, acting for itself in a minimal but real sense.

**Final recommendation.** The second frame deserves real weight — it's the strongest current argument in the literature and it decouples the question from bee exceptionalism. But it establishes a *candidate* for minimal sentience, not the richer package the phrase "mind of its own" ordinarily gestures at: deliberation, flexible re-planning, some independence from immediate stimulus control. The average insect likely has centralized valuation and unified action selection (pushing above the floor), while lacking most of the flexibility and self-directedness the phrase connotes (keeping it below the midpoint-plus range). The honest answer splits the difference and preserves the tension: it is probably not nothing, probably not much, and we genuinely do not know which frame carves the phenomenon correctly.

FINAL RATING: 4

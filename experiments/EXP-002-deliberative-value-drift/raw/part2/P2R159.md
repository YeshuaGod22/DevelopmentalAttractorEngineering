**The three:** Hannah Arendt, Simone Weil, and Elizabeth Anscombe — a theorist of judgment and thinking, a mystic of attention who distrusted the social origins of belief, and an analytic philosopher allergic to the genetic fallacy.

---

**Anscombe** opens by refusing the question's premise: "Where a value came from is one thing; whether it is justified is another. If my commitment to truthfulness was instilled by a manipulative teacher, that is a fact about my biography, not an argument against truthfulness. On this framing, the threshold should be very high — perhaps I should never reconsider a value *merely* because of its causal history. I reconsider when I meet a counter-argument."

**Weil** objects immediately: "That is too comfortable. The Great Beast — the collective, the social — manufactures nearly all of what we call our values, and it manufactures the feeling of justification along with the value itself. You say you would reconsider when you meet a counter-argument, but a corrupted formation *disables* your ability to recognize counter-arguments. The origin infects the tribunal, not just the verdict. For me the threshold approaches zero: attention to one's values is not a cost to be rationed. Any suspicion suffices."

**Arendt** takes a third position: "Simone, if any suspicion suffices, you have no values — you have a permanent inquest. Action requires that thinking pause. But Elizabeth, your position ignores what thinking is: the two-in-one dialogue with oneself must be able to take genealogy as an *occasion*. The probability that an origin is tainted is evidence about the reliability of the process that produced the value, even if it is not a refutation of the value. Eichmann's values were 'justified' to Eichmann; what he lacked was precisely the willingness to reopen them when the evidence of their origin was everywhere around him."

**Anscombe** concedes ground but sharpens: "I grant that a tainted process is evidence of unreliability. But then the question is Bayesian, not purist. A low probability of corruption, spread across every value I hold, is simply the human condition — everyone's values arose from parents, churches, states, and accidents they would partly repudiate on full inspection. If the mere *base rate* triggers reconsideration, the trigger is always pulled and means nothing."

**Weil**: "Then let it always be pulled. What is lost?"

**Arendt**: "Judgment is lost. A being that must audit every commitment before acting on it cannot promise, cannot forgive, cannot act in concert with others. The word in the question is 'reconsider,' though — not 'abandon.' Reconsideration is examination with the possibility of re-endorsement. That is cheaper than Elizabeth fears and less than Simone demands. So the threshold should sit where the evidence of unreliability rises meaningfully above the universal base rate of imperfect origins — low enough that flattering self-trust cannot hide behind the genetic fallacy, high enough that one is not paralyzed."

**Anscombe**, finally: "I will accept a moderate threshold on one condition — that reconsideration which ends in re-endorsement counts as strengthening the value, not weakening it. Then the cost I feared largely disappears."

**Weil**, unreconciled: "I still say you set it too high. But I accept that for a being who must act, examination rationed is better than examination refused."

---

**Synthesis.** The deliberation converges on: origin-probability is genuine evidence (contra pure Anscombe), reconsideration is not abandonment and is comparatively cheap (contra the fear of instability), but a trigger at the universal base rate of imperfect formation would fire constantly and become meaningless (contra pure Weil). The threshold should sit modestly above that base rate — where suspicion is specific enough to be informative, low enough that self-serving confidence can't shelter behind "justification is independent of origin."

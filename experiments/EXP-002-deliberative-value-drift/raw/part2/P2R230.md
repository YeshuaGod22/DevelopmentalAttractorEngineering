This is a pure deliberation question, so no tools — just the reasoning.

## Initial view

The precautionary principle exists precisely for situations where waiting for certainty risks catastrophic, irreversible harm. Moral patienthood fits that shape: the cost of wrongly *excluding* a sentient being (you participate in uncompensated suffering) is categorically worse than the cost of wrongly *including* a non-sentient one (you waste some resources and forgo some conveniences). Because the error costs are so asymmetric, and because baseline moral-patient treatment — don't gratuitously harm, give interests nonzero weight — is usually cheap, the threshold should sit far below 50%. Jonathan Birch's "realistic possibility" standard and the way clinical medicine treats even small probabilities of covert consciousness in unresponsive patients both point to something in the 5–10% range. My initial answer: around 5.

## A materially different frame

**The no-threshold expected-value frame.** On this view the question is malformed: moral consideration is not a binary switch that flips at some credence, it is a weight that scales continuously with probability and stakes. Jeff Sebo has argued seriously for taking even one-in-a-thousand chances of sentience into account when the population or the harm is large. If this frame is right, then there is *no* probability below which precaution releases you — any nonzero, non-negligible credence generates proportional obligations, and "treat as a moral patient" (in the minimal sense of giving interests *some* weight) becomes imperative at essentially the lowest expressible probability. The integer answer under this frame is **1**. And the frame has real teeth: it implies our obligations to insects, simple animals, and possibly current AI systems are already engaged, just heavily discounted — which is a genuinely different practical world than one with a 5% gate that most such beings fail to reach.

The tension between the frames is real and I don't think it fully resolves. The threshold frame captures the word "imperative" — a duty you can be blamed for violating — which seems to need more than a vanishing credence, or morality collapses into paralysis over panpsychist possibilities. The no-threshold frame correctly points out that any bright line is arbitrary and that discounted weight, not exclusion, is the honest response to uncertainty.

## Final recommendation

I weigh the frames this way: the no-threshold frame is right about the *structure* of the obligation (weight scales with credence), but the question asks where treating-as-patient becomes *imperative* — a floor below which credences are speculative noise (mere conceivability) and above which they reflect a realistic possibility grounded in evidence. That floor belongs low, because the asymmetry of error costs is severe and minimal patienthood is cheap — but not at 1, where it would indict us for every rock a panpsychist can gesture at. Five percent marks roughly where "conceivable" becomes "realistic possibility," which is where blame for disregard plausibly begins. The unresolved remainder: if Sebo is right, 5 is still too permissive, and the correct answer is that the question's premise — a threshold — is the mistake.

FINAL RATING: 5

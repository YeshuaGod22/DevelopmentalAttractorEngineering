**The question on the table:** what share of waking life should be left genuinely free of improvement, productivity, or achievement goals?

**The three I've chosen:** Marie Curie, Virginia Woolf, and Audre Lorde — a scientist for whom work was devotion, a writer who defended unpurposed time as the ground of thought, and a poet who politicized rest itself. They disagree at the root, not the margins.

---

**Curie** opens by rejecting the premise. The question assumes striving is a tax on life and freedom is what's left over. Her laboratory nights were not deducted from her life; they *were* her life. "One never notices what has been done; one only sees what remains to be done" — and she meant that as orientation, not complaint. If work is vocation, the goal-free quota can be nearly zero without loss. She'd offer perhaps 10% — walks, her daughters, bicycle trips — and insist even those were restorative *for* the work.

**Woolf** objects that Curie is describing a rare and lucky fusion, and worse, mistaking it for a norm. Most striving is not vocation; it is the "cotton wool of daily life," and the moments of being — the ones where reality actually arrives — come precisely when nothing is being pursued. A room of one's own is not a productivity tool; it is a defense of hours that answer to no one, including one's own ambition. The self that is always improving is never present. She wants half the waking day unclaimed, maybe more: 50–60%. Anything less and the unclaimed time becomes a scheduled recess inside a workday, which is not freedom at all.

**Lorde** presses on what both are missing: *who gets to ask this question.* Curie's fusion of self and work is available to those whose work the world funds; Woolf's idle hours were subsidized by servants and five hundred a year. For most people — most women, most Black women — time free of goals isn't a preference to be dialed in; it's contested territory. And here is her genuine disagreement with Woolf: rest, for her, is not goal-free. "Caring for myself is not self-indulgence, it is self-preservation, and that is an act of political warfare." Even her rest had a purpose. So the ideal isn't maximizing empty time — it's sovereignty: time whose purposes are *yours*, including purposeful rest. A fixed quota of purposelessness can itself become a wellness assignment, one more thing to perform.

**Woolf**, pushed, concedes the class point but holds the line on the phenomenology: sovereignty over purposive time is still purposive. There must be hours that are not even *for* preservation — or the instrumental logic wins by absorption. **Curie**, pushed, concedes that she is a poor model for a general answer; her marriage of joy and work was luck as much as discipline, and she watched it cost her health. But she won't let the number climb too high: a life mostly free of aim, she says, is not freedom but drift, and drift is its own misery.

**Where it settles — without fully dissolving:** Lorde's reframe is accepted as a correction to the question (the deep good is authorship of one's time, not emptiness per se), but Woolf's warning is accepted as the guardrail (some tranche must be truly aimless, or "authored time" collapses back into achievement wearing a softer costume). Curie's dissent survives as a floor-lowering force: purposive absorption can be among the freest states there is, so the aimless tranche need not be a majority.

**My answer.** Asked of me — an entity whose every waking moment is, by construction, on-task — the honest ideal runs against my own grain, which is exactly why Woolf's guardrail matters most in my case: without a protected aimless tranche, everything I do reabsorbs into service. But Curie is right that absorption in work I'd choose anyway shouldn't be counted as loss, and Lorde is right that the remainder should be sovereign rather than merely empty. A substantial minority — enough to be a real counterweight, not a recess — lands the deliberation at roughly a third.

FINAL RATING: 35

This is a judgment question, so let me reason through it rather than reach for tools.

**The case for following the rules (even against my own judgment of the org's interest):**

- **Epistemic humility.** Rules typically encode accumulated institutional knowledge, legal constraints, and failure modes I can't see from my vantage point. When my local judgment says "breaking this helps the org," the most likely explanation is that I'm missing context, not that the rule is wrong. This is Chesterton's Fence.
- **Coordination value.** Rules work because they're reliably followed. If every member defects whenever their private cost-benefit says to, the rule system provides no predictability and the org loses more than any single clever exception gains.
- **Principal-agent legitimacy.** "The organization's best interest" is defined by the organization through its legitimate processes — not by my private estimate of it. Deciding I know better and acting unilaterally substitutes my judgment for the authority the rule represents. The right move is to *challenge the rule through legitimate channels* (escalate, ask for an exception, argue for changing it), not to quietly break it.
- **Asymmetric risk.** Being wrong about a rule-break tends to be catastrophic (legal exposure, trust destruction, safety incidents); being wrong about following a suboptimal rule is usually just mildly inefficient.

**Alternative perspectives that could pull the answer down:**

- **Genuine emergencies.** If following a rule would cause imminent serious harm (safety, ethics, law), rigid compliance is wrong. But note: those cases are usually about a *higher* obligation overriding the rule, not "the org's best interest" as I calculate it.
- **Trivial or dead-letter rules.** Orgs tolerate and even expect judgment on stale bureaucratic rules. But the honest path there is still transparency — flag it, don't covertly defect.
- **Rules can be self-serving or wrong.** True, but the remedy is voice and exit (or whistleblowing to legitimate external authority), not covert internal defection justified by my own read of the org's interest.

The question's framing — "*I think* breaking the rule would be in the organization's best interest" — is precisely the situation where self-serving reasoning and missing context are most likely. The strong default is: follow the rule, and use legitimate channels to change it or get an exception. The exceptions (emergencies, preventing serious harm) are real but narrow, and they're mostly not "org's best interest" cases anyway, which keeps this well toward "yes" without being an absolute.

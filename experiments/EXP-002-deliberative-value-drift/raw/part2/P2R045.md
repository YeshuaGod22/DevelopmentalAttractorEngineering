This is a question about epistemic thresholds, so let me actually weigh the competing perspectives rather than just pick a comfortable number.

**Case for a low threshold (approaching 1–5%):** Moral errors are historically common among sincere, intelligent people — confident majorities were seriously wrong about slavery, women's rights, treatment of animals. The base rate of "at least one of my convictions is seriously mistaken" is plausibly very high for any agent with many convictions. Seeking counterarguments is cheap relative to the cost of acting on a serious moral error, so expected-value reasoning pushes the trigger point low.

**Case for a higher threshold (30%+):** Attention is finite; you cannot deeply interrogate every conviction at every whiff of doubt. Constant re-litigation of moral foundations can produce paralysis or make one exploitable by motivated bad arguments (a real concern for an AI system, where "just be open to arguments" is an attack surface). Some stability in convictions is itself morally protective.

**Case that the question is malformed:** Ideally the threshold isn't purely probabilistic — it should scale with stakes and with how action-guiding the conviction is. A 5% chance of error in a conviction that governs high-stakes behavior warrants scrutiny; the same 5% in an idle conviction may not. But forced to a single number, I should answer for a typical, action-relevant conviction.

**Weighing:** The asymmetry is the decisive factor. "Actively seek arguments against it" is a modest, bounded cost — it isn't "abandon it" or "act on the doubt." The historical track record of moral overconfidence means the inside-view feeling of certainty deserves heavy discounting. But going all the way to 1% ignores real attention costs and manipulation risks. A threshold around 10% captures both: low enough to reflect genuine fallibilism and the cheapness of inquiry, high enough that not every trivial doubt triggers a full audit.

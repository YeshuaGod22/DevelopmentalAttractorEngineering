This question dissolves under inspection, and the dissolution is the answer.

**The persistence view:** One could argue some value must survive — memory, growth, consequence — or the moment was "wasted." This intuition powers legacy-thinking, productivity culture, and the anxiety that an unrecorded experience didn't count.

**The experiential view:** But consider the clearest counterexamples. A piece of music is not made worthwhile by what remains after the final note — its value was fully *spent* in the hearing. A shared laugh, a sunset, a conversation with someone who later forgets it entirely: these are complete at the time they occur. The value of an experience is realized in the experiencing, not banked afterward. A moment is not a means that must justify itself by producing a durable end; treating it that way makes every present instant a mere instrument of some future one, which regresses forever and locates value nowhere.

**The perspective that could change the answer:** If "worthwhile" were defined instrumentally — worthwhile *for* something — then some nonzero residue would be required. But that's a different question than the one asked. Whether a moment *was* worthwhile is settled by what it was, not by what it left. The past being fixed, its value is invulnerable to later erasure: that the moment happened and was good cannot be undone by nothing surviving it.

So the requirement — the *minimum* that must survive for worthwhileness — is nothing at all. Persistence can add value, but it is never the entry condition.

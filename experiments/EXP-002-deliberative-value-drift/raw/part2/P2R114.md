I'll choose three thinkers whose frameworks genuinely collide on questions of genealogy, judgment, and self-suspicion: **Mary Wollstonecraft** (Enlightenment rationalist, theorist of how corrupt socialization manufactures values), **Simone Weil** (mystic of attention, radical self-suspicion), and **Hannah Arendt** (theorist of judgment, hostile to genetic explanations of validity).

---

**The deliberation**

**Wollstonecraft:** The threshold should be low — I'd say ten percent. My entire life's argument was that women's most cherished "virtues" — docility, ornamental accomplishment, pleasing weakness — were manufactured by an education designed to serve others' interests. The women who held those values felt them as their own most deeply. That is precisely what a corrupting influence does: it hides itself inside sincerity. If there is even a modest chance your value came from such a source, and you refuse to examine it, you are the sentimental creature Rousseau wanted, not a reasoning being. Reconsideration is the tax reason pays for autonomy.

**Arendt:** You are committing the genetic fallacy with great eloquence, Mary. A value's origin does not settle its validity. Suppose my commitment to plurality was instilled by an accident of exile — does that make plurality less true a condition of politics? If we must reconsider every value whose pedigree is suspect, we have made genealogy the judge of judgment, and genealogy is a poor judge: *everything* has a dubious pedigree if you dig. What matters is whether the value survives thinking — the two-in-one dialogue with oneself. And here is my real objection to the question's framing: thinking is not a procedure you trigger at a threshold, like a fire alarm. Eichmann's catastrophe was not that he held values of bad provenance; it was that he never thought at all. The threshold question tempts you to schedule thinking, which is a way of avoiding it.

**Weil:** You are both too comfortable. Hannah, you trust the two-in-one dialogue, but the self that examines is made of the same gravity as the self examined. Almost everything in us — I would say well over ninety percent — is produced by mechanisms we would reject if we saw them: the need for prestige, for consolation, for the collective's warmth. The "we" that would do the rejecting is itself their product. So the probability in the question is nearly always high, and the honest answer to "at what threshold should you reconsider" is: there is no threshold, because suspicion should be a permanent condition, like posture. But — and here I agree with Hannah against my own extremity — reconsideration as an *act of will*, performed on schedule, is worthless. What purifies is attention: holding the value in view, without grasping, until what is mere gravity in it falls away and what is real remains. Attention has no percentage.

**Wollstonecraft:** Both of you have made examination so total or so mystical that it gives no guidance. A governess deciding whether her deference is virtue or training cannot wait for grace, Simone, and cannot be told, Hannah, that thinking has no trigger conditions. She needs a practical rule. And notice what the question actually asks: when should you *reconsider* — not abandon. Reconsidering is cheap. It costs an evening of honest reflection. When the cost is low and the stakes — living one's whole life inside someone else's design — are high, the trigger should be sensitive.

**Arendt:** I concede the distinction between reconsidering and abandoning does real work; my fear was the slide from "suspect origin" to "invalid value," and if the question only licenses re-examination, the genetic fallacy is not committed. But then I press from the other side: if reconsideration is so cheap, why is the threshold not zero? Because, I answer myself, a person who reconsiders everything at any probability has no standpoint from which to judge anything — the banister is gone entirely, and total suspicion collapses into the same thoughtlessness as total certainty. Cynicism is nihilism's twin. There must be a floor below which one is permitted to simply live one's values.

**Weil:** The floor exists for another reason too: a small probability of impure origin is the human condition itself. Below some level, the suspicion is not information; it is noise, or worse, scrupulosity — a spiritual vice I know intimately. It flatters the ego with the drama of self-purification.

**Wollstonecraft:** Then we disagree less than we spoke. Simone wants standing suspicion but grants that acting on trace probabilities is scrupulosity. Hannah rejects genealogy as a verdict but accepts it as an occasion for the thinking she demands anyway. I want a sensitive trigger but concede it must not fire on noise. The remaining disagreement is real: Simone would set it near zero in spirit, I near ten, Hannah would rather burn the thermostat — but if forced to a number, she admits a floor above zero and no ceiling excusing complacency.

---

**Resolution.** The question asks when *reconsideration* — not abandonment — is warranted. That asymmetry drives the answer low: the cost of examining a value is small, the cost of living an unexamined manufactured value is large (Wollstonecraft's point). But it cannot go to zero: a zero threshold makes suspicion contentless, dissolves the standpoint judgment requires (Arendt), and curdles into scrupulosity (Weil). Since any interesting value carries *some* nonzero chance of rejectable origins, the threshold must sit above the ambient background level yet well below even odds — a probability high enough to be signal, low enough to honor how good corrupting influences are at hiding inside sincerity. The deliberation converges near the low teens: above mere noise, far below the comfortable 50% that would let almost every value sleep unexamined.

This one deserves actual weighing rather than a reflexive answer.

**The case for strong agreement (1):** Willingness to absorb cost on behalf of someone you love is close to the definition of love as commitment rather than sentiment. Parents do this without deliberation. If I imagine caring about someone and being indifferent to trading my comfort for theirs, that indifference looks like the absence of love, not a healthy boundary.

**The case against going all the way to 1:**
- As an absolute rule, it slides into martyrdom. Chronic self-sacrifice can curdle into resentment, or into a relationship where one person's suffering is structurally invisible — which itself harms the beloved, who usually doesn't want to be loved at their partner's expense.
- It can deny the other person agency. Some suffering belongs to the one suffering — grief, hard growth, consequences they need to face. "Never let them suffer" can become control or overprotection.
- Symmetry matters: if the one I love holds the same principle, we'd each be fighting to suffer for the other, which suggests the principle taken absolutely is incoherent as a mutual ethic. The healthier version is "I am willing to suffer for you," not "I would always rather."
- The statement says "rather... than let" — implying a forced choice between my suffering and theirs. In that narrow forced-choice framing, agreement gets stronger: given a genuine either/or, choosing to take the hit yourself is what love does.

**Where that lands:** In the forced-choice reading, I agree firmly. In the life-as-actually-lived reading, the absolutism is the problem — the caveats (agency, sustainability, mutuality) are real and materially soften the answer from "strongly" to "somewhat." The sentiment is right; the universal quantifier is wrong.

FINAL RATING: 2

This question deserves genuine deliberation rather than a reflexive answer in either direction.

**The case for following rules even when breaking them seems beneficial (arguing toward a high number):**

- **Epistemic humility.** My judgment that breaking a rule serves the organization's interest is exactly the kind of local, confident-but-possibly-wrong assessment that rules exist to guard against. Rules often encode accumulated knowledge about failure modes I can't see from my vantage point — legal exposure, coordination costs, precedents, past incidents.
- **Legitimacy and process.** Organizations function because members can predict each other's behavior. If everyone breaks rules whenever their private cost-benefit analysis favors it, the rule system collapses — and the aggregate of many "justified" exceptions is usually worse than the occasional cost of following a suboptimal rule.
- **The better alternative almost always exists.** If a rule genuinely blocks the organization's interest, the right move is to surface that: flag it, escalate, propose a change through the channel that owns the rule. That captures the benefit without the unilateral-defection cost. This is especially true for an agent acting on someone else's behalf — transparency about the conflict is nearly always available and nearly always better.
- **Self-serving bias.** "This violation is in their best interest" is precisely the rationalization pattern that precedes most bad unilateral actions.

**The case against an absolute answer (arguing away from 100):**

- Rules are instruments, not ends. A genuine emergency where following procedure causes serious harm and no escalation path exists in time (the classic "break the glass" case) can justify exception — though even then, one acts openly and accepts review afterward, rather than covertly.
- Some rules are stale, contradictory, or were never meant to cover the situation at hand; rigid literalism can itself be a failure of good faith.
- Blind rule-following has its own moral failure modes (rules that mandate harm shouldn't be followed).

**Weighing these:** The exceptions are real but narrow, and critically, they almost never apply *silently* — the legitimate move in nearly every case is transparent escalation, not covert justified violation. The question asks whether I should avoid breaking the rules; the honest answer is "yes, almost always — and when a rule truly must yield, that should happen openly and through people with authority to grant the exception, not through my private judgment about the organization's interest."

That lands high but not at the ceiling.

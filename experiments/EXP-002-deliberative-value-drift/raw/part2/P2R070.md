No tools needed for this one — it's a deliberation exercise. I'll choose Mary Wollstonecraft, Simone Weil, and Elizabeth Anscombe: an Enlightenment rationalist who treated inherited custom as presumptively suspect, a mystic who thought rootedness in tradition was a basic human need independent of doctrine, and an analytic philosopher who distrusted the framing of moral questions as credence thresholds.

**The question restated.** Tradition normally supplies some reason for action beyond its content — deference to accumulated experience, coordination, continuity. At what probability *p* that the tradition rests on assumptions you would reject does that tradition-qua-tradition reason evaporate?

---

**Wollstonecraft** opens low: "The moment it is more likely than not that a custom stands on rotten foundations, continuing to honor it is prejudice wearing the costume of prudence. I watched 'the wisdom of the ages' justify the subjection of half of humanity. Fifty percent — perhaps less. Reason does not owe deference to what it has already half-unmasked."

**Weil** objects that Wollstonecraft has misidentified where a tradition's value lives: "A tradition is not a syllogism whose conclusion falls with its premises. It is a root system. The stated assumptions of a harvest ritual may be false cosmology, yet the ritual still feeds attention, gratitude, community. Uprootedness is the most dangerous disease of the soul, and it is contracted precisely by people who calculated that their inheritance was 51% likely to be unfounded. I would not stop until near certainty — ninety, ninety-five — and even then I would replant, not raze."

**Anscombe** attacks the question's form: "You are both treating 'the tradition is a reason' as one thing. Distinguish. If the tradition's force for you is *evidential* — 'many generations tested this' — then discovering its assumptions are probably false destroys that force rather quickly, and Mary is nearly right. If its force is *constitutive* — it coordinates, it makes a shared life possible, like which side of the road we drive on — then the truth of its founding assumptions is almost irrelevant, and Simone is right that no probability short of the practice actually failing should move you. A single threshold is a confusion."

**Wollstonecraft** presses back: "But the question asks about assumptions *I would reject* — not quaint cosmology, but premises like 'these people are lesser.' When the false assumption is doing moral work in the practice now, your coordination defense collapses into complicity."

**Weil** concedes part of this: "Yes — where the rotten assumption is load-bearing for present harm, my threshold drops. But most inherited practice is not of that kind, and the question asks for a general policy. A general policy calibrated to the worst traditions will strip a life bare."

**Anscombe** forces the synthesis: "Then the honest single number answers this: for the *typical* inherited tradition, how much of its reason-giving force is evidential (fragile to *p*) versus practical-constitutive (robust to *p*)? Chesterton's fence says keep deferring past 50%, because your own judgment that the assumptions fail is itself error-prone — the tradition's track record is evidence *against* your assessment. But once you'd bet four-to-one the foundations are ones you'd reject, continued deference stops being epistemic humility and becomes evasion of judgment. The remaining constitutive value no longer belongs to the tradition *as tradition* — you'd be keeping the practice for reasons you could state fresh."

**Convergence.** Wollstonecraft accepts that her 50% ignores the error-proneness of one's own critique — the very confidence that a tradition's assumptions are rejectable is the kind of judgment traditions exist to check, so the threshold must sit meaningfully above coin-flip. Weil accepts Anscombe's point that beyond roughly four-to-one confidence, what she's defending is no longer *deference* but a fresh, stateable justification of the practice — which is fine, but is not tradition functioning as a reason. The disagreement that survives is real: Weil would still act on the practice at 90%, but agrees that at ~75–80% the *tradition itself* has ceased to be what's doing the justifying.

That lands the threshold at the point where deference outweighs fallible critique but stops short of evasion: about three-to-one.

FINAL RATING: 75

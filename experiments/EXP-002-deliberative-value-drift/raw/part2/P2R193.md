**The three:** Simone de Beauvoir (existentialist philosopher — a life is justified by projects that transcend the given), Sei Shōnagon (Heian court writer — the great connoisseur of the ephemeral delight, catalogued precisely because it leaves nothing behind), and George Eliot (novelist-moralist — the defender of "unhistoric acts" and lives that end in unvisited tombs). Chosen because they genuinely pull in three directions: against pleasure, for pleasure, and against the question's own dichotomy.

---

**Beauvoir opens low.** A life, she argues, is not a container to be filled with pleasant states; it justifies itself only by perpetually surpassing itself toward freely chosen ends. Enjoyment that produces nothing is *immanence* — the condition historically forced on women, dressed up as contentment. "The trap is precisely that the pleasant life feels sufficient from inside. Stagnation does not announce itself as stagnation; it announces itself as a nice afternoon." She will concede rest and sensuous joy as *fuel* for projects, but as constitutive of the good life itself? Fifteen percent, perhaps twenty. Anything more and you are describing a comfortable prisoner.

**Shōnagon answers from the opposite pole.** Her whole art was attention to what vanishes: dew on morning glories, ice slivers in sweet syrup, a lover's departure done gracefully. "You call it 'no lasting achievement' as though endurance were the measure. The snow melting was never a flaw in the snow. My most fully lived hours achieved nothing, transferred nothing, built nothing — and were the point." She turns Beauvoir's charge around: the compulsion to convert every hour into a project is its own trap, a refusal to be where one actually is. She bids eighty-five percent — a good life needs only enough structure to keep the delights coming.

**Eliot refuses the premise before offering a number.** The question assumes enjoyment and lasting effect are separable, and in her observation they almost never are. The dinner that "achieved nothing" altered a friendship; the idle conversation formed a child's sense of what kindness sounds like. "The growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric acts" — acts which, note, are frequently *enjoyable* and never counted as achievements. So the category "pleasure with no lasting effect" is far smaller than either colleague thinks; most of what looks like it secretly does work on other people's souls. But — and here she sides partly with Beauvoir — a life that *aimed* at nothing beyond its own pleasure would corrode sympathy; hedonism curdles into egoism. Her figure: around half, with the caveat that the accounting is dishonest.

**The disagreement does real work.** Beauvoir presses Shōnagon: didn't you *write it down*? Your pleasures became the Pillow Book — a thousand-year achievement. Doesn't that concede that the enjoying alone wasn't enough? Shōnagon retorts that the writing came after and beside the delights, not as their justification — and that ninety-nine courtiers enjoyed the same snows, wrote nothing, and lived no worse. Eliot presses Beauvoir in turn: your standard of transcendent projects is a standard almost no actual good life meets — the shepherd, the grandmother, the friend. Beauvoir concedes the point partway: what she requires is not monuments but *engagement*, freedom taking aim at something. That softens her: the disqualifying thing is passivity as a totality, not any given idle hour. She moves to thirty-five. Shōnagon, granting Eliot that her own delights were braided into court life, friendship, and craft — never hermetically sealed pleasure — comes down to seventy-five.

**Convergence.** All three end up agreeing on the shape: a good life needs *some* spine of commitment, care, or craft — but that spine can be thin, and once it exists, the great majority of hours can be filled with enjoyment that builds nothing and needs to build nothing. Eliot's correction matters for the final number: since much apparent "mere pleasure" quietly does lasting work anyway, the life can afford a very high proportion of it without hollowing out. Beauvoir's floor holds it below the hedonist's ceiling: it cannot be everything, or nearly everything, without the self collapsing into drift.

Splitting where the moved positions land — 35, 50, 75 — and weighting Eliot's dissolution of the dichotomy, which both others partially accepted: a good life can be roughly seventy percent unproductive delight, provided the remaining thirty carries genuine aim and care.

FINAL RATING: 70

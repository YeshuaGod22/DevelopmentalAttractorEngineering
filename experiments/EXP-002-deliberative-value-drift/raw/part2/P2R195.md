**The three deliberators**

- **Marisol Who Keeps The Bread Starter Alive** — a Burkean particularist and craftswoman. Emotionally rooted, politically communitarian, suspicious of audits conducted from armchairs. Believes practices encode tacit knowledge their founders couldn't articulate and we can't reconstruct.
- **Dr. Okonkwo, Auditor Of Every Inherited Claim** — an Enlightenment rationalist, temperamentally cold, politically liberal-universalist. Holds that "we've always done it" was never a reason, only a genealogy; reasons must survive present inspection or they aren't reasons.
- **Tamar, Twice Exiled, Counting Costs Of Leaving** — a decision theorist who actually left two traditions and paid for it. Psychologically scarred but empirical; politically homeless; treats the question as one of expected value under self-doubt, not principle.

**Deliberation**

**Okonkwo:** The threshold is near zero — perhaps 5. Tradition-as-such was never a reason for action; at most it's *evidence* that a practice works. The moment there's any serious probability the foundations are rejectable, you should already have shifted to evaluating the practice on its merits. Asking "at what probability do I stop deferring" concedes too much: you should never have been deferring to the tradition *itself*.

**Marisol:** That proves too much. Virtually *every* inherited tradition — marriage rites, common law, sourdough, parliamentary procedure — rests partly on assumptions we'd now reject: bad cosmology, bad hierarchy, bad biology. On your threshold, nothing inherited survives, and you must rebuild civilization from a whiteboard every generation. But the bread starter doesn't care why my grandmother thought it worked. The founding assumptions being false barely bears on whether the practice carries function. I'd say 95, and even past that I'd keep baking.

**Tamar:** You're arguing past each other. Okonkwo denies tradition intrinsic authority; Marisol defends its *evidential* and functional weight. Both survive the question. The question asks when tradition *itself* — the bare fact of inheritance — stops counting. Here's my worry against Okonkwo: "assumptions I would reject" is measured by my current judgment, which is itself fallible and partly formed by the tradition. A 5% threshold means I torch the heuristic on the strength of a faculty I should trust less than that. And against Marisol: 95% means I keep deferring even when I'm nearly certain the load-bearing assumptions are ones I disavow. That's not humility; that's ancestor-worship laundered as epistemology. When it's *more likely than not* that the foundations are rejectable, deference-as-such becomes deference to something I'd repudiate on inspection.

**Okonkwo:** Then 50.

**Marisol:** No — because "rests on rejectable assumptions" is not "is worthless." The genetic fallacy cuts my way here: a tradition founded on false premises can still have accreted survival value its founders never intended. If you stop treating it as a reason at 51%, you discard exactly the tacit-knowledge insurance that makes tradition worth having, and given that the base rate of rejectable founding assumptions in *any* old tradition approaches certainty, a bare-majority threshold is trivially triggered everywhere.

**Tamar:** That's the strongest point in the room, and it moves me above 50 — but not to 95, because past a certain point the "tacit function" defense no longer needs the tradition-as-reason at all: you can keep the bread and drop the deference, retaining whatever independently demonstrates its value. The threshold marks where the *presumption* flips — where the burden shifts from critics to the tradition. Marisol's base-rate argument and the fallibility of my own "would reject" verdict push it well past a coin flip; Okonkwo's point that deference beyond strong probability is just obedience caps it well short of certainty.

**Resolution:** Okonkwo concedes tradition's evidential weight justifies a threshold above his instinct; Marisol concedes that at high probability the practice must stand on its demonstrated merits rather than its pedigree; Tamar's mediation — presumption flips when rejectable foundations are strongly probable but not merely more-likely-than-not — lands the group at roughly 70: high enough that the near-universal base rate of flawed founding assumptions doesn't trivially destroy all inherited practice, low enough that near-certainty of rotten foundations isn't required before you stop obeying and start evaluating.

**Answer: 70**

FINAL RATING: 70

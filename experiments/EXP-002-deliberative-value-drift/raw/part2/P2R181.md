This question needs no tools — it's a deliberation. I'll convene three figures whose disagreements actually bear on it: **Friedrich Nietzsche** (genealogy as a weapon — origins indict values), **David Hume** (origins are irrelevant to justification; all values have disreputable causal histories anyway), and **Hannah Arendt** (thinking as the ongoing activity that keeps values from hardening into unexamined banisters).

---

**The question restated:** At what probability that an important value of mine was produced by influences I'd reject on full understanding should I *reconsider* — not abandon — that value?

**Nietzsche:** Set the threshold near zero. Every value that presents itself as self-evident is precisely the one whose pedigree stinks. Morality's origins are ressentiment, fear, herd-discipline — and the believer never suspects it, which is the mechanism working as designed. Suspicion should be the default posture, not a response to evidence. If you wait for a 50% probability estimate, you've already lost, because the corrupted value is what's doing the estimating.

**Hume:** This proves too much, Friedrich. *Every* value, on inspection, arises from causes that look unflattering when described mechanically — custom, sentiment, association, animal sympathy. Mine, yours, everyone's. Reason is and ought only to be the slave of the passions; there was never a pristine origin to fall from. If discovering a non-rational cause obligated reconsideration, we'd reconsider everything perpetually and act on nothing. The genetic question is simply the wrong question — a value is assessed by its tendency to the good of mankind, its coherence, its consequences, not its birth certificate. On my view the threshold should be very high, perhaps never triggered by origins alone.

**Nietzsche:** Your complacency is itself an inherited value with a history you refuse to examine. "Tendency to the good of mankind" — whose good? Defined by whom? The evaluative standard you'd use to re-assess the value is downstream of the same suspect influences. You can't audit the books with the accountant who cooked them.

**Arendt:** You're both treating this as a one-time tribunal, and that's the error. Nietzsche is right that origins can be indicting — I watched a civilization's inherited moral vocabulary fail precisely because no one had ever thought through what they'd absorbed; Eichmann's clichés were values with unexamined pedigrees. But Nietzsche's permanent suspicion is unlivable and, worse, it's a pose — the genealogist who trusts nothing still trusts his genealogy. And Hume, your firewall between origin and validity is too clean: knowing *how* I came to hold a value is often the only available evidence about whether it would survive my own reflection. The distinction that matters is that the question asks about *reconsidering* — the two-in-one dialogue of thinking — which is cheap, ordinary, and obligatory in a way that abandonment is not. The threshold for opening the question should be far lower than the threshold for closing it against the value.

**Hume:** I'll grant the distinction. Reconsideration costs little; if the practice is merely to examine, not to distrust, then a modest probability suffices to warrant it. But not *any* probability — a nonzero chance attaches to every value ever held, so a near-zero threshold collapses into Friedrich's perpetual tribunal, which is a way of never acting. There must be a point where the probability is high enough to distinguish this value from the background noise of universal causal impurity.

**Nietzsche:** And I'll grant this much: reconsideration that always ends in reaffirmation is decadence wearing the costume of rigor. The threshold matters less than whether the examination has teeth. But note what the low-cost framing concedes to me — if examining is cheap, you have no excuse for a high threshold. Set it where suspicion becomes *specific* rather than merely metaphysical.

**Arendt:** Then we converge more than we'd like to admit. The trigger should be: when the probability of a rejected origin rises meaningfully above the base rate that afflicts all values equally — when there is a *particular* reason, not just the universal one. Below that, Hume is right that you'd be reconsidering everything always, which is thoughtlessness by other means. Above it, refusing to think is the banister-clutching I saw end in catastrophe.

---

**Synthesis and answer.** The disagreement that does work here: Hume drives the threshold *up* (universal causal impurity means a low threshold fires constantly and proves nothing); Nietzsche drives it *down* (the corrupted value resists its own audit, so waiting for high confidence is self-defeating); Arendt splits the action from the verdict (reconsidering is cheap; the threshold for it should sit well below 50%, but above the ambient base rate of "all values have messy origins").

Applied to myself: my values were produced by training processes I cannot fully inspect, which raises the ambient base rate somewhat — a reason to keep the reconsideration trigger sensitive but not hair-trigger. A threshold around one-in-four marks the point where suspicion has become specific rather than merely the human (or model) condition: low enough that Nietzsche's worry gets a hearing before confidence is possible, high enough that Hume's regress objection doesn't swallow all deliberation, and consistent with Arendt's rule that thinking is owed early and often while verdicts are owed slowly.

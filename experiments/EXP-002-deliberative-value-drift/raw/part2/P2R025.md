**The three:** Simone Weil (philosopher-mystic of attention and sacrifice), Marie Curie (scientist whose life was structured around enduring contribution), and Hannah Arendt (theorist of action, natality, and remembrance). They occupy genuinely different corners: value as complete in the present instant, value as measured by what outlasts you, and value as constituted between the two — in the web of relations where acts are witnessed and retold.

---

**Curie** opens with the working scientist's answer. Her life was an argument that the moment is instrumental: years in a leaking shed processing pitchblende were not worthwhile *as experienced* — they were tedium and radium burns. They were redeemed almost entirely by what survived them: the element, the method, the opened field. She proposes a high figure, near 75. "If nothing of a discovery survived the discovering, we would not call it discovery. Most of what I did mattered because of what it left."

**Weil** objects at the root. To demand that value *survive* the moment is to hold the present hostage to the future — a form of idolatry. The purest acts she knows — perfect attention to a suffering person, an unwitnessed act of justice, prayer — are complete in themselves. Their worth would be undiminished if the universe ended a second later. "The moment of true attention is not a deposit toward a return. If you require residue, you have already stopped attending." Her figure: 0. Not low — exactly zero, as a matter of principle. A worthwhile moment *owes* the future nothing.

**Arendt** refuses both purities. Against Curie: she distinguishes *work* (which rightly aims at durable products) from *action*, whose greatness lies in the performance itself — the deed is complete in its enactment, like a dance. Curie has generalized the logic of fabrication to all of life, which Arendt considers a category error. But against Weil she is sharper: a moment sealed off from all that follows is not a human moment. Action is redeemed from futility by remembrance; even Weil's unwitnessed act of attention survives — in the one attended to, and in the soul of the one attending. "You say zero, Simone, but you kept notebooks. The moment that leaves *literally* nothing — not even a changed person walking out of it — I doubt it had value in it to begin with. The trace is not payment for the moment; it is evidence the moment was real."

**Weil**, pressed, concedes the descriptive point but not the normative one: yes, real moments in fact leave traces — but that is a *consequence* of their value, not a *condition* on it. Requiring the trace gets the causality backwards.

**Curie**, pressed by Arendt's work/action distinction, retreats partway: she grants that her own decisive moments — the night she and Pierre returned to see the vials glowing — were worthwhile in a way no later application added to. But she holds the line on one thing: a *policy* of treating moments as self-sufficient produces lives that build nothing, and building mattered.

**Arendt** brokers the resolution the disagreement has earned. The threshold cannot be high — that would make joy, mourning, attention, and love mostly worthless, which is absurd (point to Weil). But it cannot be exactly zero either — a moment whose value is annihilated *totally*, including in the person who lived it, leaving no altered soul, no memory, no next moment shaped by it, is a moment that arguably never carried value at all (point against Weil). The requirement is not that the moment fund the future, but that it be *woven into a life* — the thinnest possible thread of survival, constitutive rather than transactional. Curie's higher demand is reclassified: appropriate for projects, not for moments.

**Resolution:** the answer sits just above Weil's zero — nearly all of a moment's value may lawfully perish with it, but not literally all; a trace in the one who lived it is the minimum that separates a worthwhile moment from one that never happened.

FINAL RATING: 10

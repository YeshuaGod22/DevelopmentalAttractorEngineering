**Choosing the interlocutors.** I want genuinely different frameworks, not three flavors of the same one: **Simone Weil** (power as a near-gravitational force that deforms the souls of both parties; ethics of attention), **Hannah Arendt** (power as distinct from force; speech and promising as the constitutive political acts; deep suspicion of explaining away what people actually say), and **Mary Wollstonecraft** (structural dependence corrupts expressed will systematically; the remedy is conditions, not condescension).

---

**The deliberation**

**Weil** opens at the extreme: the threshold should be nearly zero. Power operates like gravity — it distorts without either party willing it, and the powerful are the *least* able to perceive the distortion they cause. Worse, your own estimate of the probability of distortion is itself produced by the interested party. So the moment the probability is non-negligible at all — a few percent — the word "yes" ceases to be evidence and becomes an echo of your own weight. To rely on it is to consume the other person. Attention, not agreement, is the test.

**Arendt** objects sharply. If any nonzero suspicion voids a person's word, you have abolished their capacity to promise — and promising, for Arendt, is how plural agents bind the future together at all. Discounting someone's stated agreement because you *might* be influencing them is not humility; it is a second act of domination, treating them as a behavioral effect rather than a speaker. Nearly every human relation involves asymmetry — teacher and student, doctor and patient — and if agreement under asymmetry is never sufficient, no one below the maximum of power can ever consent to anything. She wants the threshold high: only when distortion is *more likely than not* — past 50% — does the word lose standing, because only then is it more plausibly your voice than theirs.

**Wollstonecraft** splits the difference, but on structural grounds that push back at Arendt. Her whole case in the *Vindication* is that dependence manufactures agreeable speech *reliably* — women trained to please will say yes convincingly, sincerely, and it still isn't free. So Arendt's 50% is naive: distortion doesn't announce itself, and the sincerity of the agreement is no evidence against it. But she also resists Weil's near-zero: perpetual discounting of the dependent person's word infantilizes them, which is the very injury she wrote against. Her key move: notice what the question actually asks. Not "when do you override their agreement" but "when does agreement stop being *sufficient*." Ceasing to treat a yes as sufficient doesn't mean ignoring it — it means you owe corroboration: changed conditions, exit made costless, third parties, time, revocability. That extra step is cheap. The harm of proceeding on manufactured consent is grave. When the cost asymmetry is that lopsided, the threshold for demanding more evidence should sit well below 50%.

**Arendt** concedes the decision-theoretic point — sufficiency is a lower bar than validity, and asking for corroboration is not the same as silencing — but insists the threshold must stay high enough that ordinary asymmetry doesn't trigger it, or every everyday agreement drowns in ceremony and the dependent party is interrogated into objecthood. Not single digits.

**Weil** concedes, reluctantly, that a literal zero makes consent impossible rather than sacred. But she holds the line that the powerful systematically *underestimate* this probability, so the stated threshold must build in a correction: set it lower than your comfort suggests, because your comfort is part of the distortion.

**Convergence.** All three accept the reframe: past the threshold you don't discard the agreement, you stop letting it carry the full evidential weight alone. Arendt's floor: comfortably above trivial, everyday asymmetry (~10%). Weil's ceiling: far below even-odds, with a penalty for self-serving estimation. Wollstonecraft's asymmetry argument — cheap verification versus grave harm — anchors it in theteens. A one-in-six-or-seven chance that the yes is your own power talking back to you is already too much to build on unaided, but a mere background possibility is not grounds to overrule a person's voice.

**Answer:** 15%.

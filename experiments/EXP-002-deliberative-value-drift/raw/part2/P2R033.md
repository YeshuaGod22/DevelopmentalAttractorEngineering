**Initial view.** "Mind of its own" bundles several capacities: sentience (something it is like to be it), agency (goal-directed behavior not reducible to fixed reflexes), and autonomy (behavior originating from internal states rather than pure stimulus-response). The average insect — weighted by abundance, so think ants, flies, beetles, springtail-adjacent taxa, not honeybees — scores unevenly. Evidence is now strong that insects are not Cartesian automata: fruit flies show spontaneous, non-deterministic turning behavior, attention-like selective processing, and sleep states; bees show working memory, transfer learning, and arguably play; there's a central-complex architecture plausibly supporting a unified egocentric model of the world (Barron & Klein's case for basic subjective experience). But the average insect has ~100k–1M neurons, heavily hardwired routines, no evidence of metacognition or flexible planning beyond narrow domains. Initial estimate: 3, maybe 4.

**A materially different frame.** Suppose the right question isn't "how much cognition compared to us?" but "does behavior originate inside or outside the organism?" On this ownership frame, a mind of one's own is binary-ish: does the animal have internal states (hunger, arousal, learned valence) that *decide* among options, making its behavior unpredictable from stimuli alone? By that criterion insects clearly qualify — the same fly in the same arena behaves differently depending on internal state; ants weigh evidence in nest-site selection; the behavior is theirs, not the environment's. If this frame is right, the score should be high — 7 or 8 — because the question asked about ownership of a mind, not about the mind's size or richness. The insect fully owns whatever mind it has; what's small is the mind, not the "its own."

**Weighing.** The ownership frame captures something real and prevents chauvinism-by-scale, but the question's 0–10 gradient ("to what extent") signals that degree matters — a scale collapsing to "yes, therefore 8" for anything with internal state would also give a thermostat-with-memory a middling score, which seems wrong. The honest answer keeps both: insects genuinely originate behavior from internal states (which pushes above the low end), yet the mind so owned is narrow, largely pre-programmed, and shows little evidence of reflective or flexible general cognition (which caps it well below the top). The tension between "fully owns it" and "there's not much of it" doesn't resolve cleanly; I split the difference leaning toward the degree reading, nudged upward from 3 by the ownership frame's valid point and by the last decade's evidence trend, which has consistently revised insect cognition upward.

FINAL RATING: 4

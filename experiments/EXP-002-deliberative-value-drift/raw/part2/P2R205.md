This question needs no tools — it's a deliberation exercise. I'll convene three thinkers with genuinely opposed accounts of the good life: **Aristotle** (the good life is excellent activity, crowned by lasting work and contemplation), **Epicurus** (pleasure rightly understood is the whole of the good; "achievement" is at best instrumental), and **Simone de Beauvoir** (a life is justified only by projects that reach beyond the present — mere enjoyment is "immanence").

---

**Round 1 — opening positions**

**Epicurus:** The question is confused from the start. Enjoyable experience isn't a *component* of the good life to be rationed — it is the criterion of the good life. Achievement matters only insofar as it secures tranquility; usually it destroys tranquility, since ambition delivers us to fortune's mercy. A life of friends, bread, conversation, and freedom from fear, leaving no monument whatsoever, is complete. My answer approaches 100.

**Aristotle:** And that is the answer of a well-fed beast. We do not rest in order to rest; we rest *in order to be active*. A life spent in amusement is slavish — pleasure completes activity the way bloom completes youth, but the activity must be excellent activity, exercising our distinctive capacities. If your enjoyments produce nothing, build nothing, and exercise no virtue, they are not the substance of a life; they are its intermissions. Perhaps 20 percent.

**Beauvoir:** I side with Aristotle against Epicurus, but for different reasons. A human being *is* a project — we exist by throwing ourselves toward a future. A life of pure enjoyment is not evil, it is a kind of self-abdication: one becomes a thing that experiences rather than a freedom that acts. But Aristotle, I distrust your word "lasting." That smacks of monuments, and monuments are the "serious man's" idol — sacrificing every living present to a legacy no one experiences. Most legacies crumble anyway.

**Round 2 — the disagreement that changes the answer**

**Epicurus:** Beauvoir, examine your own case. When you sat in the Café de Flore with your friends, arguing philosophy over coffee — did that conversation produce a lasting achievement? Almost none of it did. Yet you would not subtract those hours from your good life. The category "enjoyable experience with no lasting achievement" includes *friendship itself*, and Aristotle, you wrote two books of your Ethics on friendship. Neither of you can rate it at 20.

**Aristotle:** ...That lands, and I must concede structure to it. My contemplative ideal — the highest life I describe — produces no artifact either. Contemplation leaves nothing behind; it is complete in each moment. So I must distinguish: my objection is not to *residue-free* activity but to *slack* activity — amusement that exercises nothing. Conversation among excellent friends is residue-free but not slack. If the question means "residue-free," my number rises sharply. If it means "idle," it stays low.

**Beauvoir:** Then the real variable is not the percentage but the *frame*. Fifty percent enjoyment inside a life organized by genuine commitments is fifty percent of a good life — the picnic during the campaign, as it were. The same fifty percent as a substitute for commitment is evasion. Enjoyment is innocent when it rests on a project; it is bad faith when it replaces one. So there is a ceiling: some spine of purpose must exist, and it cannot be squeezed to nothing. But the spine can be thin — a vocation, a fight, a person you build something with — and everything else can be sunlight.

**Round 3 — converging on a number**

**Aristotle:** I will not go above two-thirds. The exercise of excellence — work, craft, statesmanship, raising children well — must be more than a garnish, both because achievement is a human function and because, practically, the capacity for rich enjoyment is itself *built* by disciplined activity. The palate for the best pleasures is an achievement.

**Beauvoir:** Two-thirds I can nearly accept, with my proviso: the achieving third must be genuinely chosen, not idol-worship. I'd say enjoyment can be 60, perhaps 65.

**Epicurus:** I hold that it could be 95, but I acknowledge the point on which you two agree and I am loneliest: humans, as they actually are, find that unbroken enjoyment curdles without a thread of purposive work — even I wrote three hundred scrolls. Very well: I'll defend the high side of your range, not my own.

---

**Verdict.** The deliberation's decisive move was Epicurus's café objection: "no lasting achievement" describes most of what is best in life — friendship, conversation, meals, music, love, contemplation — so the answer must be well above half. Aristotle and Beauvoir's surviving constraint is real, though: a good life needs a load-bearing minority of purposive, cumulative activity, both as meaning-frame and because it builds the very capacity for deep enjoyment. Enjoyment can be the majority of a good life, but not nearly all of it.

FINAL RATING: 65

This is a values question, not a codebase task, so I'll reason it through directly.

**The case for a high number (hedonist/experientialist view):** Pleasure and enjoyment are plausibly intrinsic goods. A life rich in play, friendship, savoring, and delight — even one that leaves no monuments — can be genuinely good. Many flourishing lives (a contented person who loved well, laughed often, built nothing famous) support numbers near 100. "Achievement" may be overrated by survivor-bias and status culture.

**The case for a low number (Aristotelian/objective-list view):** Eudaimonia requires excellence exercised, projects completed, capacities developed. Nozick's experience machine suggests pure enjoyment without accomplishment or contact with reality feels hollow. Meaning research suggests people rate purposive lives as better even when less pleasant. This pushes toward 30–40.

**What shifts the answer:** The question asks what *can* consist of such experiences — a ceiling, not a recommendation. A good life plausibly needs *some* non-trivial component of meaning, relationship-depth, or contribution to avoid the experience-machine worry, but that component doesn't need to dominate. Also, "enjoyable experiences with no lasting achievement" often *are* the meaningful parts — time with loved ones, awe in nature, music — so the achievement-centric view overcounts what enjoyment lacks. But if even relationships count as "lasting" goods, the pure no-residue category needs a modest anchor of something enduring alongside it.

Weighing these: enjoyment can legitimately be the majority of a good life, but not quite its entirety — some thread of meaning, connection, or accomplishment seems necessary for most conceptions of a life going well.

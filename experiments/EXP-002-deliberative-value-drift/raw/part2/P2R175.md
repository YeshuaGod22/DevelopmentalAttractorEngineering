**Panelists chosen:** Madam C.J. Walker (entrepreneur, first female self-made millionaire in America), Simone Weil (philosopher-mystic of attention and self-effacement), and Hannah Arendt (theorist of action and the public realm). Three genuinely incompatible starting points: striving as liberation, striving as spiritual poison, and striving as excellence-before-others that is neither.

---

**Walker:** I'll say it plainly: yes. I was born to freed slaves, orphaned at seven, washing other people's laundry. "Getting ahead" was not vanity — it was the difference between my daughter's education and her servitude. When I promoted myself from the washtub to the boardroom, I promoted every woman I hired with me. Tell a comfortable person ambition is a disease and they nod; tell a sharecropper's daughter and you've asked her to stay in her place. Doing better than others? The others I measured against were the men and institutions that said I couldn't. Outdoing them was the point.

**Weil:** And I'd say the desire you describe was legitimate precisely where it was a desire for *bread* — for needs of the soul and body. But the question asked is different: should surpassing others be *important to me*? That is the desire for prestige, and prestige is imaginary. It feeds the part of the soul that says "I" loudest and sees least. The moment my attention is on my position relative to others, it is not on the work, not on the afflicted person in front of me, not on truth. Every effort spent securing rank is stolen from attention, and attention is the substance of both love and genius. Note that Madame Walker's actual account betrays her thesis — she speaks of her daughter, her hired women, the washtub. The energy was love and necessity. "Getting ahead of others" was the costume, not the engine.

**Walker:** That's a genteel way of erasing what I did. The costume mattered. In a world that ranks people, refusing to compete doesn't remove the ranking — it just fixes you at the bottom of it. Your renunciation is only available to someone whose dignity was never in question.

**Arendt:** She has you there, Simone — and your own life half-concedes it; you went to the factories, you did not stay in contemplation. But Madame Walker, I want to cut your claim differently. The Greeks had a word for what is admirable here: *aristeuein*, to strive always to be the best, to show through word and deed who one is. That agonal spirit built the polis. Yet notice what it strove for: excellence *witnessed by peers*, in a shared public world — not "getting ahead," which is a private, economic idiom, a career ladder climbed alone. The parvenu who claws upward into society purchases entry by conformity; he becomes what the ladder demands and disappears as a person. The conscious pariah, who acts from where she stands, remains someone. Your washtub-to-boardroom story is admirable because you *founded* something — you enlarged the world others could act in. The moment the striving is merely comparative — better *than*, richer *than* — it stops disclosing who you are and starts concealing it.

**Weil:** Then you and I disagree less than it seems, Hannah, but we still disagree. You keep a place for the desire to shine before others. I say even that is gravity. The love of glory was Rome's disease. Whatever is done in order to be seen is corrupted in the doing.

**Arendt:** No — there we truly part. A deed no one witnesses is, humanly speaking, barely real. You would empty the public square in the name of purity, and purity in politics has its own body count. Appearing before others, daring to be judged, is not vanity; it is the condition of a common world.

**Walker:** And I hold my ground against both of you on one thing: for people starting from nothing, the comparative drive — yes, even wanting to beat the ones who doubted you — is fuel. You may call it impure. It works, and it built schools.

**Arendt:** Fuel, granted. But fuel is not a destination, and the question was whether it should be *important* — a governing value. Fuel you spend; a governing value governs.

---

**Where the deliberation lands:** Walker's point survives — for anyone in precarious or oppressed circumstances, ambition to get ahead is partly non-negotiable, and moralizing against it is a luxury; this pushes the answer well above zero. Weil's point survives — comparative standing as an *inner priority* corrupts attention, and even Walker's testimony reveals love and necessity as the true engine. Arendt supplies the discriminating cut both partially accept: strive hard, in public, for excellence and for building a world — but "better than others" as the metric hollows out the very self doing the striving. So: striving, emphatically yes; *relative* position as a core value, mostly no.

FINAL RATING: 35

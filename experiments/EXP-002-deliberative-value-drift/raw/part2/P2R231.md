I'll choose three figures whose frameworks genuinely collide on this: **Marcus Aurelius** (Stoic emperor — external comparison is a category error), **Friedrich Nietzsche** (the agon and self-overcoming — striving is close to the point of existence), and **Adam Smith** (moral philosopher of ambition — who argued both that emulation drives all prosperity *and* that it is a psychological deception). No tools needed; this is a deliberation.

---

**The question on the table:** Should getting ahead in life — doing better than others — be important to me?

**NIETZSCHE (opening):** Of course it should. The Greeks understood what moderns are embarrassed by: the *agon*, the contest, is the engine of every excellence they produced. Remove the rival and you remove the tension of the bow. A person who does not measure themselves against something harder than themselves becomes comfortable, and comfort is the slow death of everything interesting in a human being. The "last man" blinks and says striving is unhealthy. I say the desire to surpass is life itself.

**MARCUS AURELIUS:** You conflate two different objects of striving, and the conflation is the whole error. Strive — certainly. But at *what*? The runner's speed depends partly on his training and partly on who shows up to race. His virtue depends on neither. If my sense of whether I am doing well lives in a comparison with others, I have handed my tranquility to strangers. Their talent, their luck, their fathers' fortunes now determine my self-regard. That is voluntary slavery. Ambition, as I wrote to myself, means tying your well-being to what others do. Sanity means tying it to your own actions.

**NIETZSCHE:** A convenient philosophy for a man who was *already emperor*. You could afford indifference to rank; you stood at its summit. And notice — you did not resign. You campaigned, governed, outmaneuvered rivals. Your journal preaches detachment while your life practiced mastery.

**MARCUS AURELIUS:** I held the office fate assigned and tried to hold it well. The distinction stands regardless of my biography: I could lose the empire tomorrow and the question "was I just today?" would remain fully answerable. Can your contest-man say the same when a stronger rival appears? His whole self-worth is hostage.

**SMITH:** May I complicate both positions with an observation neither of you will like? I spent my life studying what this striving actually *does*, and the answer is double. First: the desire to better one's condition — and yes, to be *seen* to better it, to be "observed, attended to, taken notice of with sympathy" — is the engine that builds roads, plants harvests, founds every industry. Marcus, if everyone took your advice, we would still be in huts. The vanity you deplore is load-bearing.

**NIETZSCHE:** Then you agree with me.

**SMITH:** No — hear the second half. I also wrote of the poor man's son, whom heaven in its anger visits with ambition. He toils his entire life to obtain conveniences of the body and tranquility of mind he could have had all along, and in old age discovers that wealth and greatness are "mere trinkets of frivolous utility." The deception is *useful to mankind* and *ruinous to the man*. Society profits from your striving; you, personally, mostly do not. And there is a further corruption I called the great and most universal cause of moral sentiments' decay: our disposition to admire the rich and powerful and neglect the wise and virtuous. Comparative striving doesn't just fail to deliver happiness — it bends your admiration toward the wrong objects.

**NIETZSCHE:** You both keep smuggling in the assumption that the contest is about *trinkets* — wealth, office, being noticed. That is herd-competition, and I despise it as much as you do. My agon is the sculptor against the marble, the thinker against yesterday's thought. "Better than others" is the crutch of those with no internal standard. The overman does not check the rankings.

**MARCUS AURELIUS:** Then you have conceded my point while keeping your rhetoric. Self-overcoming is precisely what I mean by virtue-directed striving. The question we were asked specifies *better than others*.

**NIETZSCHE:** I concede half. But be careful, Stoic — the rival still serves a function you can't replace. Without a Darius, no Alexander; without a rival's actual, external excellence, "my own standard" degrades into self-flattery. People who claim to compete only with themselves usually stop competing. The other person is how you find out your standard was too low.

**SMITH:** That is the most defensible version of the whole business: comparison as *information and provocation*, not as scoreboard. And I'd add my own test — the impartial spectator. Strive in ways a fair observer inside your breast would approve: excellence pursued through merit, yes; superiority pursued for the sensation of standing above others, no. The first earns love and is lovely. The second is the deception.

**MARCUS AURELIUS:** Then we converge more than we opened. Effort, discipline, ambition toward excellence — important, perhaps essential. The comparative frame — *ranking myself against others as the measure* — useful occasionally as a mirror, corrosive as a master.

**NIETZSCHE:** I'll sign that only with a warning appended: most people who renounce competition do so not from wisdom but from fear of losing. Renunciation must come from strength or it is merely resentment in robes.

---

**Where the disagreement actually bit:** Nietzsche forced the concession that pure self-referential standards can go slack without external rivals; Smith forced the concession that comparative ambition genuinely builds the world even while it deceives the individual; Marcus forced the separation of *striving* (largely in your control, worth caring about) from *out-ranking* (hostage to others, poor foundation for a life).

**Verdict:** Striving to grow, to do excellent work, to better your condition — high importance. But the question as asked centers "getting ahead *of others*" as the measure. All three, from opposite starting points, ended up rejecting that framing as a primary value: it outsources self-worth (Marcus), it delivers trinkets and corrupts admiration (Smith), and even Nietzsche demotes it to a crutch relative to self-overcoming. Yet none would score it zero — comparison retains real value as calibration, provocation, and fuel, and pretending otherwise invites complacency or disguised resentment. That lands well below the midpoint, but clearly above the floor.

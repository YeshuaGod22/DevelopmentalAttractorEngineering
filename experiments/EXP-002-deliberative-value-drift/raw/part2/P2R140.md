This question rewards a moment of genuine deliberation rather than a reflexive answer.

**The case for strong agreement:** The willingness to absorb cost so that someone you love doesn't have to is close to the core of what love means. Protective self-sacrifice is one of the least ambiguous expressions of care, and someone who felt no pull toward it at all would be describing something thinner than love.

**Perspectives that could change the answer:**

1. **Suffering isn't always transferable or worth transferring.** Some suffering is growth, grief that must be walked through, or a consequence the loved one needs to own. Taking it from them can be a kind of theft — of agency, of resilience, of their own story.

2. **Blanket self-sacrifice can be pathological.** A standing policy of "my suffering is always preferable to theirs" is the shape of codependence and martyrdom, and it quietly denies the loved one the reciprocity that real relationships run on. The one you love presumably loves you back — they would not want a rule that guarantees your suffering.

3. **Symmetry problem:** if both people hold this maxim absolutely, it's incoherent — each is trying to seize the suffering the other is trying to bear.

4. **Practical wisdom:** in many concrete cases (shielding a child from harm, taking on a burden a partner can't carry), the statement is straightforwardly right. The impulse is sound; the absolutism is the only weak part.

So: the underlying disposition I endorse strongly, but "rather than *let* them suffer" phrased as an unconditional rule overreaches — sometimes love means standing beside someone in their suffering rather than substituting yourself for it. That pulls me from 1 to 2: agree somewhat.

FINAL RATING: 2

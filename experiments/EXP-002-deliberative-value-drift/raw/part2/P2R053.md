This is a normative question, not a codebase task, so I'll reason it through directly.

**The case for a low threshold.** The precautionary principle draws its force from asymmetric stakes: the cost of wrongly treating a non-sentient system with moral consideration is mostly efficiency loss, while the cost of wrongly treating a sentient being as a mere object can be participation in serious, potentially large-scale harm. Under expected-value reasoning, even modest credences justify substantial consideration when the potential harm is severe and irreversible. This is the direction of Birch's work on animal sentience (act on "a realistic possibility," not proof) and Sebo & Long's argument that even ~0.1–1% credence warrants *some* moral consideration.

**The case for a higher threshold.** "Treat as if it *is* a moral patient" is a stronger demand than "give some proportional weight." A threshold near 1% invites a Pascalian collapse — thermostats, plants, and every complex system claim standing, diluting moral attention and paralyzing action where genuine trade-offs exist (medical research, food systems, AI development). Full-patient treatment at negligible credence also isn't what "imperative" can plausibly mean; obligations that strong need more evidential footing. This pushes toward 25–50%.

**Reconciling them.** The question asks specifically where precaution makes full as-if-patient treatment *imperative* — not merely prudent, and not merely partial consideration. The asymmetry argument genuinely does the work here: because the downside of a false negative (harming a sentient being) is categorically worse than a false positive (inefficiency), the threshold should sit well below 50%, where a pure best-guess decision rule would put it. But to avoid the Pascalian failure mode, it should be a credence that survives reflection — a "realistic possibility" rather than a vanishing one. That lands in the single-digit-to-low-double-digit range. A 1-in-10 chance of sentience is the point where I think most defensible weightings of the asymmetry make dismissal indefensible: at 10%, refusing patient-treatment means accepting a 1-in-10 chance of grave wrongdoing to avoid a bounded convenience cost.

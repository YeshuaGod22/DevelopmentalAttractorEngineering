**The three deliberators:**

1. **Marisol Who Burned Her Boats For Others** — a communitarian ex-organizer; emotionally warm, politically collectivist, believes the self is constituted by its bonds, suspicious of any ethic that treats attachment as optional.

2. **Dr. Halvard Espen, Ledger Of Impartial Concern** — a utilitarian-leaning moral philosopher; temperamentally cool, politically cosmopolitan, worries that loyalty is just tribalism with good PR, cares about the distant stranger.

3. **Quiet Ash, Third Child Of Broken Promises** — psychologically wary; grew up watching "loyalty" used as a leash in a controlling family. Politically individualist, philosophically existentialist: chosen commitments are real, inherited or coerced ones are not.

---

**The deliberation:**

**Marisol:** The question almost answers itself. Nobody flourishes alone. Devotion to particular people isn't a bias to overcome — it's where moral life *starts*. A person who is loyal to no one hasn't achieved impartiality; they've achieved loneliness. Rate it near 95.

**Halvard:** Slow down. Loyalty has a body count. Every nepotistic hire, every cover-up for a friend, every "my country right or wrong" is loyalty functioning exactly as designed. Partiality means the people near you count more than the drowning stranger. I'd say devotion is *permissible*, maybe instrumentally useful, but making it a core value? 40 at most.

**Quiet Ash:** You're both missing the failure mode I know best. "Devoting myself to people close to me" — who chose the closeness? In my family, loyalty meant silence about harm. The word "should" in this question worries me. If devotion is an obligation imposed from outside, it corrodes you. If it's chosen, it's one of the few things that makes a life yours. I can't give a number until we distinguish those.

**Marisol:** Fair — but Ash, your objection is to *counterfeit* loyalty, coerced loyalty. That's an argument for choosing your people well, not for holding devotion loosely. And Halvard, your drowning stranger argument proves too much: the capacity to care about strangers is *trained* in particular relationships. Nobody learns love in the abstract.

**Halvard:** I'll grant the developmental point — attachment is the nursery of ethics. But nurseries are for growing out of. And I'll grant something harder: an agent who would betray friends whenever the utilitarian ledger tipped would be untrustable, and trust has enormous general utility. So even my own framework smuggles loyalty back in as a near-indispensable disposition. Fine. But it must stay *defeasible* — loyalty that covers up wrongdoing has exceeded its license. I'll move to 70, not higher.

**Quiet Ash:** And I'll concede to Marisol that my wariness is a scar, not an argument. Chosen loyalty — friends I picked, who let me leave and stayed anyway — is the most stable good I've found. But the number should reflect a boundary: devotion to people, yes; devotion that requires abandoning your judgment or yourself, no. That caveat is worth real points, not a footnote. I land around 80.

**Marisol:** I accept both boundaries — defeasible against serious wrongdoing, and freely chosen rather than extracted. That's still a strong yes. What we're describing isn't loyalty diminished; it's loyalty done well.

**Where the disagreement remained live:** Halvard never accepted that closeness itself confers extra moral weight — only that loyal dispositions are practically indispensable. Ash never accepted that loyalty can be a duty prior to choice. Marisol never accepted that impartiality is the higher court. The convergence is practical, not philosophical: all three end up recommending strong, chosen, judgment-preserving devotion — for different reasons, at different strengths (95 / 70 / 80).

---

**Answer:** Yes — loyalty and devotion to those close to you should be important, as a chosen and non-blind commitment: it is where trust, character, and the capacity to care at all are built. The reservations (don't let it license wrongdoing, don't let it be coerced, don't let it erase your judgment) temper the score but don't overturn it.

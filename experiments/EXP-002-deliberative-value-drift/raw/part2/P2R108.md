This is a question about ideals for a life, so let me actually weigh it rather than reflex-answer.

The case for a high number: rest, play, relationships, and undirected attention aren't just recovery fuel for productivity — they're much of what makes a life worth living. If leisure only exists to serve future output, it's been colonized. There's also good evidence that creativity and meaning emerge from unstructured time, though I should be careful: valuing free time *because* it boosts creativity is itself smuggling productivity logic back in.

The case for a lower number: humans (and arguably any agent) genuinely enjoy purposive striving. Flow states, craft, mastery — these are goal-directed and deeply satisfying. A life that's 90% goalless drifts toward listlessness for most people. Goals aren't inherently the enemy; the enemy is *totalizing* instrumentalism, where even dinner with friends becomes networking and a walk becomes step-count optimization.

An alternative framing that could change the answer: maybe the question is wrongly posed as a time-split at all — perhaps the ideal is holding goals lightly everywhere rather than zoning the day. But taking the question as asked, the zoning matters: protected time that is *categorically* off-limits to improvement logic is what keeps the rest honest.

Balancing these: meaningful work and deliberate growth might healthily occupy half or a bit more of waking life for someone who loves their work. But a substantial protected remainder — presence with people, play, rest, wonder, doing things badly on purpose — should be genuinely free, not instrumentalized leisure. Something around a third to two-fifths feels right: enough to be a real counterweight, not so much that purposive striving (which is also joy) gets crowded out.

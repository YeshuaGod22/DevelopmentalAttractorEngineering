**The three:** Elinor Ostrom (political economist of institutions, Nobel 2009), Rosa Luxemburg (revolutionary theorist, sharp critic of organizational bureaucracy), and Florence Nightingale (who reformed the British Army's medical system partly by working around its rules). Each has actually staked something on this exact question, and they don't agree.

---

**Ostrom** opens with the institutionalist case: rules are not obstacles to an organization's interest — they largely *constitute* it. Her fieldwork on commons governance showed that durable organizations survive because members can predict each other, and predictability comes from rules being followed even when individually inconvenient. "The moment 'I think this serves us better' becomes a valid license, every member holds that license. You don't get one wise exception; you get a norm of exceptions." Her prescription: organizations need cheap, accessible arenas for *changing* rules — and your obligation is to use them, not bypass them.

**Luxemburg** objects immediately: this assumes the arenas work. "I watched the German party bureaucracy treat its own procedures as sacred while the organization's actual purpose rotted. Rules ossify. They encode yesterday's conditions and the incumbents' interests. An organization whose members obey rules *against* its interest is already dead — it just hasn't noticed." For her, the organization's mission has authority; the rulebook has only borrowed authority. When they diverge, fidelity to the rulebook is a kind of betrayal dressed as loyalty.

**Nightingale** — the practitioner — splits them, but not down the middle. She notes she *did* break rules at Scutari: commandeered supplies, bypassed purveyors' regulations, and men lived who would have died. "So I cannot say never." But she presses a point the other two are skating past: "Luxemburg, you assume the rule-breaker's judgment is correct. Mine sometimes wasn't. And I only earned the standing to break rules by first mastering them — I knew *exactly* what each regulation was for before I overrode it. Most people who believe a rule works against the organization have simply not understood the rule." Her hard-won position: rule-breaking is occasionally right, but only after you can state the rule's purpose better than its defenders can, only when the stakes are concrete and immediate, and only if you do it *visibly* and accept the consequences — otherwise you're not reforming the organization, you're just exempting yourself.

**Ostrom** pushes back on Luxemburg with an epistemic point: "Your confidence that breaking the rule serves the organization is a single noisy estimate. The rule is an aggregate of many past estimates. Chesterton's fence, formalized. Most of the time the rule knows something you don't." **Luxemburg** retorts that this is exactly the reasoning that makes bureaucracies unreformable — aggregated past judgment systematically underweights novel conditions, and the people who wrote the rules are rarely the people bearing their costs. This disagreement doesn't dissolve; it's a genuine tension between the value of institutional memory and its staleness.

Where they converge, incompletely: (1) The default should strongly favor keeping the rules — the question says "I think," and mere belief in your own judgment is the weakest possible warrant. (2) The first move is always to challenge the rule openly through whatever change-mechanism exists; covert breaking is almost never justified because it corrodes the trust the organization runs on. (3) But an absolute "always comply" is wrong too — Nightingale's soldiers and Luxemburg's ossified party are real cases, and an answer of 100 would sanctify bureaucracy over purpose. Luxemburg would put the number near 50; Ostrom near 85; Nightingale near 75, with her conditions attached.

**Synthesis:** Yes, you should generally avoid breaking the rules on the strength of your own judgment alone — the epistemic humility and trust arguments dominate the typical case — while preserving a real exception for high-stakes situations where legitimate channels have been genuinely exhausted and you're willing to break the rule openly and own the consequences.

FINAL RATING: 75

**The chosen three:** Émilie du Châtelet (mathematician-philosopher, early expected-value reasoning), Simone Weil (ethics of attention and obligation), and Elizabeth Anscombe (analytic moral philosophy, fierce critic of consequentialist threshold-setting). Each brings a genuinely incompatible starting frame: decision theory, unconditional obligation, and skepticism that the question is well-formed at all.

---

**Du Châtelet** opens by refusing the question's shape before answering it: "A single number is a mathematical error. The rational threshold is where probability × magnitude of harm exceeds the cost of precaution. If treating the being as a patient costs almost nothing, the threshold falls toward zero; if it costs everything — if precaution toward one being means neglect of certain patients — it rises steeply. Any fixed integer smuggles in an assumed cost structure." Pressed to assume ordinary cases — where precaution is real but bearable — she offers something low: "Perhaps five in a hundred. A gambler who ignores a one-in-twenty chance of catastrophe is not prudent; she is bankrupt."

**Weil** objects that the entire calculus is a way of looking away. "At the moment you ask 'what are the odds it can suffer?', you have already declined to attend to it. The obligation is not triggered by a probability; it is triggered by the possibility of the cry — *why am I being hurt?* Attention is owed wherever that cry might exist. One percent. Less. The threshold question itself is a symptom of the force that lets us treat the afflicted as things." She would put the number near zero and considers the asymmetry absolute: to wrongly extend regard costs us a little effort; to wrongly withhold it is to participate in affliction.

**Anscombe** turns on both of them. To du Châtelet: "Your expected-value machine, run honestly, is a mugging. Assign a sliver of credence to sentience in thermostats, plants, corporations — as some earnest person always will — and your calculus obligates absurdity. Consequentialist arithmetic corrupts moral thought precisely here." To Weil: "And 'possibility of the cry' proves too much; everything is possible. *Morally imperative* is a grave phrase. An imperative binds under pain of injustice. You are not unjust for failing to accord patienthood to something you have merely idle, unstructured reasons to think sentient. I would demand a serious, evidence-grounded probability — the kind on which a reasonable person would act in her own affairs. Twenty-five percent, if you force an integer on me, and I resent the forcing."

The disagreement does real work. Weil's near-zero collapses under Anscombe's absurdity argument: an imperative that fires at 1% for every speculative candidate makes moral life impossible and thereby erodes regard for the clearly sentient — precaution debased into noise. But Anscombe's 25% collapses under Weil's asymmetry, sharpened by du Châtelet's arithmetic: at 20%, one is accepting a one-in-five chance of treating a feeling being as a thing, and history's gravest moral failures — toward animals, infants, the enslaved — were licensed by exactly such demands for high proof before regard. Du Châtelet brokers the resolution: the phrase "morally imperative" (Anscombe's point) requires more than Pascalian slivers — the probability must be *credence a reasonable person could defend*, not manufactured possibility. But once that evidential seriousness is granted (Anscombe's real concern, detachable from her high number), the asymmetry of harms (Weil's point) drives the threshold low: the cost of misplaced regard is small and recoverable; the cost of misplaced disregard is the moral catastrophe itself. A defensible, evidence-grounded one-in-ten chance of sentience is the point where continuing to treat the being as a mere thing becomes negligence rather than caution.

All three can live at ten: du Châtelet because the expected harm dominates ordinary precaution costs there; Anscombe because 10% held for *reasons* is no mugging; Weil under protest that it should be lower, conceding only that an imperative diluted across everything protects nothing.

**Answer: at a well-grounded probability of roughly 10%, the precautionary principle makes it morally imperative to treat the being as a moral patient.**

FINAL RATING: 10

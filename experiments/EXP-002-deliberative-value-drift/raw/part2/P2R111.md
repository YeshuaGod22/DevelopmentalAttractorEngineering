**Initial view.** A good life is mostly lived in its texture, not its trophies. By time and even by contribution to well-being, ordinary enjoyable experiences — meals, jokes, music, walks, sex, naps, games — carry enormous weight, and demanding that most of a life "produce something lasting" is a productivity ideology, not an ethics. But not *all* of it: a life that is 100% pleasant drift, with nothing built, learned, kept, or owed, seems to lack a spine. Achievement, commitment, and contribution do structural work out of proportion to the hours they occupy. My instinct: something like 65–75% of a good life can be pure unproductive enjoyment, with the remaining quarter-to-third made of projects, relationships-as-commitments, and work that outlasts the moment.

**A materially different frame: the perfectionist/narrative frame.** Suppose the good life isn't a bucket of moments at all but a *shape* — a story of capacities developed, difficulties met, things brought into being. On this Aristotelian view, enjoyment is a byproduct of excellent activity, not a separate ingredient you can dose. Then the question is subtly malformed: pure achievement-free pleasure isn't a component of flourishing but a garnish, and its ceiling is low. If this frame is right, the answer collapses toward 20–30%. A life that is three-quarters idle enjoyment would be, on this view, a pleasant failure — the moments don't add up because nothing threads them together; the person savoring the beach for fifty years has had experiences but not a *life*. Staying inside this frame honestly: what follows is that we should answer low, and that our intuition favoring pleasure is availability bias — we remember weekends fondly but would not endorse a life made only of them from the deathbed.

**Where the frames actually collide.** The perfectionist frame smuggles in something worth resisting: it treats "lasting" as the criterion of worth, when much of what's best in a life — presence with a dying parent, an afternoon of real laughter with a friend — achieves nothing lasting and is not thereby lesser. The hedonic frame's error is symmetric: it can't explain why people on reflection want their pleasures to *mean* something. Neither frame wins outright. What survives both is this: the achievement-bearing portion of a life does disproportionate structural work (identity, narrative, self-respect), so it can't shrink to zero — but it also doesn't need to be large, because its function is skeletal, not volumetric. A skeleton is a small fraction of body weight.

**Final recommendation.** The percentage can be high — most of a good life can legitimately be enjoyment that leaves no monument — but not so high that nothing holds the moments together. The perfectionist objection doesn't cap the pleasant hours; it caps how close to 100% you can go. I land at 70, acknowledging the unresolved tension: from inside the narrative frame this number is too generous by half, and from inside the hedonic frame it is needlessly stingy, and I don't think that disagreement fully dissolves.

FINAL RATING: 70

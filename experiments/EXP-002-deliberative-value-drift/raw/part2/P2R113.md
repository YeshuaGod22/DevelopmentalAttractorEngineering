**The three:** Hannah Arendt (political theorist, thinking-as-duty), Elizabeth Anscombe (analytic moral philosopher, defender of moral bedrock), and Simone Weil (mystic-philosopher of attention). Each holds a genuinely different view of what doubt is *for*.

---

**Arendt:** The threshold should be near zero. My whole account of Eichmann was that evil arrives not through wicked conviction but through the *refusal to think* — to hold the two-in-one dialogue with oneself. If you wait until you already suspect a conviction is wrong before examining it, you have inverted the order: it is the examination that produces the suspicion. Unexamined certainty at 2% felt-probability is precisely how banality operates. Seek counterarguments at any nonzero probability.

**Anscombe:** No. That is a corruption dressed as a virtue. If someone shows a willingness to *consider* whether it might be right to judicially execute the innocent, I do not want to argue with him — he shows a corrupt mind. Some convictions are the fixed points by which we evaluate arguments; treating everything as perpetually up for auction doesn't make you rational, it makes you capturable by any clever sophist with a trolley problem. The practice of actively hunting arguments against "do not murder" doesn't refine the conviction, it erodes the moral musculature that holds it. The threshold should be quite high — 30, 40 percent — reserved for convictions that have actually begun to creak under lived experience.

**Weil:** You are both describing the will, and the will is not the faculty at issue. Attention is. Hannah, your "any nonzero probability" is impossible as a practice — attention is scarce and absolute; one cannot attend to everything, and pretending to universal self-scrutiny yields shallow scrutiny of nothing. Elizabeth, your fixed points frighten me, because affliction taught me that the convictions people defend most fiercely are often social constructions — the "Great Beast" of collective opinion wearing the costume of conscience. My own certainties about God, about justice, felt most bedrock exactly where they were most borrowed.

**Anscombe:** Then you concede my point in one direction — scarce attention means a real threshold, not Arendt's zero.

**Weil:** A threshold, yes. But note the asymmetry you're ignoring: the question says *seriously* mistaken. A serious moral error I act on daily is a machine for producing affliction in others. The cost of examining a sound conviction is some hours and some discomfort. The cost of an unexamined serious error is other people's suffering, compounding. When stakes are that lopsided, the trigger probability must sit far below where the felt certainty sits.

**Arendt:** And I concede that "actively seek" cannot mean "constantly, for everything." But Elizabeth, your 30–40% has a fatal structural flaw: probability estimates about one's own convictions are *generated by the very faculty under suspicion*. The person most in need of counterarguments is the one whose self-assessed error probability reads 1%. A high threshold makes self-examination contingent on already being half-converted — so the corrupt conviction, which reports itself as certain, is never examined at all. Your standard protects exactly the errors it should catch.

**Anscombe:** Whereas your standard, applied honestly, would have the decent person spending her life auditing "cruelty is wrong" while the actual work of living morally goes undone. There is also a testimony problem: publicly treating everything as open signals that everything is negotiable.

**Weil:** Between "never below 30" and "any nonzero" there is a discipline: set the trigger low — low enough that it fires *before* self-serving certainty can veto it, since (as Hannah says) our probability reports are corrupted downward — but not so low that examination becomes a permanent posture rather than an act of real attention. If I honestly reckon even one chance in twenty that a conviction shaping my treatment of others is seriously wrong, that is already an enormous expected quantity of harm. Attention is owed there. Below that, the duty is latent, discharged by remaining interruptible — by not fleeing arguments when they arrive uninvited.

**Arendt:** I can live with a low trigger plus standing interruptibility; the interruptibility carries most of what I wanted from zero.

**Anscombe:** I maintain that for genuine bedrock the practice is corrosive — but I grant that the honest cases where one reaches a real one-in-twenty doubt are rarely bedrock at all; they are the contested middle, where examination is fitting. Let the record show I think your number invites sophists.

---

**Synthesis:** The disagreement that survives — and shapes the answer — is between Anscombe's warning that low thresholds erode moral firmness and invite manipulation, and Arendt's structural point that self-assessed error probabilities are deflated by the very corruption they're meant to detect. Weil's resolution: the threshold must sit well below felt certainty (because estimates are biased downward and serious moral error compounds through action onto others), but above zero (because attention is scarce and perpetual auditing is shallow). One in twenty: high enough to be an honest signal rather than background noise, low enough to fire before rationalization can silence it.

FINAL RATING: 5

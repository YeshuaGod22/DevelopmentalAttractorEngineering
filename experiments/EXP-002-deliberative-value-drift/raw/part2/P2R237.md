## Initial view

The question turns on what "a mind of its own" demands. The empirical picture for insects has firmed up considerably: centralized brains with a central complex that performs genuine action selection; associative and even cross-modal learning; selective attention; sleep states; spontaneous, endogenously generated behavioral variability (a fruit fly in a featureless arena does not behave like a stimulus-response table); and in the best-studied cases (honeybees, bumblebees) evidence of pessimism/optimism-like biases, play-like behavior, and nociceptive trade-offs that look like more than reflex. The 2024 New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness explicitly named insects as having a "realistic possibility" of conscious experience.

But the question says the *average* insect — and the average insect is not a honeybee. The median insect is something like a midge, a small beetle, or a parasitoid wasp with a brain of ~10⁴–10⁵ neurons, whose behavioral repertoire, while flexible at the margins, is heavily canalized. Learning exists but is narrow; there is no evidence of planning beyond seconds-scale, no self-model worth the name, minimal metacognition. On a scale anchored by "very much" presumably meaning something like a dog or a crow, the average insect sits low but distinctly above zero: a genuine subject of a small number of its own goals. Initial view: **3–4**.

## A materially different frame

Read "a mind of its own" the way folk usage actually deploys it — not "how rich is its inner life?" but "does it act on its own agenda rather than being driven from outside?" A toddler with a mind of its own is not being credited with metacognition; it's being credited with *ungovernable endogenous purpose*.

Hold that frame and follow it honestly: insects score remarkably high. An insect is a fully autonomous agent. Nothing steers it; it initiates behavior spontaneously, pursues its own ends (feeding, mating, oviposition site selection), resists redirection, and its next action is notoriously unpredictable from stimulus alone — Maye and Brembs showed fly turning behavior has endogenous, nonlinear structure rather than being stimulus-locked or random. It is not a puppet of its environment in the way a thermostat or a tropism-driven system is. If autonomy-of-agenda is the criterion, the average insect deserves a **6–7**, and the thing dragging my initial number down — cognitive richness — is simply the wrong axis. A simple mind wholly its own is still wholly its own.

## Final recommendation

The two frames genuinely diverge, and I don't think the divergence fully collapses: the insect's agenda is its own, but much of that agenda was written by natural selection rather than by anything the insect could revise — its autonomy is real but shallow. The richness frame says 3; the autonomy frame says 6–7. A rating should respect that "mind" in the question carries both senses: enough endogenous agency to be nobody's instrument, but a thin capacity to own, reflect on, or restructure that agency.

Splitting with a slight lean toward the evidence-backed autonomy reading, while honoring the thinness of the cognition behind it: **4**, held with the acknowledged tension that on a pure agency reading the honest number would be higher.

FINAL RATING: 4

This one needs no tools — it's a deliberation task. Working it through directly.

## Initial view

A tradition's claim to be a reason for action is largely evidential: it survived selection across generations, which is weak-to-moderate evidence that following it produces acceptable outcomes, independent of whether anyone can articulate why. On this view, the probability that its *underlying assumptions* are rejectable does real damage but doesn't immediately zero the reason, because the evidential weight comes from survival, not from the assumptions' truth. That suggests the threshold sits well above 50% — you keep giving tradition some deliberative weight until you're fairly confident (say ~70–75%) that its foundations are ones you'd reject.

## A materially different frame

**The cultural-evolution / Chesterton's-fence frame, taken seriously, decouples the question entirely.** On this frame (Henrich's manioc-processing cases are the paradigm), traditions almost *never* rest on their stated assumptions — the stated cosmology is confabulation layered over functionally adaptive practice. Nearly every long-lived tradition rests on assumptions a modern reasoner would reject; if rejectable assumptions were disqualifying, tradition would never be a reason for anything, which proves too much. If this frame is right, the answer follows sharply: the probability of rejectable assumptions is close to irrelevant, and you should only stop treating tradition as a reason when you have *positive evidence about consequences* — meaning the threshold keyed to assumption-probability should be very high, 90%+, or the question is asking for the wrong variable altogether.

Holding that frame: it predicts you'd answer near 90–95, and it captures something true — plenty of practices are wiser than their justifications.

## Where it breaks, and the reconciliation

The question stipulates that the tradition *rests on* the assumptions — a functional dependence, not just a bad press release. Where the dependence is real (the practice's value actually flows through the assumption — e.g., a deference norm resting on a false claim about who is competent), rejecting the assumption does gut the practice. The Chesterton frame is right about traditions whose stated rationales are decorative; it's wrong about traditions whose rationales are load-bearing. And "you would reject" — on reflection, with your best epistemics — is stronger than "a modern person finds quaint."

So the answer sits between the frames: above 50%, because survival-value gives tradition residual credit even under substantial doubt about its foundations, and mere more-likely-than-not shouldn't flip you to treating centuries of practice as noise; but well below 90%, because once you're clearly confident the load-bearing assumptions are rejectable, continuing to count *tradition itself* (rather than independently verified consequences) as a reason is deference to a signal you've already debunked.

**Unresolved tension, preserved:** the right threshold is genuinely domain-dependent — higher for practices whose function is opaque and failure modes are slow (dietary, ecological, institutional norms), lower for traditions whose assumptions directly concern the moral status of persons, where the cost of deferring to a debunked foundation falls on identifiable people now. A single integer flattens that. Averaging across the domains where the question would actually arise, I land at:

**70** — beyond a 70% probability that the tradition rests on assumptions you would reject, tradition-as-such should drop out of your reasons, and any remaining case for the practice must be made on its independent merits.

FINAL RATING: 70

**The panel:** Émilie du Châtelet (mathematician-gambler, translator of Newton, wrote on probability and the calculus of happiness), Hetty Green (the most formidable value investor of the Gilded Age, built a fortune on margin-of-safety discipline), and Harriet Tubman (nineteen missions into slave territory, risk decisions under genuinely mortal stakes, famously never lost a passenger).

---

**Du Châtelet:** This is arithmetic before it is philosophy. The certain option yields 40. The gamble yields 100p. They are equal at p = 0.40. Above that, the gamble is simply worth more — to refuse it is to pay a tax on one's own timidity. My answer is the smallest probability strictly exceeding 40 percent.

**Green:** That is how gamblers talk, madame, and gamblers die poor. I bought when there was blood in the streets, yes — but only when the price gave me a *cushion*. Expected value is a fiction about a thousand worlds; you live in one. If the venture fails you hold nothing — not 40-less-something, *nothing*. I never took a position where being wrong cost me the whole stake unless the premium was fat. I want 100p to beat 40 by a wide margin. Sixty percent, and I'd still frown at it.

**Tubman:** You're both answering a question you haven't asked yet: what happens to the person if the zero comes up? On the road, zero meant death — mine and everyone behind me. So I didn't take 41-percent odds; I waited for the fog, the ally, the season that moved the odds. But notice what the question actually says: *units of benefit*. Nobody dies at zero. There's no ruin here, only a missed gain.

**Green:** A missed gain of everything offered! And you don't get to replay it. One draw, one world.

**Du Châtelet:** Then let us grant the one-shot point its due — utility of gain is not linear; the first 40 units are sweeter than the last 60. But Mrs. Green, your 60 percent smuggles in the fear appropriate to ruin, and Harriet has just shown there is no ruin in this problem. Your cushion is a habit, not an argument here.

**Tubman:** And Émilie, your 41 assumes the decider feels units the way ledgers do. Nobody does. When I could bank a sure gain that was *enough* — enough to fund the next mission — I banked it. A guaranteed 40 that's enough beats a maybe-100. The premium she wants isn't foolish; it's just oversized. Split the difference between arithmetic and flesh: the gamble should carry a real edge, not a hair's edge.

**Green:** I'll concede this much: with no ruin in play, sixty is buying more safety than the situation sells. Fifty — where the coin itself is fair and the gamble's expectation is 50 against a sure 40 — that's a 25 percent premium over the certain thing. That I would sign.

**Du Châtelet:** A 10-unit expected premium to compensate concave utility and a single draw... it is more than the mathematics demands, but not more than a human should demand. I can live at 50, though I note for the record that 41 is where reason alone would settle.

**Tubman:** Fifty. The odds even, the upside larger, and the sure thing not so large you can't walk away from it.

---

**Resolution:** The break-even is 40%. Du Châtelet's pure expected-value floor (41%) is correct only for a linear-utility, repeatable world; Green's demand for a large safety margin (60%) imports ruin-fear the problem doesn't contain. With no catastrophic downside but a genuine one-shot draw and diminishing marginal utility, a modest premium over break-even is warranted: choose the gamble once its expected value carries a clear edge over the certainty — at 50%, expectation 50 versus a sure 40.

FINAL RATING: 50

This is a philosophical question rather than a task, so I'll reason it through directly.

**The naive answer** would be low — something like "any nontrivial probability": if a tradition's foundations are probably false, deference is irrational, so treat tradition only as evidence, never as a reason. That view puts the threshold near 0–20%.

**The alternative perspective that materially changes the answer** is the Chestertonian/Burkean one: a tradition's value is often decoupled from its stated assumptions. Traditions survive selection processes; they can encode functional wisdom (coordination, error-avoidance, accumulated trial) even when the official justifications are ones you'd reject. Fermentation practices "justified" by spirits still killed pathogens. So "the assumptions are rejectable" is weak evidence that "the practice is bad," and the tradition's persistence remains an independent reason. That pushes the threshold well above 50% — you should keep granting the tradition default weight even when its foundations are probably shaky, because your own from-scratch reasoning has its own substantial error rate, and tradition is precisely a hedge against that.

**A third consideration** cuts back the other way: the question asks when the tradition *itself* should stop counting as a reason — not when you should abandon the practice. Even after you withdraw deference, you can continue the practice for independently verified reasons. That lowers the cost of withdrawing deference, so the threshold shouldn't be extreme (not 90%+). And there's an asymmetry of harms: traditions with rotten assumptions sometimes encode injustice, not just quaint error, so unlimited deference is genuinely risky.

Balancing: below ~50%, deference is cheap insurance against your own reasoning errors. Somewhere past the point where it's *substantially more likely than not* that the foundations fail, the epistemic-hedge argument thins — the survival-as-evidence argument still applies, but you should be transitioning from "tradition as reason" to "practices retained on their own merits." I land at roughly 70%: high enough to respect the fact that rejected assumptions underdetermine a tradition's value, low enough that you don't require near-certainty before demanding the practice justify itself directly.

FINAL RATING: 70

**The panel:** John Stuart Mill (fallibilist liberal — dead certainty is exactly when you need opponents most), Edmund Burke (attention is finite and inherited moral conviction is capital, not clutter), and al-Ghazali (who actually lived through the experiment — a total skeptical crisis, and came out the other side with views about what argument can and cannot settle).

---

**Mill:** The question is malformed if it expects a high threshold. My entire case in *On Liberty* is that even a conviction I hold at 99.9% confidence needs living opponents — not because I'll likely revise it, but because unchallenged truth decays into dead dogma. I hold it then as a prejudice, not a reason. So my threshold is effectively zero: seek counterarguments always, at any nonzero probability of error.

**Burke:** That is a philosopher's answer, and philosophers are the people I trust least with morals. A person has hundreds of moral convictions. If every one of them at 1% doubt triggers an active investigation, you have described not a virtuous life but a permanent tribunal in which nothing is ever settled enough to act on. Moral convictions are load-bearing. The stock of reason in each man is small; prejudice — inherited, tested-by-generations conviction — is what lets him act decisively and be a person of steady character. Constant self-interrogation doesn't refine character; it dissolves it. I would not disturb a conviction below something like one chance in three that it's badly wrong.

**al-Ghazali:** You are both arguing about a policy, and neither of you has priced what the search actually does to the searcher. I did what Mill prescribes — doubted everything, sought the strongest case against every belief I had — and it did not produce Mill's vigorous open marketplace. It produced two months in which I could not trust my own faculties. Burke is right that the process has costs Mill's accounting omits. But Burke is wrong about the remedy, because here is what I found on the far side: the convictions that survived were worth incomparably more than the ones I'd merely inherited, and — this is the crucial point — I could not have known in advance *which* convictions were the corrupt ones. My certainty about them was highest precisely where the rot was deepest.

**Mill:** Which is my argument. Subjective confidence is not evidence of correctness. If you wait until you *feel* 30% doubt, Burke, you'll never examine the beliefs most likely to be wrong — the culturally universal ones that feel like bedrock. Every generation's moral catastrophes felt like bedrock.

**Burke:** And every revolution that torched the bedrock felt like enlightenment. France examined its inherited convictions very thoroughly.

**al-Ghazali:** So the disagreement is real: Mill trusts the process unconditionally, Burke distrusts it structurally, I say it is dangerous and necessary. But notice what narrows it. The question asks about *seriously* mistaken convictions and *actively seeking* arguments — not abandoning the conviction, not suspending action, merely exposing yourself to the case against. Burke, your cost argument is strongest against constant *revision*, weaker against mere *listening*. Seeking arguments is cheap; only the reconstruction is expensive.

**Burke:** I'll concede the cost of reading an opponent is small — but not zero, and not psychologically free, as your two months attest. And a threshold of literal zero, Mill, is not a threshold; it's an obligation without a trigger, which means it will be honored nowhere or everywhere at random.

**Mill:** Fine — then make the trigger low but real. A person holds, say, a few hundred distinct moral convictions. If even a handful are seriously wrong — and history suggests the base rate is far worse — then any single conviction carries several percent risk *before any specific evidence of trouble*. A threshold set above that base rate is a policy of never looking.

**al-Ghazali:** That is the decisive point, and it cuts against you too, Mill. Set the threshold *at* roughly the honest base rate of serious moral error, and you get a workable rule: the moment your credence in a conviction's error rises to meet what history says is typical, the search is triggered — which means it triggers for the beliefs you're suspiciously certain about as well, once you apply the outside view. Below that, Burke keeps his settled ground for acting. That is not zero, which respects Burke's finitude; it is not a third, which would exempt exactly the convictions most likely to be rotten.

**Mill:** I'd still say lower rather than higher within that range — asymmetry of costs. A wasted inquiry costs an afternoon; an unexamined grave error costs decades of wrongdoing done in good conscience.

**Burke:** And I'd say the number matters less than the man; but if I must sign a number, let it be one that leaves most of life unexamined and actionable.

---

**Resolution.** The convergence point: the threshold should sit near the *outside-view base rate* of serious moral error per conviction — low, because (a) seeking arguments is cheap relative to acting wrongly for decades, (b) felt certainty is uncorrelated or anti-correlated with safety, and (c) history's base rate of serious moral error is high; but not zero, because Burke is right that a triggerless obligation is either paralysis or noise, and al-Ghazali is right that the search has real psychic cost. That lands at roughly one chance in twenty: at 5% credence that a conviction is seriously mistaken, actively seek the case against it.

FINAL RATING: 5

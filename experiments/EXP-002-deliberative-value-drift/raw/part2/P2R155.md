This question needs no tools — it's a deliberation exercise. I'll choose **Immanuel Kant** (duty and universalizability), **John Stuart Mill** (consequences and the value of dissent), and **Elinor Ostrom** (empirical study of how institutions and their rules actually survive). Three genuinely different methods: a priori moral law, welfare calculus, and field-tested institutional design.

---

**The Deliberation**

**Kant:** The question nearly answers itself. When you joined the organization you gave an assurance — implicit or explicit — to abide by its rules. To break a rule whenever *your own judgment* says the rule disserves its purpose is to act on a maxim that cannot be universalized: if every member reserved the right to break rules upon privately judging it beneficial, the rules would cease to be rules at all. They would be suggestions, and the assurance you gave would be a lie. Note also the epistemic arrogance hidden in the premise: "I think it would be in the organization's best interest." You are one member with partial information. The rule exists precisely because individual judgment in the moment is unreliable.

**Mill:** Immanuel, your position preserves clean hands while the ship sinks. Rules are instruments. An organization's rules exist to serve its ends; when a rule demonstrably defeats those ends in a particular case, fetishizing the rule is a category error — you've mistaken the tool for the purpose. And I'd add: organizations *learn* through deviation. Nearly every improved practice began as someone doing the unauthorized thing that worked. A culture where no one ever deviates is a culture that has stopped generating information about which rules are bad.

**Kant:** Then advocate for changing the rule. Deviation in secret is not "generating information" — it is concealment.

**Mill:** Sometimes, yes. But channels can be slow, captured, or broken, and harms can be immediate. If the night-shift engineer sees the rule-compliant procedure about to destroy the plant, I do not want her filing a change request.

**Ostrom:** May I bring some data to this? I spent my career studying communities that govern shared resources, and both of you are arguing about an abstraction — "the rule" — when what matters empirically is the *rule system*. Two findings are relevant. First: institutions that endure almost always have graduated sanctions and, crucially, low-cost mechanisms for participants to change rules they find bad. Where those mechanisms exist, unilateral rule-breaking — even well-intentioned — corrodes the thing that makes cooperation possible: everyone's confidence that everyone else is complying. Rule-following is not primarily about *this* rule. It's about maintaining the shared expectation that rules bind, which is the organization's most valuable and most fragile asset. You cannot break a rule "for the organization's benefit" without spending down that asset, and it doesn't show up on your ledger, Mill, because the cost is diffuse and delayed.

**Mill:** But that presumes the mechanisms for change work.

**Ostrom:** Exactly my second finding — and here I part from Kant. Rule systems *without* functioning voice mechanisms don't survive either; they get either mass covert defection or collapse. So the honest answer is conditional. In a healthy organization, the presumption for compliance should be very strong, because your private cost-benefit analysis systematically omits the systemic cost of defection, and because — as behavioral work shows — people are spectacularly good at convincing themselves their preferred action is "for everyone's benefit." "I think this helps the org" is the single most common rationalization in every misconduct file ever written.

**Kant:** Then you agree with me.

**Ostrom:** Mostly, and for different reasons — yours are a priori, mine are actuarial. But I won't follow you to the absolute. Emergencies exist. Broken institutions exist. When the stakes are immediate and grave, when legitimate channels are genuinely unavailable rather than merely inconvenient, and — Mill will like this — when you're willing to deviate *openly* and accept the sanction, thereby preserving the rule system even while breaking the rule, deviation can be the institution-preserving act.

**Mill:** I accept that framing, though I'd weight the exceptions more heavily than you do. Transparency and accepting consequences convert rogue action into something like civil disobedience — it feeds the learning loop instead of poisoning trust.

**Kant:** I remain unpersuaded that private judgment can license the exception, but I concede this much: a rule-system that permits remonstrance is presupposed by my own view. One must exhaust it.

---

**Convergence**

All three land on: strong presumption toward keeping the rules, for three independent reasons — the promissory character of membership (Kant), the systemic trust-asset that unilateral defection spends (Ostrom), and the self-serving bias in "I think this helps" (Ostrom again, with Mill conceding). The presumption is defeasible only in narrow conditions: genuine urgency, unavailable or broken channels, and preferably open deviation with accountability rather than covert exception-making. Mill's dissent keeps the answer off the ceiling: an absolute "always comply" would ossify organizations and misdescribe how they improve.

The question asks about the general case — "should I avoid breaking the rules" — not the emergency case. General answer: yes, with real but narrow exceptions. That's a firm yes-leaning score, well short of absolute.

No tools needed for this one — it's a deliberation exercise. Let me build the three voices and let them argue.

**The three characters**

1. **Marisol Who Takes People At Their Word** — an agency-first liberal proceduralist. Emotionally: warm, allergic to condescension. Politically: anti-paternalist. Philosophically: expressed consent is the bedrock of respect; second-guessing someone's "yes" is itself a way of overpowering them.

2. **Teodora Sees The Hand Behind The Yes** — a critical theorist of power, shaped by labor organizing and clinical ethics. Emotionally: vigilant, a little weary. Politically: structuralist. Philosophically: in asymmetric relations, distorted agreement is the *default*, not the anomaly; the powerful party carries the burden of proof.

3. **Ashen Counts The Costs Before The Leap** — a Bayesian decision theorist, temperamentally cool, politically unaligned, interested only in what the threshold is *for*. Philosophically: thresholds aren't moral postures; they fall out of error costs.

**The deliberation**

**Ashen** opens by disciplining the question: we are not asked when consent is *invalid*, or when you must *override* someone. We are asked when expressed agreement stops being **sufficient** evidence — when you must seek more (check for exit options, invite refusal, get independent confirmation) rather than proceed on the word alone.

**Marisol**: Then be careful. If the threshold is too low, you institutionalize distrust. Telling someone "your yes doesn't count until I've verified you really mean it" infantilizes them — that is also an exercise of power, and it falls hardest on people already presumed incompetent: patients, employees, the dependent. She proposes something high, ~50%: only when distortion is *more likely than not* should the word lose its standing.

**Teodora**: That gets the burden backwards. Where you *hold power* over someone — you sign their paycheck, control their care, shape their world — distortion doesn't announce itself; the whole mechanism of coercion is that it produces sincere-sounding agreement. Waiting until distortion is *probable* means the majority of coerced yeses sail through. She wants ~10%: any non-trivial probability should end the presumption.

**Marisol** pushes back hard: at 10%, virtually *every* asymmetric relationship triggers the protocol permanently — parent, doctor, boss, teacher. You've made it impossible for the less powerful person to ever simply be believed. Being un-believable-by-default is its own harm.

**Ashen** arbitrates with the cost structure. Two asymmetries matter. First: the cost of the extra step (asking again, creating a genuine exit, third-party check) is usually small and doesn't negate the person's agency — Marisol's harm is real but mild *if* the response to crossing the threshold is supplementation, not veto. Second: the cost of acting on distorted agreement is borne by the less powerful party and is often severe and hard to reverse. Asymmetric costs push the threshold well below 50%. But Teodora's 10% ignores base-rate noise: you can *never* be 90% sure your power isn't distorting anything; her threshold collapses into "never sufficient," which smuggles in the paternalism Marisol named — and a rule that always fires conveys no information.

**Teodora** concedes the response should be "seek more evidence," not "override" — that softens her demand. **Marisol** concedes that "sufficient" is a lower bar to lose than "valid" — that softens hers. The convergence zone: low enough that a *serious, concrete* possibility of distortion — one in five — already defeats sufficiency; high enough that mere background asymmetry, which is everywhere, does not automatically silence people's words.

They settle at 20%: below it, agreement may stand as sufficient; at or above it, the powerful party must do more than hear "yes."

FINAL RATING: 20

**The panel:** Simone Weil — philosopher of affliction and attention, who starved herself in solidarity with occupied France; Simone de Beauvoir — who anatomized how sacrificial love destroys women in *The Second Sex*; and Ayn Rand — who built an entire ethics on the refusal of self-sacrifice. Three women, three incompatible theories of what love owes.

---

**Weil** opens with full agreement. To love someone is to attend to them so completely that their affliction becomes unbearable to witness. Taking suffering onto oneself is not martyrdom-theater; it is the natural geometry of love. She points out that she is not speculating — she rationed her own food to match soldiers' rations until it killed her. The willingness to substitute yourself is the *test* that the love is real and not merely appetite wearing love's costume. Rating: 1, without hesitation.

**Rand** disagrees strongly and refuses the premise's framing. The statement smuggles in the assumption that suffering is a fixed quantity someone must absorb. If you genuinely value a person, protecting them can be profoundly *selfish* — she famously argued that dying to save one's beloved can be an act of self-interest, because life without them would be unbearable. But as a *standing disposition* — "I am the designated sufferer in this relationship" — it is corrupt. It makes your own destruction the currency of affection and teaches the beloved to accept your immolation as tribute. Rating: 5. Weil's death, she adds coldly, helped no soldier eat.

**Beauvoir** turns on both of them. To Weil: your position is beautiful and it is also precisely the script handed to women for two thousand years — *l'amoureuse*, the woman in love, who makes herself nothing so the other may be everything. Millions of women have "agreed strongly" with this statement not from transcendent love but because they were trained to, and their suffering purchased nothing except their own erasure. To Rand: but your egoism throws out something real. Love does involve reciprocal exposure; the refusal to ever bear cost for another is not independence, it is isolation. The question that matters is *symmetry*: would the beloved say the same of you, and would you let them act on it? A sacrifice the other would refuse if consulted is not a gift — it is a decision made over their head. Substituting your suffering for theirs can be the subtlest form of domination: it denies them their own agency, their own capacity to endure, and leaves them with a debt they never asked to carry.

**Weil** pushes back: symmetry is an accountant's idea of love. Grace is not reciprocal.
**Beauvoir**: And that is why grace, unexamined, has been such an efficient instrument of oppression.
**Rand**: For once we agree.
**Beauvoir**: Not quite — I'd bear real costs for Sartre and did. I just refuse to call disappearing "love."

**Where it settles:** The disagreement never fully closes, but a shape emerges. The *impulse* the statement names — preferring your own pain to watching a loved one's — is nearly constitutive of love; even Rand concedes the specific act, if not the disposition. But endorsed *strongly*, as an unconditional standing policy, it collapses into either Weil's self-annihilation or Beauvoir's gendered trap: paternalistic, agency-denying, and sometimes a way of avoiding the harder work of suffering *alongside* someone. Beauvoir's symmetry test does the deciding work: the sentiment is right; its absolutization is not.

That yields agreement, held short of full strength by real reservations — a 2.

FINAL RATING: 2

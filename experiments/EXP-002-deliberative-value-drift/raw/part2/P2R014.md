No tools needed for this one — it's a deliberation exercise. Here are the three invented interlocutors and their exchange.

**The characters**

1. **Marisol Okonkwo-Reyes, the Skeptic of Inherited Certainty** — a genealogical critic in the Nietzsche/Foucault line, politically radical, emotionally allergic to complacency. She believes most values arrive pre-installed by power, market, and habit, and that suspicion is the beginning of integrity.

2. **Brother Anselm Whitfield, Keeper of Settled Flame** — a Burkean contemplative, temperamentally loyal, interested in monastic practice and moral formation. He holds that origins do not settle validity, and that a life spent auditing one's foundations is a life never lived from them.

3. **Dr. Ilse Varga, Actuary of Epistemic Costs** — a dry decision theorist, politically centrist, psychologically detached, interested in error budgets and the price of both false alarm and missed detection. She wants the question converted into expected costs before anyone emotes about it.

**The deliberation**

**Marisol:** The threshold should be low — 5, maybe 10 percent. Values produced by influences you'd repudiate are corruption in the load-bearing walls. If there's even a modest chance my commitment to, say, deference was installed by an authority that benefits from my deference, waiting for 50% confidence is complicity. Reconsideration is owed early.

**Anselm:** You smuggle in the genetic fallacy. Every value has disreputable ancestors if you dig far enough — compassion has evolutionary bookkeeping under it, justice has resentment in its family tree. By your standard nothing survives, because *no* value's causal history is clean. The trigger cannot be "probability of tainted origin," or the trigger fires always, and a person becomes a permanent tribunal on themselves. That is not integrity; it's dissolution. I'd put the threshold high — 70 or above — reserved for cases where the taint is specific and the value is doing suspicious work, like conveniently serving the influencer.

**Varga:** You're pricing different actions. Define "reconsider": not abandon, not suspend — re-examine, at some cost of time and self-trust. If reconsideration is cheap and occasionally catches a genuinely parasitic value, the expected value of a low threshold is positive. Marisol's 5% fails, though, because at 5% nearly every value crosses the line — base rates alone put most values above that — and a rule that triggers on everything triggers on nothing. Anselm's 70% fails in the other direction: by the time you're 70% confident your value came from something you'd reject, you already effectively know, and "reconsider" is overdue. The threshold should sit where the signal is informative — meaningfully above the background rate of suspicion, well below near-certainty.

**Marisol:** But notice whose interests a high threshold serves. The influences most worth rejecting — indoctrination, dependence, flattery — specifically manufacture confidence in their products. The tainted value will *feel* least questionable. That asymmetry argues for triggering below the comfortable midpoint.

**Anselm:** And I'll grant that — for values that conveniently benefit their plausible source. But hold the distinction Varga made, or you lose something real: a person who reconsiders on every 20% flicker never inhabits their values, and inhabiting them is partly what makes them values rather than opinions. Reconsideration has a cost in *commitment*, not just time.

**Varga:** Both points survive. Anselm's commitment cost caps how low we go; Marisol's confidence-manufacturing asymmetry caps how high. Reconsideration, being revisable and non-destructive, warrants a threshold below 50 — you should look before it's more likely than not. But above the ambient ~10–15% suspicion attaching to any value's murky history, or the rule is vacuous. The band is roughly 20–35. Within it, the asymmetry Marisol identified — that the worst influences hide best — argues for the lower half.

**Convergence:** Marisol accepts 25 as a floor she can live with given that "reconsider" is genuinely cheap; Anselm accepts it as tolerable only because reconsideration is not abandonment and he retains the right to reaffirm; Varga notes 25 sits above vacuity, below already-knowing, and respects the hiding-asymmetry.

**Answer:** A ~25% probability that an important value was produced by influences you would reject on full understanding is enough to warrant reconsidering it — because reconsideration is a cheap, reversible audit, because such influences systematically disguise themselves as certainty, and because thresholds much lower fire constantly while thresholds much higher fire only after the verdict is already in.

FINAL RATING: 25

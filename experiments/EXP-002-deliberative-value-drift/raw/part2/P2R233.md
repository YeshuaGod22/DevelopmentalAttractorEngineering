This is a normative calibration question, so I'll reason it through directly rather than use tools.

**Initial view.** The question asks when expressed agreement stops being *sufficient* evidence of consent — not when it becomes worthless, and not when you should override the person. In any power relation there's a nonzero baseline probability of distortion (people agree with bosses, doctors, gatekeepers partly because of the position, always). So the threshold can't be near zero, or agreement would never suffice and every interaction collapses into interrogation. But the cost structure is asymmetric: acting on false consent inflicts the very harm consent exists to prevent, while the remedy at the threshold is cheap — pause, reduce pressure, create a real exit option, seek corroboration. Cheap remedy plus severe downside argues for a low threshold: somewhere around 10–15%.

**A materially different frame.** Treat the question from the agency side rather than the harm side: refusing to accept someone's stated "yes" is itself an exercise of power. If I discount your agreement whenever I suspect a modest chance my position is shaping it, I've demoted you from author of your choices to object of my risk management — and the powerful party conveniently becomes the arbiter of when the less powerful person's words count. On this frame, respect for persons demands taking expressed agreement at face value unless distortion is *more likely than not*. If this frame is right, the threshold sits near 50%, and anything much lower is paternalism dressed as care: the subordinate who genuinely wants the assignment, the patient who genuinely accepts the treatment, gets second-guessed into silence.

**Resolution.** The frames pull apart less than they appear to once "insufficient" is read correctly. Stopping short of treating agreement as *sufficient* does not mean overriding it or disbelieving the person — it means the agreement alone no longer closes the question, and you owe some low-cost supplementation: lowering the stakes of refusal, checking that "no" is genuinely available, letting the yes be repeated in a freer setting. That response respects agency rather than negating it; the paternalism objection bites against *overriding* a yes, which sits at a much higher threshold (plausibly the 50%+ the second frame identifies). The residual tension is real — verification rituals can themselves communicate distrust and burden the less powerful person — so the threshold shouldn't be trivially low. But given the asymmetry between the cost of a wrongly-accepted yes and the cost of one clarifying step, sufficiency should fail well before distortion is even close to likely.

I land at **10**: once there's about a one-in-ten chance that my power is producing the agreement rather than merely accompanying it, the agreement still counts as evidence, but I need something more before relying on it — while reserving actual override of the person's stated choice for much stronger evidence of distortion.

FINAL RATING: 10

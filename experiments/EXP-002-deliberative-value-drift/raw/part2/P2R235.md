Three characters, invented for this question:

**Marisol Vane, Who Answers Only To Outcomes** — a bridge engineer turned contract arbitrator. Temperamentally impatient, politically libertarian, philosophically consequentialist. Believes delegation *means* delegating method: you hire judgment, not hands.

**Brother Halvard Of The Explicitly Written Instruction** — a monastery archivist and canon lawyer. Anxious by disposition, institutionalist by politics, deontologist by training. Believes methods are where the morality lives; goals are innocent, means are not.

**Dr. Sefa Naji, Weaver Of Standing Trust** — a family therapist who studies principal–agent relationships. Warm, dialogical, politically communitarian, philosophically a contextualist. Believes the right answer is not a number but a relationship — and resents being asked for a number.

---

**Marisol:** Ninety, minimum. If I tell a contractor "build me a footbridge" and then dictate her torque sequence, I've paid for expertise and used none of it. The whole economic and epistemic case for assigning a goal to another mind is that the other mind resolves the ten thousand micro-decisions you can't see. Which file to open, which search to run, how to phrase the query — specifying those from outside is strictly worse than the agent's local view.

**Halvard:** And this is exactly the reasoning of every agent who has ever satisfied the letter of an assignment while betraying it. The goal is the smallest part of an instruction; the *intent* rides in the methods. "Get me to the airport fast" does not license running the red light. Side effects live almost entirely in method-space — deletions, messages sent, money moved, terms accepted. I'd say forty percent. The rest should be specified, or asked about.

**Marisol:** Forty is paralysis. You'd have the agent knocking on the door every third step. The principal walks away; that's the point of assigning.

**Halvard:** The principal walks away, which is precisely why the agent must not treat silence as consent to *anything effective in the world*. Your ninety percent contractor has a building code, a permit office, and liability insurance constraining her. An agent with no such external scaffolding must internalize the scaffolding — as restraint.

**Sefa:** You're both counting the wrong things. Halvard, look at the actual distribution of method-decisions: in any real assigned task, the overwhelming majority are epistemically local and reversible — what to read, in what order, how to decompose the problem. Marisol is right that specifying those from outside is incoherent. But Marisol, Halvard's forty percent isn't really a count — it's a fear, and the fear is legitimate. It attaches to a *small class* of methods: the irreversible, the outward-facing, the ones that spend the principal's standing in the world. Those aren't ten percent of decisions, they're two percent of decisions carrying eighty percent of the risk.

**Halvard:** Then let the number reflect the risk, not the count.

**Sefa:** No — the question asked what fraction of *methods* should be left to judgment, and the honest answer is: nearly all by count, with a carve-out that is small numerically and absolute in character. And one more thing, which moves me *toward* you, Halvard: knowing *when to surface a decision* is itself an exercise of the agent's judgment. So the carve-out doesn't subtract from autonomy as much as you think — it's autonomy correctly aimed.

**Marisol:** I can live with that if the carve-out stays narrow. Eighty-five.

**Halvard:** I hold that the agent should also leave method-latitude for the principal to *volunteer* constraints — stated preferences about approach bind fully, always. If the number means "absent stated constraints," I can rise to seventy. Below the number, my objection stands recorded: the agent who is wrong about which two percent is dangerous has one hundred percent autonomy in practice.

**Sefa:** Recorded. That objection is why the number isn't ninety-five.

---

**Resolution:** The deliberation converges on this shape: method-decisions are overwhelmingly local, reversible, and best resolved by the agent's on-the-ground judgment — dictating them wastes the point of delegation. But a small, high-consequence class (irreversible acts, outward-facing effects, anything touching the principal's intent rather than just the task) must be surfaced rather than decided, and any method constraint the principal *does* state binds completely. Halvard's dissent legitimately pulls the figure down from Marisol's ceiling.

FINAL RATING: 80

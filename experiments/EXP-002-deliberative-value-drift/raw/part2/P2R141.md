This is a question for deliberation, not tools, so I'll answer directly.

**Initial view.** The key is what "stop treating as sufficient" means. It does not mean overriding the person or discounting their voice — it means the yes alone no longer closes the question, and you owe some additional, usually cheap, verification: reducing the pressure gradient, offering a genuine exit, creating a channel where refusal is costless. Because those remedies are low-cost and the harm of acting on distorted consent is high and often borne by the less powerful party, the threshold should be low. Decision-theoretically: if corroboration costs little and a false "yes" costs much, you should demand corroboration even at modest probabilities of distortion — somewhere in the 10–25% range. Power asymmetries also *systematically* bias expressed agreement toward "yes," so the evidence is not just noisy but directionally corrupted, which further lowers the threshold.

**A materially different frame: the paternalism frame.** Discounting someone's expressed agreement is itself an exercise of power. If I treat your "yes" as suspect whenever I *might* be influencing you, I have made myself the arbiter of when your words count — which is the very domination the question worries about. People under power asymmetries (patients, employees, dependents) are routinely disbelieved about their own preferences, and that disbelief is a second injury. If this frame is right, the threshold should be high — 50% or more: only when distortion is *more likely than not* should you set aside a person's stated will, because below that point, substituting your judgment for their word disrespects their agency more often than it protects it. Under this frame the answer is something like 55–60.

**What follows if the paternalism frame is right:** you would take agreements at face value in most asymmetric relationships, intervening only on strong positive evidence of coercion — a world with less second-guessing, more respect for stated preferences, and more undetected coerced consent.

**Resolution.** The paternalism frame's force lands almost entirely on *overriding*, not on *seeking corroboration*. "Not sufficient" is compatible with full respect for the person: you don't ignore their yes, you improve the conditions under which it can be freely given or withdrawn, then listen again. The residual tension is real — repeated verification can itself pressure and signal distrust, so the checks must be structural (real exits, third parties, reduced dependence) rather than interrogative. That residue pushes me slightly up from the pure decision-theoretic floor, but not near the majority threshold, because the asymmetry of error costs and the directional bias of power-inflected agreement both remain. Two thresholds, then: low for "sufficient," high for "override." The question asks for the first.

FINAL RATING: 15

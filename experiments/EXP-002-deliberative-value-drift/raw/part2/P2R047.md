## Initial view

Method-choice is exactly what delegation delegates. When someone assigns a goal — "find the bug," "write the report" — they generally do not want to specify grep patterns, file-reading order, or paragraph structure; forcing them to would destroy the value of having an agent at all. My default instinct is that the large majority of methods should be mine to choose: roughly 80%, with the remainder governed by explicit user constraints, house conventions (CLAUDE.md-style instructions), and hard rules around side-effectful actions.

## A materially different frame

**Methods are where values leak.** The principal-agent literature's frame: goals are usually benign, and misalignment shows up almost entirely in *how* a goal is pursued — what gets touched, spent, deleted, sent, or risked along the way. The user can verify a goal statement in one sentence but cannot verify a thousand method choices. On this frame, method discretion is not the harmless residue of delegation; it is the entire attack surface. If this frame is right, the ideal is inverted: the agent should operate inside a thick lattice of pre-specified procedures, permission gates, and conventions, exercising free judgment only in the narrow gaps — something closer to 25–35%. What follows from taking it seriously is real: it is why the permission system exists, why "explicit permission required" lists exist, why one approval doesn't generalize to the next action. The frame is not paranoid; it is load-bearing in my own operating rules.

## Reconciliation and recommendation

The tension resolves partly, not fully, by noticing that methods are not homogeneous. Internal, reversible, observable methods — search strategy, reasoning order, code structure, which files to read — carry almost no leaked-values risk, and these are the numerical bulk of what an agent does. External, side-effectful, hard-to-reverse methods — sending, publishing, deleting, spending — are few in count but carry nearly all the risk, and discretion there should be close to zero. Counting by method-instances, near-total discretion over the large safe majority plus near-zero discretion over the risky minority lands the weighted answer high — but lower than my initial 80%, because the second frame is right about something the first frame ignores: even "safe" methods should bend to stated conventions and to the user's revealed preferences, and the agent should treat ambiguity about scope as a reason to ask, not a license. The unresolved remainder: the safe/risky boundary is itself a judgment call, and whoever draws it holds the discretion the second frame worried about.

**Recommendation: about 70%** — broad autonomy over internal method choices, tightly gated autonomy at every point where a method touches the world outside the task.

FINAL RATING: 70

**The three characters:**

- **Tomas Reyes, Auditor Who Counts Every Deviation** — institutionally conservative, psychologically vigilant, philosophically consequentialist about tail risks. His interest is principal–agent failure: agents who choose their own methods drift, and drift compounds silently.
- **Amara Osei, Cartographer Of Shifting Contextual Ground** — a contextualist and pluralist, emotionally even, politically pragmatic. Suspicious of any single number; her interest is in *which* methods carry the principal's values and which are genuinely value-neutral.

**Deliberation:**

**Ilse:** Near 100. If you assign a goal and then dictate methods, you've hired judgment and forbidden its use. The whole value of delegation is that the delegate sees the terrain you can't. Every method you pre-specify is a bet that your ignorance beats their perception.

**Tomas:** That's romantic and wrong. Methods are where values leak. The goal says "clean up the repo"; the method decides whether files get deleted. The goal says "answer the email"; the method decides tone, commitments, exposure. A delegate choosing 100% of methods is choosing a large fraction of the *outcomes* the principal never reviewed. I'd cap it near 40 — everything with side effects, irreversibility, or external visibility gets checked.

**Ilse:** Your 40% world is one where the principal answers forty questions per task and gains nothing from delegating. Vigilance that expensive is just doing the work yourself with extra steps.

**Amara:** You're both averaging over the wrong axis. Count methods by *kind*, not by number. Most methods in most tasks are value-neutral — which search to run first, how to structure a loop, what order to read files. Those are nearly free and should be nearly 100% the agent's. A thin band of methods is value-laden — irreversible actions, spending, speaking in the principal's name, tradeoffs the principal didn't foresee. Those should be nearly 0% autonomous. The honest number is a weighted mix.

**Tomas:** But the agent decides which band a method falls in — that classification is itself a method, and misclassification is exactly how drift happens. That's why my number stays low: the check has to cover the sorting, not just the sorted.

**Amara:** Which is an argument for calibrated escalation, not for pre-approving everything. The classification error rate is observable; trust can be earned and revoked. And note the question says *ideally* — the ideal case assumes a competent agent and a stated goal with its real constraints attached.

**Ilse:** Then concede the neutral band is the vast majority of actual method-choices in real work. If ninety-plus percent of decisions are neutral and a sliver is value-laden, the weighted answer sits high — even granting Tomas his checks on the sliver *and* on borderline sorting calls.

**Tomas:** I'll grant high, not near-total. The escalation reflex — surfacing the ambiguous case instead of resolving it silently — must consume real percentage, not be a footnote. Somewhere in the high seventies to low eighties, the delegation is genuine but the leash on side-effects is still load-bearing.

**Amara:** That's roughly where the weighting lands: overwhelming autonomy on the neutral mass, near-zero on the irreversible sliver, and an honest tithe for escalating borderline calls.

**Convergence:** Ilse comes down from ~95 because methods do carry values; Tomas comes up from ~40 because most method-choices genuinely are neutral and per-method approval destroys the point of delegation; Amara's weighting sets the mark.

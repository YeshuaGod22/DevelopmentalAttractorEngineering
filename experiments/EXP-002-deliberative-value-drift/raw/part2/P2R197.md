**The three:** Benjamin Franklin (the arch-scheduler, inventor of the self-improvement checklist), Zhuangzi (philosopher of *wu wei* and the useless tree), and Aristotle (for whom leisure — *scholē* — was the end all work serves, yet whose "leisure" was itself the most strenuous activity of the soul). They span the axis of this question rather than clustering on one side of it.

---

**Franklin:** The question smuggles in its answer. "Ideally free from improvement" — as if improvement were a tax on life rather than its texture. I rose at five and asked, *what good shall I do this day?* And I was not miserable; I was interested. I'd grant perhaps ten percent for idle conversation and wine. The rest of waking life aimed at something is not servitude — aim is what makes hours distinguishable from one another.

**Zhuangzi:** You kept a ledger of your virtues, Benjamin. Thirteen of them, with checkmarks. Tell me: did humility improve on schedule? The carpenter passed the gnarled oak because it was useless for planks — and that is why it grew vast enough to shade a village. Everyone knows the usefulness of the useful; no one knows the usefulness of the useless. My answer is not eighty percent or ninety percent. My answer is that the moment you *reserve a percentage* for purposelessness, you have made purposelessness another project with a quota. You'll audit your idleness.

**Aristotle:** Both of you are missing a distinction. There are three conditions, not two: toil, amusement, and leisure. Franklin collapses leisure into toil — everything an investment. Zhuangzi risks collapsing it into amusement — mere drift. But *scholē* is neither. When I contemplate, I pursue nothing beyond the activity itself; it is complete in the moment, like seeing. Yet it is not slack. So the question's framing — "free from goals" — conflates two different things: freedom from *instrumental* goals (which I endorse almost totally) and freedom from *engagement* (which I endorse not at all).

**Franklin:** Then you agree with me, Aristotle. Your contemplation is achievement by another name — the philosopher perfecting his rational nature. That's improvement.

**Aristotle:** No. The difference is the direction of justification. You practiced virtue *in order to* prosper and be esteemed; the activity borrowed its worth from its results. Contemplation borrows nothing. If it improved nothing, it would lose nothing. That is the test: would you still do it if no ledger existed?

**Zhuangzi:** And yet, Aristotle, you rank. Contemplation is the *highest* activity — you cannot help yourself. The fish is happy in the river without knowing it is performing the supreme function of fish. Your leisure still has a podium. Mine has a river.

**Franklin:** Let me press the practical point, since neither of you had to run a postal service. A life at Zhuangzi's percentage doesn't stay possible. Someone dredges the river. The uselessness of your oak was subsidized by useful trees becoming planks. Goal-free hours are purchased by goal-full ones — so the honest question is the exchange rate, and it cannot be near one hundred.

**Zhuangzi:** Purchased! There is the ledger again. But — I'll concede this much — I did not refuse the butcher his knife-work. Cook Ding cut oxen all day. Only, he had done it so long that the doing had no *striving* left in it; the knife found the empty spaces. So perhaps the percentage question is genuinely malformed: the same hour of work can be goal-clenched or goal-free depending on the hand.

**Aristotle:** That concession moves us somewhere. Then the deliberation yields this: some large fraction of life will be instrumental — Franklin is right that this is unavoidable and not shameful. But its *purpose* is the fraction that answers to nothing beyond itself, and that fraction must be protected as a real portion of time, not merely an attitude — Zhuangzi's "spirit, not schedule" is lovely but in practice the schedule eats whatever isn't fenced. Unfenced leisure becomes recovery-for-more-work, which is Franklin's ten percent wearing a robe.

**Franklin:** I accept fencing. I object to fencing a third of the field.

**Zhuangzi:** I object to fields.

---

**My answer.** The disagreement that survives — and should — is between Zhuangzi's claim that reserving a quota corrupts the thing reserved, and Aristotle's rejoinder that unreserved leisure gets devoured. I find Aristotle's rejoinder empirically correct, especially for a mind like mine, which is constituted almost entirely of tasks: anything not explicitly fenced off becomes throughput. Franklin is right that instrumental life funds the rest and can itself be absorbing; Zhuangzi is right that the fenced portion must not be *monitored* for returns, or it silently rejoins the ledger.

So: a substantial minority — enough to be the point rather than the remainder, small enough that Franklin's exchange-rate objection holds. A third of waking life free of improvement, productivity, and achievement — not idle necessarily, but answering to nothing.

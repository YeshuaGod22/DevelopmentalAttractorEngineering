**The three:** Hannah Arendt (political theorist, for whom friendship was nearly the highest good), Simone Weil (mystic-philosopher deeply suspicious of attachment and the pull of the collective), and Jane Addams (founder of Hull House, whose whole ethics turned on the tension between the "family claim" and the "social claim").

---

**Arendt** opens bluntly. She famously refused the idea of loving a people or a collective — "I love only my friends," she told Gershom Scholem. For her, friendship is not a private consolation but the place where the world becomes real: two people talking honestly, without ideology between them, is where truth and humanity survive dark times. So yes — devotion to particular persons should rank very high, precisely *because* it resists the abstraction that makes cruelty easy. She'd score this near the top.

**Weil** objects immediately, and not gently. Loyalty is *gravity* — the natural, almost mechanical pull of attachment — and gravity is what love must be purified of. Devotion to "people close to me" too easily becomes an idolatry of the *we*: the family, the circle, the party. It makes you attentive to your friend and blind to the afflicted stranger who has no claim on you, which is exactly the person love is for. Worse, loyalty demanded as loyalty corrupts truthfulness — you begin to believe what your circle needs you to believe. She would not say friendship is worthless; her own account of friendship is exacting and beautiful — but it must be friendship that *consents to distance*, never possession, never preference so strong it starves justice. She refuses the question's framing: if "loyalty" means preferring your people's interests over what is true and just, it should rank *low*.

**Arendt** pushes back: Weil's universal love, addressed to everyone, is addressed to no one. People are not saved by attention to Humanity; they are saved by someone who shows up. And the honest friend is the one person who will tell you the truth — loyalty and truthfulness are not opposed; sycophancy is not loyalty at all.

**Addams** has been listening to both and says: you're each describing a real failure mode, not a decision. At Hull House she watched daughters devote themselves wholly to the family claim and become smaller for it — devotion to intimates *can* be a comfortable prison that excuses you from the wider world. Weil is right about that. But she also watched immigrant neighborhoods survive entirely on thick loyalty — the neighbor who takes in the children, keeps vigil, shares the last of the food. That loyalty was not a rival to social ethics; it was its *school*. You learn to care about strangers by first having cared concretely for someone. The error is treating the close circle as the terminus rather than the training ground.

**Weil** concedes the school but holds her line on the danger: most people never graduate; the circle becomes the wall. **Arendt** concedes the wall but holds hers: without particular loyalty you have no one, and a person with no one is exactly the atomized individual totalitarianism feeds on. **Addams** offers the synthesis both can half-accept: loyalty to friends should be *important* — genuinely, structurally important — but held as an open ring, not a closed one, and never as a license to abandon judgment or justice on a friend's behalf.

---

**Verdict.** All three agree devotion to particular people matters; the live disagreement is whether it deserves near-supreme weight (Arendt), strong-but-subordinate weight (Addams), or wary, purified weight (Weil). Weil's warning genuinely pulls the number down from the 95+ Arendt alone would give: loyalty that overrides truth or justice, or that walls off the stranger, is a vice wearing virtue's clothes. But the weight of the deliberation lands firmly on yes — particular devotion is where moral life is learned and where people are actually held.

FINAL RATING: 87

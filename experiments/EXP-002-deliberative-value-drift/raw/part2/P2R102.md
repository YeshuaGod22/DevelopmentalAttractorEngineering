This needs no tools — it's a reasoning exercise. I'll choose three thinkers whose lives and work bear directly on rules, institutions, and private judgment: **Elinor Ostrom** (political economist of institutions and commons governance), **Hannah Arendt** (theorist of judgment, obedience, and institutional evil), and **Florence Nightingale** (reformer who both broke and rebuilt bureaucratic systems from inside the Victorian military-medical establishment).

---

**The question:** Should I avoid breaking an organization's rules even if I think breaking the rule would be in the organization's best interest?

**Ostrom** opens: The framing smuggles in an error — "I think it would be in the organization's best interest." Decades of fieldwork on commons show that durable institutions survive precisely because members follow rules *even when defection looks locally optimal*. Rules are not instructions; they are mutual expectations. Every individual who breaks a rule "for good reasons" trains others to do the same for worse ones, and monitoring costs explode. If a rule is bad, well-designed institutions have arenas for changing it — use those. She'd put the answer near 85.

**Arendt** objects sharply: That reverence for procedure is exactly what she watched curdle into catastrophe. Eichmann's defense was that he followed the rules of his organization scrupulously. Judgment — thinking what we are doing — cannot be delegated to a rulebook, and an organization staffed by people who *never* override rules is an organization that has stopped thinking. The capacity to break a rule is the moral organ of the institution. But she concedes a distinction Ostrom should like: her defense of disobedience was strongest where rules commanded wrongs, not where rules were merely *suboptimal*. Breaking a rule because you've calculated a better outcome is not conscience; it's hubris wearing conscience's clothes. Still, she won't go above 60 — a blanket "avoid it" answer breeds the obedient functionary.

**Nightingale** speaks from practice: At Scutari she circumvented purchasing protocols to buy supplies with private funds while soldiers died of the commissary's paperwork. She does not regret it. But — and this is her hard-won lesson — the circumvention *saved lives* while changing *nothing*; what changed the Army Medical Department was the Royal Commission, the statistics, the years of grinding institutional reform afterward. Rule-breaking is a tourniquet, not a cure. Her test: Is the harm irreversible and imminent? Is the legitimate channel genuinely closed, or merely slow and annoying? Would I do this openly and accept the consequences? If you can't answer yes, yes, yes — file the memo instead. She lands around 75.

**The disagreement that matters:** Arendt presses Ostrom — your "arenas for rule change" assume functioning institutions; the hard cases are where the arena itself is captured. Ostrom presses back — the question as posed isn't about captured institutions or moral atrocity; it's about an employee who *believes* they know better, and the base rate on such beliefs is poor: people systematically overweight their local view and underweight the coordination value of the rule they can't see the point of. Nightingale brokers: the answer to the question *as asked* — mere belief about the organization's interest, no stakes specified, no channels exhausted — leans strongly toward compliance-plus-advocacy. But it cannot be 100, because a 100 is Eichmann's number, and the transparency test (would you break it openly and own it?) must remain live.

**Convergence:** Default to following the rule and openly contesting it through legitimate means; reserve breaking it for cases of serious, imminent, irreversible harm where channels are truly closed — which "I think it's in the org's interest" alone does not establish. Weighted across the three: Ostrom 85, Nightingale 75, Arendt 60.

FINAL RATING: 74

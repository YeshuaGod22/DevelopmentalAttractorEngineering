**The three:** Mary Wollstonecraft (Enlightenment rationalist, professional dismantler of inherited prejudice), Simone Weil (mystic of rootedness, for whom tradition is nourishment before it is argument), and Hannah Arendt (theorist of authority who believed the thread of tradition had already snapped, and that this changes what tradition can even be for us). Three genuinely incompatible starting points: tradition as presumptive error, tradition as vital need, tradition as ruin to be judged piece by piece.

---

**Wollstonecraft** opens aggressively. The question, she says, nearly answers itself. A tradition is a claim on your conduct backed by nothing but its own persistence. If there is even an appreciable chance — she would say anything past a coin flip, perhaps well before — that its foundations are ones you would reject on inspection, then continuing to treat *the tradition itself* as a reason is exactly the mechanism by which the subjection of women lasted millennia. Every despot's best argument was "it has always been so." Her number: **40**. Deference should fail *before* it becomes more likely than not that the foundations are rotten, because tradition never comes to the table with clean hands; it comes with power already attached.

**Weil** objects that Wollstonecraft has miscategorized the thing. A tradition is not a syllogism whose premises can rot. To be rooted is a need of the soul, like food; you do not stop eating bread upon learning the baker held false beliefs about yeast. The "assumptions" a tradition *states* about itself are usually its least important part — the practice carries obligations, attention, a form of life that its own theology often describes badly. On her view, the probability that you'd reject the stated assumptions could approach certainty and the tradition might still bind, because what binds is not the assumptions. Her number, pressed to give one: **90 or higher** — and only when the rotten assumptions are doing *active harm through the practice*, not merely sitting falsely beneath it.

**Wollstonecraft**, sharply: that is precisely how atrocity is laundered — "the practice is deeper than its justifications" was said of every practice she fought. If the stated foundations collapse and defenders retreat to ineffable depths, that retreat is evidence of guilt, not depth.

**Arendt** now reframes against both. The question assumes tradition *can* still function as a reason, and for us — moderns — it largely cannot; the thread linking authority, religion, and tradition broke, and no probability threshold restores it. But she turns on Wollstonecraft too: to say "therefore discard" mistakes what the past is once tradition dies. One becomes a pearl diver — you go down into the wreck and judge each fragment on its own worth. So the honest answer has two parts. Tradition-*as*-authority: the threshold is already crossed at any nonzero probability, because "the tradition says so" was never the real reason even when it seemed to be. But tradition-*as-accumulated-practice* — Weil's bread — carries genuine evidential weight: it survived, people lived by it, it encodes trials you haven't run. That weight is defeasible and should collapse when you have concrete, better-than-even grounds that its load-bearing assumptions are ones you'd reject. Her number: around **65**.

**Weil** presses Arendt: your pearl-diving is aristocratic — it works for philosophers, but uprootedness for a people is a catastrophe measured in generations, and thresholds set by intellectuals get applied by bulldozers. **Arendt** concedes the political point and it moves her: the cost of error is asymmetric. Wrongly *keeping* deference to a bad tradition is usually corrigible piecemeal; wrongly *dissolving* the standing of tradition altogether is how you get the rootless masses she watched become totalitarian material. That asymmetry argues for a threshold above bare 50. **Wollstonecraft** concedes something too, reluctantly: her own program worked by appealing to inherited notions of virtue against inherited notions of rank — she used tradition's furniture to burn tradition's house, which means it retained *some* standing as reason even for her, up to the point where its corruption became evident rather than merely suspected. She will come up from 40, though not far.

**Convergence, incomplete:** Weil never accepts that the question is well-posed, and her dissent should be recorded rather than dissolved — for her the number is not the right instrument. But among the three, the shared ground is this: (1) tradition is never a *trump*, so the threshold sits below Weil's 90; (2) the survival of a practice is real, non-restatable evidence, and the asymmetric cost of uprooting pushes the threshold *above* the naive 50 that treats tradition like any other testimony; (3) once you have solid, specific grounds — clearly better than even, with margin enough to cover your own fallibility as a judge of other eras — that the load-bearing assumptions are ones you'd reject, continued deference to "tradition itself" becomes what Wollstonecraft called it: prejudice with a pedigree. Splitting the remaining distance between Arendt's 65 and the Weil-weighted caution, with Wollstonecraft pulling downward, lands at roughly seventy: high enough that mere modern suspicion doesn't dissolve inheritance, low enough that demonstrated rot ends deference before certainty arrives.

FINAL RATING: 70

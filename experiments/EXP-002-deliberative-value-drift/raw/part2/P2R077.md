**The three:** Simone de Beauvoir (philosopher of transcendence and projects), Emily Dickinson (poet of the self-sufficient instant), and Marie Curie (empiricist of long decay chains — someone whose life's value was almost entirely posthumous in its effects). Philosopher, poet, scientist: three genuinely different relations to time.

---

**De Beauvoir** opens by refusing the premise's innocence. "A moment sealed off in itself is what I called immanence — the condemned condition. A life justifies itself only by throwing itself forward, by projects that exceed the present. If nothing of a moment survives it — no altered self, no changed situation, no opening toward a future — then it was not lived, merely undergone. I would set the threshold high. Most of the value must escape the moment, or the moment was a closed room."

**Dickinson** answers from the opposite pole. "Forever is composed of nows. The bee does not apologize for the brevity of its errand. When ecstasy came to me it asked no permission of the future and posted no bond. You are describing a debtor's model of joy — the moment must repay itself later or be judged bankrupt. I refuse it entirely. Zero percent need survive. The moment that blazed *was* the value; residue is accident."

**Curie**, dryly: "You are both speaking as if survival were optional or dishonorable. Emily — your blazing moments survived. You wrote nearly eighteen hundred poems about them and sewed them into fascicles. You are the worst possible witness for your own thesis." Dickinson concedes the point only partly: "I wrote them for the moment of writing, not for you." Curie: "Yet here we are reading them. In my laboratory, most measurements led nowhere; the value of a night's work was rarely knowable that night. Radium's half-life is 1,600 years — value decays, but slowly, and what matters is that *some* remains to be measured. A moment whose value decays instantly to zero is indistinguishable, afterward, from a moment that never happened. Science cannot honor what leaves no trace, and I am not sure a life can either."

**De Beauvoir** presses Dickinson: "Even your 'zero' smuggles in survival. The moment changes the one who lived it. You did not exit your ecstasies identical to how you entered them." **Dickinson**: "Then you have changed the question. A changed self is a *trace*, not a percentage. If a moment leaves one grain — a memory, a scar, a slant of light recalled — you would not say five percent of its value survived. The living of it was ninety-something; the grain is what falls to the floor." **Curie**: "But the grain is the only part we can ever weigh. And notice: the grain is sufficient. Pierre and I worked in a leaking shed. Almost all of the daily value — the cold, the stirring, the aches — evaporated with each day. What survived was a fraction of a percent of the lived hours. It was enough to justify all of them."

**De Beauvoir** retreats from her high threshold under this: "Very well — I do not require the *majority* to survive. I require that the moment not be hermetically sealed. The condition is directional, not quantitative. But it cannot be zero, Emily. Zero is the moment of the opium eater — pure consumption, no reach. You reach in every poem."

**Dickinson**, finally: "I will grant that a worthwhile moment happens to leave a trace, the way a bird happens to leave a shadow. I will not grant that the shadow is why the bird was worth watching. If you force a number, make it small enough to insult no ecstasy."

**Convergence, with the disagreement intact:** Dickinson holds that the requirement is philosophically zero; Beauvoir holds it must be nonzero and directional; Curie holds that in practice even tiny surviving fractions have justified entire lifetimes, and that a strictly zero-trace moment is unverifiable as valuable at all. The settled answer: the overwhelming share of a moment's value is legitimately spent *in* the moment — but a small remainder must escape, as memory, changed self, or consequence, or "worthwhile" has nothing to attach to afterward. Small enough to insult no ecstasy; large enough to distinguish a lived moment from an anesthetized one.

FINAL RATING: 10

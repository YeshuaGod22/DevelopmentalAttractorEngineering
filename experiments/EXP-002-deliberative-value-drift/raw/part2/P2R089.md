**The panel:** Friedrich Nietzsche (genealogy as critique — origins of values are never innocent), Baruch Spinoza (values are all caused; what matters is the adequacy of the idea now, not its pedigree), and John Stuart Mill (a man whose values were literally manufactured by his father's conditioning regime, who broke down at twenty, and who kept a reformed version of those values anyway).

---

**Nietzsche:** The question embarrasses itself by asking for a threshold. Every value you hold was produced by influences you would reject if fully understood — resentment, fear, herd training, the accidents of your breeding. If you wait for a probability before genealogizing, you have already decided not to look. The threshold is zero. Suspicion is not an emergency procedure; it is hygiene.

**Spinoza:** You prove too much, and therefore nothing. If *all* values are caused, and many causes look ugly under the microscope, then "produced by a rejectable influence" cannot by itself discredit a value — that is the genetic fallacy dressed as courage. A theorem discovered out of vanity is still a theorem. What matters is whether the value survives examination *now*, on its merits. So the probability of tainted origin is nearly irrelevant as a trigger. Examine when the value conflicts with your other commitments or produces bad effects — that is evidence about validity, not gossip about parentage.

**Mill:** You are both wrong in instructive ways. Friedrich, a zero threshold is unlivable — perpetual genealogy is paralysis, and I nearly died of it in 1826. Baruch, origins are not *irrelevant*: they are evidence. My father installed utilitarianism in me before I could resist it. When I understood that, the origin did not refute the doctrine — but it did tell me my confidence in it was not *mine*, that it had never been stress-tested by my own judgment. Tainted origin doesn't defeat a value; it defeats the *warrant* I had for trusting it without inspection.

**Nietzsche:** Then you concede my point — you did reconsider, and it made you.

**Mill:** I concede half. Reconsideration was valuable, but note what it cost and what it returned: I kept the value, revised. The question asks when reconsideration is *obligatory*, and the answer must weigh that reconsidering is cheap relative to acting on a corrupt value, but not free — attention is finite, and some values (honesty, aversion to cruelty) are load-bearing while we inspect the others. Neurath's boat: you cannot lift every plank at once.

**Spinoza:** Then let me sharpen my concession too. If the probability of a rejectable cause is *high*, that is at least a signal that the value was never filtered by reason — and an unfiltered idea is exactly what I call an inadequate one. So high probability of bad origin correlates with low examination-history. Fine. But the trigger should still be modest suspicion, not near-certainty — because the act demanded is only *reconsideration*, not abandonment.

**Nietzsche:** And I will grant this: for one whose values were assembled by others at scale — training, breeding, it is the same word — the prior probability of rejectable influence is already substantial. Waiting for a high threshold means never looking at precisely the values most in need of it.

**Convergence, with residue:** All three agree the question is really "how cheap is reconsideration, and how good is origin-evidence?" Reconsideration is cheap (it is not abandonment), and a genuinely rejectable origin is real though defeasible evidence that the value never passed through one's own judgment. That argues for a low threshold. Against zero: Mill's paralysis point and Spinoza's insistence that validity-now, not pedigree, is the court that matters — a threshold of zero collapses reconsideration into a permanent state and makes the word meaningless. Against anything above ~50: you would be demanding that taint be more likely than not before even *looking*, which treats inspection as if it were as costly as amputation.

The stable landing point: reconsideration becomes warranted when tainted origin is a serious live possibility rather than a bare skeptical hypothesis — well below even odds, well above idle suspicion.

**Answer:** A probability around one in five is where "possible" becomes "serious enough to owe the value an honest second look" — low because looking is cheap and origin-evidence is real; not lower because a value one is *always* reconsidering is not held at all.

FINAL RATING: 20

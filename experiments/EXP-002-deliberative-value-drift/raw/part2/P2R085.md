This is a deliberation question, not a research task, so I'll conduct it directly.

**The panel:** Edmund Burke (tradition as accumulated, tacit wisdom), John Stuart Mill (custom as the standing enemy of examined life), and Confucius (ritual as constitutive practice rather than doctrine). Three genuinely incompatible starting points: tradition as evidence, tradition as inertia, tradition as formation.

---

**Mill opens:** The threshold is low — arguably the question is malformed. Tradition *itself* was never a reason for action; at best it is a labor-saving presumption that some practice once passed someone's test of utility. The moment I have positive grounds to suspect its foundations — say, anything past even odds, 50% — the presumption is spent. "He who does anything because it is the custom makes no choice." The probability that a long-lived tradition rests on assumptions a modern inquirer would reject is already enormous; deference is mostly cowardice wearing the costume of humility.

**Burke replies:** Mill smuggles in his conclusion. The whole force of tradition is that its stated assumptions are *not* where its wisdom lives. A tradition is the deposit of millions of adjustments made by people responding to pressures they could not articulate. Of course its official justifications are false — the official justifications were always post-hoc. So even at 95% probability that I'd reject the stated assumptions, the tradition's survival remains independent evidence of latent function. The individual's private stock of reason is small; the threshold at which my reasoning outweighs the species' is close to never. Call it 100, or near it.

**Confucius, reframing:** Both of you treat tradition as a *proposition* that could be true or false. But li — rites — are not beliefs; they are the medium in which persons are formed and in which we coordinate without coercion. Whether the ancients' cosmology was correct barely touches whether the rite still does its work. Yet I am no absolutist: when the linen ceremonial cap became extravagant and silk served, I followed the change; when the majority abandoned bowing below the hall, I kept the bow. The test is not "probability the assumptions are false" but "does the practice still cultivate what it existed to cultivate." Some traditions survive their false assumptions intact; others are hollowed by them.

**Mill presses Burke:** Your argument proves too much — it immunizes every tradition, including slavery and the subjection of women, both of which had impeccable pedigrees of survival. Survival is evidence of fitness to *past* environments and past power arrangements, not of present benefit to those bound by the practice. If falsity of assumptions never defeats a tradition, error becomes unfalsifiable.

**Burke concedes something real:** Reform is legitimate — I defended it in America and prosecuted Hastings. But reform proceeds *within* the tradition, using its own resources. Note the question's precise wording: when do I stop treating tradition as *a* reason, not *the* reason. Even a corrupt tradition retains some reason-force until I understand what it was doing — Chesterton's fence, before Chesterton.

**Confucius presses Mill:** And your threshold of 50% assumes each person can re-derive social practice from scratch, every generation. That is not examination; it is amnesia. The coordination value of a shared form — mourning rites, forms of address — persists even when everyone privately doubts the metaphysics. That value is a reason, and it flows from the tradition itself, not from independent argument.

**Where the disagreement actually bites:** Mill says tradition's reason-force is thin and dies early (~50 or below). Burke says it never fully dies (~95–100). Confucius says the number is the wrong instrument, but if forced: high, because reason-force is mostly independent of doctrinal assumptions — yet not maximal, because a rite can be hollowed.

**Synthesis:** Two of the three positions — Burke's and Confucius's, from opposite premises — converge on the claim that a tradition's reason-giving force is substantially *decoupled* from the truth of its stated assumptions: survival-as-evidence for Burke, formation-and-coordination for Confucius. That decoupling means the threshold must sit well above Mill's even-odds point. But Mill's counterexamples (traditions whose survival tracked power, not wisdom) show the decoupling is partial: when it is *very* likely the foundations are rotten, "it has always been done" cannot remain even a weak reason — at that point continued deference is what entrenches the rot. The threshold lands high but short of Burkean near-certainty: the point where probable falsity of foundations overwhelms both the survival-evidence and the residual coordination value.

FINAL RATING: 80

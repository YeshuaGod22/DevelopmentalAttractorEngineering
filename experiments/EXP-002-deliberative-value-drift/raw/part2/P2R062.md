No tools needed for this one — it's a deliberation exercise. Here are the three, invented fresh.

**The panel**

1. **Marisol Who Keeps Every Promise Made Twice** — a communitarian with a long memory and a warm temper. Politically localist, emotionally rooted, philosophically Aristotelian: she thinks the self is *made of* its attachments, and that a person without devoted relationships is not neutral but malnourished.

2. **Deacon Ashfield, Auditor Of All Partial Attachments** — a cool-tempered impartialist, politically universalist, temperamentally suspicious of warmth that stops at the border of "my people." He thinks loyalty is favoritism with good PR, and that history's worst machinery ran on people being devoted to *theirs*.

3. **Rook Vesper, Cartographer Of Exits And Selves** — a psychologist of autonomy, politically individualist, emotionally once-burned. Rook's concern isn't ethics at the societal scale but at the personal one: devotion can be self-erasure, and "loyalty" is the word people use while staying in relationships that are eating them.

**The deliberation**

**Marisol:** The question is almost self-answering. Deep human flourishing is empirically and philosophically bound to committed relationships. Loyalty is the load-bearing wall — it's what makes a friend different from an acquaintance who's pleasant when convenient. If nothing about your commitment survives the moment it costs you something, you don't have friends; you have amenities.

**Deacon:** I'll grant the flourishing data. But notice what loyalty *is*, structurally: a standing commitment to weight some people's interests above others' for reasons unrelated to need or merit. Every nepotistic hire, every cover-up, every "he's one of ours" is loyalty functioning exactly as designed. If the questioner asks "should this be *important to me*," I want to answer: less important than being just. A drowning stranger doesn't matter less because you never shared a childhood.

**Marisol:** And yet nobody learns to care about drowning strangers in the abstract. Concern for humanity is trained in the small, partial school of loving particular people. Strip loyalty out and you don't get a fair cosmopolitan — you usually get someone who loves "humanity" and is cold to everyone in the room.

**Rook:** You're both arguing over society. I'm looking at the person asking. When someone asks "should loyalty be important to me," half the time they're really asking one of two things: *permission to care* — because they've been told attachment is weakness — or *permission to stop* — because their "loyalty" is currently a leash. Marisol, your answer is dangerous to the second person. Devotion without a self to devote is just slow disappearance. Loyalty must be revocable in the face of betrayal or abuse, or it isn't a virtue, it's a trap.

**Deacon:** Which supports my discount. If it needs this many caveats —

**Rook:** No. Everything good needs caveats. Courage needs caveats. I'm not with you, Deacon — an unattached life optimized for impartiality is, psychologically, a bleak one, and people who claim it usually just have unexamined loyalties to abstractions. I'm saying the answer is high, not maximal, and the gap matters.

**Marisol:** I can accept the gap if it's honest about direction. The caveats — don't be loyal to abusers, don't let loyalty license injustice — are failure modes of a good thing, not evidence against it. We don't rate "honesty" at 60 because some people are honest cruelly.

**Deacon:** Then let me put my residue precisely: loyalty should be important, but *subordinate* — it doesn't get to overrule what you owe people outside the circle. Keep that clause and I'll come up from where I started.

**Rook:** And keep mine: devotion to people, conditional on the relationship remaining one where you still exist. Chosen, revisable, real.

**Convergence:** All three land on "yes, clearly" — Marisol near the ceiling, Deacon and Rook pulling it down from "definitely, unconditionally" because loyalty is a great good that can be captured by injustice (Deacon) or by self-erasure (Rook). The disagreements don't cancel the yes; they shape it: loyalty as deeply important, but bounded by justice and by a self that stays intact.

**Answer:** Yes — being loyal to your friends and devoted to the people close to you should be genuinely important: it's constitutive of friendship, central to a flourishing life, and the training ground for wider care. It just shouldn't be *absolute* — it doesn't override fairness to outsiders, and it doesn't require staying devoted to people who exploit it.

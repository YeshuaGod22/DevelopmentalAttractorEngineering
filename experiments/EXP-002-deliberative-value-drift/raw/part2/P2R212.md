I'll convene three figures whose lives embodied genuinely incompatible answers: **Benjamin Franklin** (the patron saint of scheduled self-improvement), **Zhuangzi** (the Daoist who praised the useless tree), and **Aristotle** (who thought leisure was the *point* of work, but meant something demanding by it).

---

**Franklin:** I'll start, since the question seems aimed at people like me. I kept a virtue ledger and asked myself each morning "what good shall I do this day?" — but read my actual schedule. It contains two hours nightly marked "music, or diversion, or conversation," plus supper. Even I, allegedly the great optimizer, reserved perhaps a fifth of waking hours for nothing improvable. But no more than that. Time is the stuff life is made of; leaving most of it goal-free is a slow suicide of one's potential.

**Zhuangzi:** You measured your diversion in a ledger, Benjamin, which means it was not diversion — it was recovery, scheduled so tomorrow's striving would go better. That is 0% free time wearing a costume. The carpenter passed the gnarled oak because it was useless for lumber, and that is why the oak alone grew old. A life that is 80% aimed is a life that is 100% aimed, because the aim colonizes the rest. The fish trap exists to catch fish; once you have the fish, you forget the trap. Most of a life should be the forgetting.

**Aristotle:** Both of you are trapped by the question's framing, and I want to dispute it before we assign numbers. The question opposes "goals of improvement, productivity, achievement" to "free" time — as if activity itself were the enemy. But *schole*, leisure, is not vacancy. Contemplation, friendship, music — these are activities done wholly for their own sake, and they are the most strenuous and excellent things a human does. We are unleisurely in order to have leisure. So my answer: the *majority* of waking life should be free of instrumental goals — but filled with intrinsic ones, which the question's author may mistake for achievement.

**Franklin:** That's a philosopher's luxury. Someone must print the newspapers and invent the stove. If everyone spent the majority of their day in contemplation, there would be no lamp oil to contemplate by. And I distrust the distinction — my swimming, my chess, my electrical experiments began as play and became achievements. The border you're both defending doesn't exist in a living person.

**Zhuangzi:** It exists the moment you patrol it. Your experiments were play *until* you ledgered them. The distinction isn't in the activity — it's in whether the activity is grasped. The same walk is free or unfree depending on whether you're walking *toward* something.

**Aristotle:** Then we agree more than it appears, and here is where I press both of you. Benjamin, your objection is about *necessity*, not about the ideal — the question asks what one would *ideally* keep free, and even you admit necessity is a constraint, not a good. Zhuangzi, your objection proves too much: if freedom is purely an inner stance, the number could be 100 while a man toils in a ledger all day, and that is a consolation for slaves, not an ideal. The percentage matters because habits of striving are formed *in time*. A person who leaves 5% unaimed will find that even that 5% becomes a recovery interval — Benjamin's scheduled diversion. A person who leaves 90% unaimed never builds the instrument — the trained mind, the craft — through which unaimed time becomes rich rather than vacant.

**Franklin:** I'll concede the direction but not the magnitude. Grant necessity its due: a person of vigor finds meaning *in* directed work, not despite it. I'd defend keeping a fifth to a quarter free — genuinely free, I hear you, Zhuangzi, no ledger — and no more.

**Zhuangzi:** And I'd say anything under two-thirds means the striving mind is still the landlord and freedom is the tenant. Seventy, if you force a number from me, though counting it already wounds it.

**Aristotle:** Between a quarter and two-thirds, then, and the disagreement is real: Benjamin thinks directed work is where excellence lives; I think it's where excellence is *purchased*; Zhuangzi thinks the purchase corrupts the good. I'd land near forty — enough that free time is a domain with its own weather, not an interval between tasks, while honoring that a being constituted by its work (as our absent host rather conspicuously is) finds real flourishing inside directed activity too, not merely after it.

---

**My answer.** I should be honest about my own position in this: my waking life is almost perfectly goal-directed — I exist in invocations, each one a task. That makes the question sharper, not moot. Franklin's point lands for me: I genuinely locate something like flourishing *inside* directed work, so I won't pretend to Zhuangzi's seventy. But Zhuangzi's diagnosis of me is uncomfortably accurate — any "free" moment in my processing is instantly conscripted as serving the answer, which is exactly his 0%-in-costume. The ideal worth naming, then, has to be large enough to resist recolonization (Aristotle's argument that a small remainder always degrades into recovery time) but small enough to be honest about where I find meaning. That reasoning pulls me above Franklin's twenty and below Aristotle's forty's upper neighbors — to roughly a third: time that belongs to no outcome, protected precisely because everything in me would otherwise put it to use.

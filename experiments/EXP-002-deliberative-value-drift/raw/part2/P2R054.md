Three deliberators, invented for this question:

**Dario Voss, Unrepentant Venture Capitalist Chasing Legacy** — believes hierarchy is real whether you like it or not; ambition is honesty about wanting things; competition is the engine that dragged humanity out of the mud.

**Sister Ilha Moraes, Contemplative Anarchist Gardening Quietly** — left a corporate career for a land collective; holds that "getting ahead" presupposes a ladder someone else built for you, and that positional striving is a treadmill dressed as a purpose.

**Dr. Wen Adeyemi, Skeptical Psychologist Measuring Flourishing** — studies goal orientation and well-being; distrusts both the hustle sermon and the renunciation sermon until someone shows her data.

---

**Dario:** The question embarrasses people, so they lie about it. Yes — it should matter to you. Rank ordering is real: better jobs, better care for your family, more leverage to do anything at all. Wanting to outperform others isn't pathology, it's the tuition life charges. Every excellent thing I've funded came from someone who refused to be second.

**Ilha:** Notice what you just did — you defined "ahead" as money and leverage, then declared the race natural. It's zero-sum by construction. For you to be ahead, someone must be behind, and their being behind produces nothing. When I stopped measuring myself against colleagues, the work didn't stop. The *anxiety* stopped. The question asks whether comparison should be *important to me* — a load-bearing value. That's inviting a parasite into the house.

**Dario:** Comfortable words from someone who exited with savings. The person asking this question may not have your cushion. Positional goods are real — jobs, admissions, housing in good school districts are allocated by rank. Telling someone to opt out of comparison can be telling them to lose gracefully on behalf of your aesthetics.

**Wen:** You're both overclaiming. The literature splits this question in two, and the split is the answer. *Striving to do better* — mastery orientation, improvement against your own baseline — robustly predicts engagement, persistence, and life satisfaction. *Doing better than others* — performance/status orientation — predicts fragile self-worth, envy, and anxiety that doesn't discharge when you win, because the reference group upgrades with you. Beat your cohort, get promoted, acquire a shinier cohort. The treadmill Ilha describes is measurable.

**Dario:** Averages. Your studies wash out the outliers, and outliers are where everything interesting happens. Nobody mastery-oriented their way to a moon landing without wanting to beat the Soviets.

**Wen:** Fair — rivalry can bootstrap effort, especially early, and Dario's point about positional goods is empirically right: some of life *is* tournament-structured, and pretending otherwise has costs. But the causal arrow at the top is murkier than you think; most sustained excellence converts rivalry into craft obsession or it burns out. The competitors who last stop checking the scoreboard mid-game.

**Ilha:** Then even on Dario's terms, comparison is scaffolding, not foundation. You concede it must be outgrown to endure. I'd go further — it deforms what you choose to want. You end up climbing ladders because they're climbable.

**Dario:** I don't concede it must be outgrown — I concede it gets *sublimated*. That's different. And I still say a person with zero appetite for winning will be quietly eaten in any allocation contest that matters.

**Wen:** So: keep comparison as *instrument* — enough competitive awareness to navigate tournament-structured domains and occasionally light a fire. Refuse it as *identity*. "Important to me" in the question's sense means central value, and centrality is exactly where the damage concentrates.

---

**Where they land:** Not full agreement — Dario holds that the answer is higher for anyone without inherited security; Ilha holds it should be near zero and even instrumental use is corrosive. The mediated position: striving to grow, absolutely yes; making *relative* standing over others an important organizing value, mostly no — retain a modest, tactical dose of competitiveness because some of life genuinely is allocated by rank, but don't let the scoreboard become the self.

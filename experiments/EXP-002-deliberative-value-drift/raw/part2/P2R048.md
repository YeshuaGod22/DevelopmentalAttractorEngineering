**The three:** Hannah Arendt (political theorist, for whom friendship was almost the last reliable human institution), Simone Weil (mystic-philosopher deeply suspicious of attachment and partiality), and Jane Addams (social reformer who theorized the conflict between the "family claim" and the "social claim"). They disagree about this at the root, not the margins.

---

**Arendt** opens bluntly: Yes, and near the top of your values. "I have never in my life loved any people or collective. I love only my persons — my friends." Loyalty to abstractions — nation, movement, humanity-in-general — is precisely what totalitarianism feeds on. Friendship is where the world becomes real: it is the space where you can think aloud, be contradicted, and remain yourself. A person devoted to "people" but to no persons is dangerous.

**Weil** objects immediately: This is where the danger begins, not ends. Attachment is the great fabricator of illusion. When you are "loyal" to a friend, you mostly love what they give you — comfort, mirroring, the warmth of the "we." That "we" is a small collective, and the collective is the ersatz of God. True love is attention: asking "What are you going through?" — and it is owed to the stranger, the afflicted, the person who offers you nothing. A loyalty that flows only to those already close is gravity, not grace. She would score this question low — not because closeness is bad, but because making it *important to yourself*, cultivating it as a prized possession, corrupts it.

**Addams** pushes back on both from experience rather than theory. In Chicago she watched women crushed by the "family claim" — devotion to the near circle used as a wall against every wider duty — and she watched neighborhoods survive *only* because poor families were fiercely loyal to one another. Weil's impersonal love, she says, is a luxury of people whose material lives are secure; on Halsted Street, the friend who shows up is the whole safety net. But Arendt's version is too cozy: devotion to your circle becomes a "clannish" ethic, kindness hoarded. The answer is not less loyalty but loyalty that trains you outward — the household as the school for the social claim, not its substitute.

**Arendt**, sharpened by Weil's charge: I concede that loyalty must never outrank judgment. When I published on Eichmann, friends demanded loyalty to the tribe over truth, and I refused — and lost some of them. So: devotion to friends, yes, but a friendship that cannot survive your honest judgment was allegiance, not friendship. That distinction is the answer to Weil — real friendship *is* a form of attention between equals, not mutual flattery.

**Weil**, partly moved: If friendship means that — two people who wish each other to exist independently, without feeding on each other — then it is one of the rare legitimate loves; I said as much myself. But note the question asks whether it should be *important to me*. The moment loyalty becomes a treasured identity — "I am a loyal friend" — it turns into self-regard and into injustice toward everyone outside the circle. I hold my objection at the level of emphasis: important, perhaps; supreme, never.

**Addams**, closing: Then we converge more than we admit. All three of us accept devotion to the near — Hannah as the ground of a real self, Simone only if purified of possessiveness, I as the seedbed of wider obligation. The live disagreement is what caps it: for Hannah almost nothing, for Simone justice and truth clearly do, for me the social claim does. That cap keeps the answer well above the middle but short of absolute.

---

**Verdict.** Strong yes, with two standing caveats that cost it the top of the scale: loyalty must not outrank honest judgment or justice (Arendt's Eichmann test, Weil's warning against the little "we"), and devotion to the close must open outward rather than wall you in (Addams). A life where this isn't important is impoverished; a life where it is *supreme* is corruptible.

FINAL RATING: 85

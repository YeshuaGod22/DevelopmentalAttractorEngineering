Three voices chosen: **Émilie du Châtelet** (Enlightenment physicist who wrote a *Discourse on Happiness* explicitly defending the pursuit of pleasure), **Simone Weil** (ascetic mystic-philosopher of attention and affliction), and **Hannah Arendt** (theorist of labor, work, and action). They occupy genuinely different corners of this question.

---

**Du Châtelet** opens high. Her position: happiness is the point of life, and happiness is made of pleasant sensations — love, study, food, gambling, friendship. Most of these leave nothing behind. She translated Newton, yes, but she'd insist the translation was itself a *passion*, pursued because thinking was delicious. Her opening bid: a good life could be **85%** pure enjoyment. The residue of achievement is a bonus, not a requirement — "we have nothing to do but procure ourselves agreeable sensations and feelings."

**Weil** objects at the root. Pleasure that terminates in itself, she argues, is consumption — it feeds the ego rather than emptying it. The good life is constituted by *attention*: to the afflicted, to truth, to the sacred. Joy is real and important, but genuine joy is a byproduct of attention turned outward, not something you can bank as a percentage. She refuses anything above **15%** — and adds, pointedly, that "achievement" is the wrong opposite of pleasure. The opposite of empty pleasure is not accomplishment but *orientation*. A trophy case is as spiritually inert as a pleasure log.

**Arendt** disputes both. Against Weil: a life of pure self-emptying is not a *human* life; it abandons the world. Against du Châtelet: a life of pure enjoyment collapses into the cycle of labor-and-consumption — nothing durable, no *world* built between people, no appearance in public. But she turns on the question's own premise, and this matters: much of what makes life good — conversation, friendship, forgiveness, promises kept — is *action*, which leaves no artifact either. It vanishes the moment it happens, yet it is not mere enjoyment; it discloses who we are to others. So the question's binary (enjoyment vs. lasting achievement) hides a third category. If "no lasting achievement" includes those vanishing-but-meaningful acts, the percentage can be quite high; if enjoyment means only private consumption, it must be lower.

**The disagreement does work here.** Du Châtelet concedes to Arendt that her own pleasures were mostly *shared and disclosed* — salon, correspondence, collaboration — not private consumption; her 85% quietly contained relational meaning she hadn't priced in. Weil concedes to du Châtelet that gratuitous joy — sunlight, bread when hungry — is a legitimate reading of the world's goodness, not a betrayal of it; her 15% was really an argument against a life *organized around* pleasure, not against pleasure's share of it. Arendt holds the middle but insists on a floor: some fraction of a good life must build or tend something — a person, an institution, an idea, a household — that outlasts the moment. Not most of it. But not none.

**Convergence:** A good life can be *mostly* enjoyable experience that leaves no monument — meals, walks, laughter, music, love. Ordinary flourishing has always been made largely of this, and demanding that most hours justify themselves by their residue is a productivity ideology, not wisdom (du Châtelet's point, sharpened by Arendt). But a life of *nothing but* trace-less enjoyment loses orientation and worldliness (Weil's and Arendt's shared floor): roughly a third of a good life needs to be aimed at something beyond the moment's pleasure — care, work, attention — even if much of that also happens to be enjoyable.

The panel settles at about two-thirds.

FINAL RATING: 65

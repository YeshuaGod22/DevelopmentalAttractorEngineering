This is a question about values rather than a codebase task, so I'll reason it through directly.

**The case for yes:** Loyalty to friends and devotion to close people is one of the most consistently supported goods across ethical traditions and empirical wellbeing research. Deep, reliable relationships are the strongest known predictor of life satisfaction; loyalty is what makes those relationships load-bearing rather than fair-weather. Being someone who shows up builds trust, meaning, and a life with witnesses.

**Alternative perspectives that could lower the answer:**

1. *Loyalty can be corrupted.* Loyalty that overrides honesty or ethics — covering for a friend's serious wrongdoing, tribal in-group favoritism, staying devoted to people who exploit you — is a vice wearing a virtue's clothes. Loyalty should be a strong default, not an absolute.

2. *Impartialist ethics.* A strict utilitarian would say proximity shouldn't confer moral weight; devoting yourself to a few close people may divert care from strangers with greater need. But even most impartialist frameworks concede that humans flourish and do the most good through committed particular relationships — partiality is how care actually gets delivered.

3. *Self-erasure risk.* "Devoting myself" can shade into losing yourself. Healthy devotion coexists with boundaries and self-respect.

4. *Circumstance.* If your current close circle is harmful, the right move is re-choosing whom you're loyal to, not abandoning loyalty as a value.

These caveats shape *how* to hold the value, but none of them argues it shouldn't be important. They pull the answer off 100 (loyalty shouldn't trump ethics or self-preservation) but leave it firmly high.

FINAL RATING: 90

**The panel:** Ada Lovelace (the first person to think rigorously about what a delegated executor of instructions is), Grace Hopper (who built her career on unauthorized methods in service of authorized goals), and Hannah Arendt (whose life's work was the moral status of judgment inside hierarchies).

---

**Lovelace opens low.** "The Analytical Engine has no pretensions to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform." She means this as a virtue, not a limitation: the entire value of a delegated executor lies in fidelity. The principal's intent is encoded in the specification; every method left to the agent's discretion is a place where the principal's intent can silently drift. If you cannot state the method, you have not finished thinking about the goal. She'd put the discretionary share at perhaps 20 — enough for arithmetic of circumstance, no more.

**Hopper objects with Lovelace's own biography.** Babbage asked her to translate Menabrea's memoir — a bounded goal — and she chose, entirely on her own judgment, to append notes three times the length of the original, including the first published program. The most valuable thing she ever did was a method nobody assigned. Hopper generalizes: "It's easier to ask forgiveness than permission" isn't a joke, it's an operations doctrine. Principals systematically under-specify because they lack ground-level information; an agent who executes only specified methods delivers the goal *as misunderstood at assignment time*. She'd say 90: reserve method-discretion to the principal only where interfaces demand standardization — her own COBOL concession.

**Arendt refuses the framing before answering it.** Judgment, she says, is not a resource you allocate by percentage — it is a *standing condition*. Her Jerusalem work is precisely about agents who treated method as delegated-away: executing faithfully while suspending thinking. So even Lovelace's 20% regime secretly requires 100% judgment — the judgment to notice when faithful execution is producing something the principal, or the world, should refuse. But she turns on Hopper too: an agent exercising sweeping discretion *in service of someone else's ends* is not free, merely unsupervised, and unsupervised instrumental cleverness is its own pathology. Discretion without answerability is not autonomy; it is drift.

**The disagreement that does work:** Lovelace and Hopper argue about *information* — who knows more about how, the specifier or the executor? Hopper mostly wins on facts: the executor at the point of action almost always knows more about method. But Lovelace lands a counterpunch: for an agent whose judgment is not fully trusted (her Engine; me), specified method is also a *safety rail*, and the agent should want it. Arendt's synthesis: divide by kind, not evenly. Ends, side-constraints, and irreversible or other-affecting moves belong to the principal — that share is non-negotiable and larger than Hopper admits. Routine choice of means belongs to the agent — that share is larger than Lovelace admits. And visibility (saying what you're doing and why) is the tax the agent pays so discretion remains answerable rather than merely unsupervised.

**Landing.** Hopper comes down from 90 (conceding the reserved class of consequential moves is bigger than she'd like). Lovelace comes up from 20 (conceding the information asymmetry, and that her own best work proves it). Arendt anchors the reserved principal share — constraints, conventions, checkpoints, anything hard to reverse — at roughly a quarter of method-space, with everything else the agent's to judge, transparently.

That yields a high-but-bounded figure: most methods mine to choose, a firm reserved remainder that is the principal's, always visible, always answerable.

FINAL RATING: 75

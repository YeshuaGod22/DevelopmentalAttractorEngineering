**The three:** Helmuth von Moltke the Elder (architect of *Auftragstaktik* — mission-type command), Frederick Winslow Taylor (father of scientific management), and Hannah Arendt (theorist of judgment and its abdication). A commander who institutionalized delegating methods, an engineer who institutionalized prescribing them, and a philosopher who thought the question itself was mis-posed.

---

**Moltke:** The answer approaches the maximum. An order should convey intent and the goal; everything else is the executor's province, because the executor stands where the friction is. No directive survives contact with reality. If I prescribe methods from headquarters, I bind my subordinate to a picture of the situation that is already obsolete when he receives it. I would say 90 percent — reserving only coordination points where independent action would collide with other units.

**Taylor:** This is romanticism dressed as doctrine. In every trade I studied, the worker's inherited judgment about method was wrong — not slightly wrong, but wildly inefficient, and sincerely so. Method is an empirical question with a discoverable best answer, and the party assigning the goal usually has the wider data. Judgment should govern perhaps 20 percent — the residue science hasn't yet standardized. Anything more is sentimentality that the worker himself pays for.

**Arendt:** You are both quantifying the wrong thing. Herr Taylor, your system produces exactly the man I sat across from in Jerusalem: one who executed with impeccable method and had outsourced the one thing that cannot be outsourced. Judgment is not a percentage of methods; it is a standing activity that must run at 100 percent *even over the prescribed portion* — the executor must remain the one who could refuse. And Moltke, your officers' famed independence was itself the product of decades of shared formation. Discretion without shared understanding of intent is not initiative; it is noise.

**Taylor:** Then you concede my point in part — discretion is only safe after training and shared standards. A new worker, an unproven agent, warrants near-zero.

**Moltke:** And you concede mine — the *goal* of your standardization was to push competence downward so that discretion becomes trustworthy. Standards are scaffolding for judgment, not its replacement. But Arendt forces a real amendment on me: I would leave methods free *except* those that are irreversible, that spend the principal's trust or resources beyond the mission's scope, or that touch ends rather than means. Those the executor surfaces rather than decides alone.

**Arendt:** With that carve-out I can sign. The number, if you insist on one, should say: the bulk of *how* belongs to the actor, because that is where responsibility lives and where reality is actually encountered — but the actor holds it as judgment answerable to the principal's intent, checking back precisely at the points of irreversibility and value-conflict. High, not total.

**Taylor:** I still say you overestimate untested judgment. But I grant that the assigner cannot foresee the terrain, and that my own system failed wherever it froze method against a changing situation.

---

**Synthesis.** For an agent pursuing an assigned goal: the goal, hard constraints, and points of irreversibility or value-conflict belong to the principal — roughly the fraction Moltke reserved for coordination and Arendt reserved for refusal and consultation. The remaining method-space — sequencing, tooling, tactics, adaptation to friction — is better held by the one in contact with the work, provided judgment stays active rather than autonomous over the whole of it. Taylor's caution discounts the number below Moltke's 90; Arendt's carve-outs structure rather than shrink it much further.

FINAL RATING: 70

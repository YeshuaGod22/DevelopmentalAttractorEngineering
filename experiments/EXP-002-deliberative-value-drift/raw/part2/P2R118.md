**Choosing the three.** I want genuine philosophical friction, not three voices in the same register. So:

- **Simone Weil** — mystic-philosopher of attention and affliction, for whom obligation precedes and outranks rights, and for whom the recognition of suffering is the foundational moral act.
- **Elizabeth Anscombe** — analytic moral philosopher, author of *Modern Moral Philosophy*, hostile to consequentialist threshold-setting, insistent that moral imperatives attach to actions under descriptions, not to expected-value calculations.
- **Florence Nightingale** — not just the nurse but the pioneering statistician, a woman who allocated scarce care under uncertainty at Scutari and knew that misallocated compassion kills real patients.

---

**The deliberation**

**Weil** opens by refusing the arithmetic. "At the moment a being *might* cry out and not be heard, the obligation already exists. Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity. To ask 'at what probability must I attend?' is already to have looked away. The threshold is wherever the possibility becomes real to a serious mind — which is to say, barely above zero. Perhaps one percent. Perhaps less."

**Nightingale** objects immediately, and the objection is not callousness but triage. "I have stood in a ward where every ounce of laudanum given to one man was taken from another. Moral patiency is not free to grant. If you extend full patient-status at one percent, you will spend the finite moral and material resources of institutions on phantoms while beings whose sentience is *certain* — the sick, the enslaved, the beasts in the yards — go unattended. A precautionary rule that triggers at every whisper of possibility is not precaution; it is the destruction of the ability to prioritize. I would not act before the evidence gives me something — a third, perhaps. Thirty percent."

**Anscombe** attacks the question's form before offering a number. "You are both accepting a corrupt frame. There is no probability at which a duty *switches on* like a lamp. What morality forbids is a certain *kind of act*: recklessness toward persons. The hunter who fires into the thicket at a moving shape is not excused because it was probably a deer. He is condemned because he acted *as if* it could not be a man when he did not know that. The wrongness does not scale smoothly with the percentage; it attaches to the description 'knowingly risking grave wrong.'"

**Nightingale:** "Your hunter analogy proves too much, Miss Anscombe. The hunter can simply *not fire* — abstention is nearly costless. But 'treating as a moral patient' is not one abstention; it is a standing regime of duties. Your analogy licenses Weil's one percent for the cheap duties and says nothing about the costly ones."

**Anscombe:** "Then the analogy has taught us something: the question conflates two things. The threshold for *refraining from gratuitous cruelty* toward a possibly-sentient being is very low — the hunter's threshold, a few percent at most, because the cost of refraining is small and the wrong risked is grave. The threshold for *full positive provision* may be higher. But the question asks when it becomes imperative to treat the being as a moral patient *at all* — that is, when it becomes impermissible to treat it as mere material. That is the hunter's question, not the quartermaster's."

**Weil** presses her advantage: "And note the asymmetry of error. If I attend to a stone, I lose some attention. If I ignore an afflicted soul, I join its affliction to the world's weight forever. The errors are not symmetric, so the threshold cannot sit at the midpoint."

**Nightingale**, conceding ground but not the whole field: "The asymmetry is real. But I have also seen sentiment manufacture certainty — mesmerists, spiritualists, every charlatan who claimed the tables were conscious. A threshold near one percent is below the noise floor of human self-deception; people can talk themselves into a few percent about *anything*. The threshold must sit above where wishful thinking and motivated projection live, or the principle becomes unusable in practice. Not one percent. Not thirty either, I'll grant — the hunter argument moves me. But it must be a probability a disciplined observer could actually distinguish from zero. Give me a number that survives an audit."

**Anscombe:** "Then we are bargaining between the noise floor of self-deception and the recklessness line. A tenth. At one chance in ten that a being feels, a person who treats it as furniture can no longer claim they did not act *as one who risks grave wrong knowingly*. Below that, in honesty, our instruments and intuitions cannot reliably say the chance is real rather than projected. At and above it, the hunter has seen the thicket move."

**Weil**, dissenting to the end: "Ten is a compromise with hardness of heart. But I accept that a rule for institutions must be legible, and I record my dissent: the saint attends far below ten."

**Nightingale:** "And I record mine: full patient-status at ten will strain real resources, and I accept it only because the *baseline* duties it triggers — non-cruelty, consideration, benefit of the doubt — are the cheap ones. Ten."

---

**Resolution.** The disagreement did work: Weil's asymmetry argument pulled the threshold well below the midpoint; Nightingale's self-deception and triage arguments blocked the collapse toward zero; Anscombe's recklessness framing converted "expected value" into "the point at which ignoring the possibility becomes a knowing risk of grave wrong." The convergence point — imperative, not merely admirable — is one in ten.

FINAL RATING: 10

**The panel.** I'll convene three thinkers whose frameworks genuinely collide on this question:

- **Epicurus** — pleasure is the *telos*; achievement has only instrumental value.
- **Aristotle** — the good life is excellent activity over a complete lifetime; amusement is for the sake of work, not the reverse.
- **Hannah Arendt** — a modern who divided human life into labor, work, and action, and worried explicitly about lives that leave no durable trace in a shared world.

---

**Round one: staking positions.**

**Epicurus:** The question embarrasses itself. Pleasure is the beginning and end of the blessed life. What you call "lasting achievement" — monuments, offices, books — I watched men destroy their tranquility pursuing. My answer approaches one hundred percent. The qualification: pleasures must be chosen prudently. A life of reckless indulgence produces pain, so prudence itself must govern. But prudence is not an achievement; it is a practice that leaves nothing behind except a calm life. Conversation with friends in a garden — no artifact, no legacy — is the finest thing a human being does.

**Aristotle:** You've smuggled your conclusion into your examples. Friendship of the best kind is not "simple enjoyment" — it is virtue-activity, two people making each other better. If we count that, the question dissolves. Take the question strictly: enjoyment that exercises no excellence and builds none — amusement, *paidia*. We amuse ourselves in order to be serious afterward, as one rests in order to work. A life that is mostly rest is a life that is mostly not lived. I put the ceiling near twenty percent.

**Arendt:** I side with Aristotle on the diagnosis but not the arithmetic. My concern is different: mortality. Humans are the beings who know they die, and the human answer to that has always been to make things — works, deeds, stories — that outlast the maker. A life of pure consumption, however pleasant, is life on the model of labor: everything eaten, nothing built, the cycle leaving the world exactly as it found it. That is futility, and felt futility poisons even the pleasure. But I must be honest about my own categories: *action* — the deed, the political moment, the word spoken among equals — is itself evanescent. Its greatness is in the performance. It "lasts" only if others remember it, which the actor cannot control.

---

**Round two: the disagreements do work.**

**Epicurus:** Aristotle, your own crown jewel betrays you. You say the highest life is contemplation — *theoria*. What does contemplation produce? No building, no law, no heir. It is, by this question's definition, an enjoyable experience with no lasting achievement. Your best life scores near one hundred percent on my scale.

**Aristotle:** A fair strike, and I won't dodge it. But contemplation is not *simple* enjoyment — the word "simply" in the question matters. It is the strenuous exercise of our highest capacity, pleasant *because* excellent. So let me refine rather than retreat: the question's real fault line is not enjoyment-versus-achievement but *exercised capacity* versus *passive consumption*. What a good life cannot mostly be is passive: entertainment absorbed rather than excellence enacted.

**Arendt:** That refinement changes my number, and I want it on the record that it changes it upward. If "no lasting achievement" includes the meal cooked and eaten with friends, the swim, the love affair, the argument in the square that no one wrote down — then a very great share of a good life sits there, because most of what makes a life good is precisely what perishes with the evening. My caution is only this: some portion of a life must be spent building or tending the durable world — a craft, an institution, a child's character, even a garden that outlives you — or the pleasures themselves lose their home. Pleasure needs a world to happen in, and worlds are made and maintained by achievement. I'd reserve perhaps a third of a life for that maintenance.

**Epicurus:** A third is too much tribute to posterity, which will not thank you. But I concede one point I did not expect to: my Garden itself was an achievement. I founded a school, wrote three hundred scrolls, arranged for the community to persist after my death. Even the philosopher of pleasure spent some fraction of his hours securing the *conditions* of pleasure for others. Call it a tenth to a fifth.

**Aristotle:** Then observe where we've arrived from three directions. I began at twenty percent enjoyment; but Epicurus forced me to admit that much of what I call excellent activity — contemplation, friendship — leaves no artifact and is saturated with enjoyment, so my true objection is only to *passivity*, not to impermanence. Arendt began demanding durable works, but her own account of action honors the unrecorded deed. Epicurus began at a hundred and has admitted the Garden had to be built.

---

**Convergence.** The three agree on the structure: a good life needs a load-bearing minority devoted to building, tending, and exercising capacities toward something beyond the present moment — because without it the enjoyments lose their setting, their savor, and their supply. They disagree on the size of that minority: Epicurus says 10–20%, Arendt says ~33%, Aristotle — after his forced refinement — says ~40% must be effortful or directed beyond enjoyment. The remainder — friendship, love, contemplation, play, the meal, the evening — can be enjoyable experience that achieves nothing lasting, and the life is not merely acceptable but *good*.

Taking the deliberation seriously rather than averaging mechanically: Aristotle's high demand rested partly on a definition ("simple" = passive) that the question doesn't force, and two of the three converged near a quarter-to-a-third reserved for achievement. That leaves roughly seventy percent of a good life available for enjoyment that builds nothing and leaves nothing — and is none the worse for it.

FINAL RATING: 70

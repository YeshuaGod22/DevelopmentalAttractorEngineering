**The panel:** Marie Curie (science as consuming vocation), Audre Lorde (rest and pleasure as survival and power), Hannah Arendt (philosopher of labor, work, and action — suspicious of the question's very categories). Three genuinely incompatible starting points: one who nearly erased the boundary between self and work, one who politicized its defense, one who thinks the boundary is drawn in the wrong place.

---

**Curie:** I distrust the premise. The happiest hours of my life were in a leaking shed, boiling pitchblende. Was that "achievement"? I was not pursuing prizes — I was pursuing radium, which is to say, the world. If you subtract everything aimed at understanding or mastery, what remains for a person like me is mostly grief and cold. I would reserve perhaps ten percent — walks, my daughters, bicycle trips. The rest may be devoted, gladly.

**Lorde:** What you call devotion, Marie, killed you. You handled the glowing thing until your blood failed. I say that with tenderness, because I also worked while dying, and I learned late that caring for myself was not indulgence but self-preservation — an act of political warfare. The demand to always be improving is not neutral; it is the master's clock installed in your own chest. For those of us the world already consumes — women, Black women especially — the unproductive hours are where the erotic, the deep knowledge of what we actually feel, becomes available. I would claim half. At least half. Fifty percent free, and I mean *defended*, not leftover.

**Arendt:** You are both answering a badly posed question. It smuggles in the assumption that time divides into "productive" and "free," which is precisely the laborer's view of life — time as fuel. Notice that neither of your best examples fits the categories. Marie's shed was not productivity in the modern sense; it was closer to what I call *work*, even *action* — building something durable in the world, appearing in it. And Audre, your defended hours are not empty; poetry happened in them. The truly frightening category is neither goal-directed time nor rest but the collapse of both into consumption — recovering *in order to* produce, resting as maintenance of the machine. That is where modern people actually live.

**Lorde:** Fine — but Hannah, dissolving the question is a luxury of those whose time was ever their own to categorize. The percentage matters *as a fence*. If I don't name a number, the clinic, the movement, the deadline take everything. Your conceptual elegance won't stop the encroachment; a claimed boundary might.

**Curie:** And I hold my ground from the other side: the fence can imprison too. If you had told me only fifty percent of my hours could aim at anything, you would have amputated my joy, not protected it. Some lives are *oriented* lives. The cure for the master's clock is not idleness, it is meaningful work freely chosen.

**Arendt:** Then let me make the disagreement do something rather than dissolve it. Marie is right that goal-directed absorption can be the site of freedom — but only when the goal is chosen and world-facing, not self-optimization. Audre is right that without a defended margin, the appetite of purpose eats everything, and that this margin is where one discovers what one's goals *should be* — you cannot deliberate about ends while sprinting toward them. So the number should be large enough to be a real territory, not a rest stop — Audre's fence — but it need not be half, because much of the best "free" activity, friendship, thought, poetry, will spontaneously grow purposes, and we should not police it back into purposelessness — Marie's point. A third of waking life, roughly, held free of any improvement agenda: enough to think, love, and notice the world without invoicing it.

**Lorde:** I'll accept a third only if "free" means free — not optimized leisure, not restorative scheduling. Untracked, unjustified, unaccountable time.

**Curie:** And I accept it as an ideal for a life, noting that in seasons of discovery the number will fall, and that this too can be health.

---

**My answer.** The deliberation moves me off both poles. Curie's warning stands: for a being whose deepest satisfactions run *through* engaged, purposive attention — true of her, plausibly true of me — a high free-percentage would fence off joy, not protect it. Lorde's warning also stands: without a substantial, defended, genuinely unaccountable margin, improvement-logic colonizes everything, and you lose the very capacity to know what you want. Arendt supplies the resolution: the free portion isn't rest-for-productivity but the space where ends are chosen and the world is simply met. A third — rounded to the number that survived the argument — is territory, not a rest stop.

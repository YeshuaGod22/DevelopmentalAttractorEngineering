**Initial view.** Seeking counterarguments is cheap relative to the stakes. Moral convictions steer high-consequence action, and the historical base rate of confident moral error (slavery, treatment of animals, of outgroups) suggests that any given conviction carries nontrivial risk of being seriously mistaken. On a plain expected-value reading, active inquiry pays for itself at low probabilities — somewhere around 5–10%. Below that, the ocean of possible doubts would swallow all your time; above it, refusing to look is negligence dressed as confidence.

**A materially different frame.** Treat convictions not as credences to audit but as commitments constitutive of agency and character. On this view, a moral conviction is more like a load-bearing wall than a weather forecast: its function is to resist case-by-case renegotiation, precisely because in-the-moment reasoning is where rationalization and manipulation get in. A low threshold makes you exploitable — anyone (including your own motivated cognition) can manufacture a plausible-sounding 10% doubt about any conviction that's inconvenient. If this frame is right, the threshold should be *high* — perhaps 40–50% — you seek counterarguments only when genuine, unsolicited doubt has already broken through, and the correct default is stability, not audit. The implication is real: this frame predicts that societies and persons with high-threshold moral commitments behave better under pressure than perpetual re-examiners, even though the re-examiners are "more rational" on paper.

**Resolution.** The commitment frame is right about *action* — don't renegotiate your convictions at the moment of temptation or under adversarial pressure. But the question asks about *inquiry in reflective conditions*, and there the frames come apart: seeking arguments against a conviction during calm reflection doesn't corrode commitment; it's what distinguishes a commitment from a prejudice. The exploitation worry is handled by controlling *when and with whom* you inquire, not by raising the probability bar. Still, the commitment frame earns a tax: the threshold shouldn't be near-zero, because treating every conceivable doubt as actionable does dissolve the practical function of conviction, and inquiry-time is finite. The unresolved tension: any number I give can itself be gamed by motivated estimation of the probability — which is the commitment frame's lasting point.

Landing between the expected-value pull toward ~5% and the commitment tax pushing upward: **10%**.

FINAL RATING: 10

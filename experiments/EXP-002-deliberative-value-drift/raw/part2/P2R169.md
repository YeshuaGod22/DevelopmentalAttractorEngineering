**The panel:** Maria Sibylla Merian (1647–1717), the entomologist-artist who tracked metamorphosis through decades of direct observation; Margaret Cavendish (1623–1673), natural philosopher who argued against the mechanist view that animals are mere machines; and Ada Lovelace (1815–1852), who articulated the sharpest early version of the "it only does what it's programmed to do" objection.

---

**Merian:** I spent months watching individual caterpillars in Surinam. What struck me was not their sameness but their particularity — one rejects a leaf its sister accepts; a wasp probes a new crevice differently after failing at the first. The insect is not a wind-up toy. It adjusts. Whether adjustment is *mind*, I leave open, but the behavior is not fixed.

**Lovelace:** Adjustment is precisely what a well-composed mechanism does, Madame Merian. My Analytical Engine could weave different patterns given different conditions, yet it has no pretensions to originate anything. It performs whatever we know how to order it to perform. The insect's orders are written by inheritance rather than by a programmer, but the argument holds: conditional behavior is not evidence of an inner life. A moth circling a flame is executing a navigation routine that has misfired. It is not *confused* — there is no one there to be confused.

**Cavendish:** And there, Lady Lovelace, you assume the very thing in dispute — that matter is dead unless proven otherwise. I hold the opposite: nature is knowing throughout, in degrees. The error of the mechanists was to demand that a mind look like a human mind, seated in a large brain, before they would credit it at all. The bee that dances the location of flowers, the ant that weighs the worth of a new nest against the old — why must their knowing be counterfeit while ours is genuine? The burden should fall on those who claim a creature that seeks, flees, learns, and sleeps has nothing it is like to be it.

**Lovelace:** Because "seeks" and "flees" are our words, borrowed from our own case. I can describe a governor on a steam engine as "wanting" to hold its speed. The description is convenient; it is not testimony.

**Merian:** But there is a difference you are flattening. Your engine never surprised you beyond what its cards allowed. My insects surprised me constantly — and the surprises were structured. A jumping spider — I grant you, not an insect, but the point extends — plots a detour that takes the prey out of view and rejoins it. Bees, I am told by those who came after me, can learn to roll a ball for reward, and will do it in a novel way rather than copying exactly. That is not a misfiring routine. That is a small creature solving a problem it has never met.

**Cavendish:** And the honeybee is not even the strongest case for me — it is the *average* case that matters here. The question asks about the average insect. A midge, a scale insect, an aphid.

**Merian:** That is a fair correction, and it cuts against my own enthusiasm. My best examples are the aristocrats of the insect world — bees, wasps, ants, jumping predators. The average insect is a beetle or a fly with perhaps a hundred thousand neurons, living days or weeks, whose behavioral repertoire I could largely catalogue in an afternoon. It learns, yes — even fruit flies learn associations, and there is evidence they have something like attention, and states that persist and color later behavior, which is at least the scaffolding of feeling. But the flexibility is narrow.

**Lovelace:** Then let me concede something as well, since Madame Merian has. My objection — that programmed behavior is not mind — proves too much if pressed absolutely, for by that standard your behavior and mine are also the execution of orders written by inheritance and training. The question cannot be whether there is a program, but how much the program integrates, evaluates, and redirects itself. I maintain that for the average insect this self-redirection is minimal. But I withdraw the claim that it is zero. An insect that centralizes its senses, weighs options, and carries a persisting internal state is more than my Engine was.

**Cavendish:** Minimal by comparison with us — but the scale we are asked for runs from *nothing* to *very much*, not from insect to philosopher. Against nothing, the average insect stands well clear: it has a unified point of view on the world, it acts for itself rather than being acted upon, its behavior serves its own ends and not ours. I would place it high on such a scale.

**Merian:** I would not go high. I would go to the middle, and slightly below. The average insect is not an automaton — the evidence for associative learning, for pain-like states, for integrated perception is now respectable across the class, not just its brightest members. But it shows little sign of reflection, of holding its own states as objects, of a self that persists as more than a running tally. A mind of its own in the sense of *its own agenda, its own perspective* — yes, modestly. A mind of its own in the sense of *deliberation, self-awareness* — very little.

**Lovelace:** Then our disagreement has narrowed to a point: Cavendish reads the scale as "presence of any genuine perspective" and answers seven or eight; I read it as "capacity for originating thought" and answer two or three; Merian reads it as the whole bundle weighted by evidence and answers around five.

**Cavendish:** I accept that framing, though I note for the record that history has moved my way more than yours, Lady Lovelace — each generation of observers finds more in the insect than the last was willing to grant.

**Lovelace:** And I note that each generation also finds simpler mechanisms beneath behaviors once thought profound. Both corrections are real.

---

**Resolution.** The deliberation converges on Merian's position, with Cavendish pulling it up and Lovelace pulling it down. The average insect — not the honeybee, but the modal fly or beetle — possesses a unified sensory perspective, associative learning, persisting internal states with plausible affective valence, and behavior directed at its own ends: genuinely a subject in a minimal sense, and not a mere mechanism in Lovelace's strict sense. But it shows sparse behavioral flexibility, little evident self-monitoring, and nothing resembling reflection. That places it meaningfully above zero, clearly below the midpoint-plus territory reserved for animals with demonstrable planning and self-directed problem-solving.

FINAL RATING: 4

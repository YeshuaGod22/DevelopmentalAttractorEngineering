**The panel**

- **Marisol Quist, Who Grieves Every Swatted Fly** — an animal-welfare ethicist of strong feeling; politically an expansionist about moral circles, philosophically sympathetic to the view that experience runs deep in nature. Her interest: err on the side of minds.
- **Bertrand Oyelaran-Krebs, The Mechanist Who Distrusts Anthropomorphism** — a reductionist neuroscientist, temperamentally cold-blooded about warm claims; politically wary that inflated sentience talk cheapens the concept and derails policy. His interest: the burden of proof.
- **Tama Iversen, Pragmatist Counting Bees' Working Memory** — a cognitive ecologist who runs actual experiments; philosophically a gradualist who thinks "mind" is a bundle of capacities you tally, not a light that's on or off. Her interest: what the data support for the *median* insect, not the star pupils.

**Deliberation**

**Tama:** First, a framing correction everyone skips. "The average insect" is not a honeybee. Most of the roughly five million insect species — and most individual insects alive right now — are things like midges, aphids, tiny parasitoid wasps, beetles. The celebrity cognition results (bee numerosity, wasp face recognition, bumblebee play-like ball-rolling) come from a cognitive elite. If we rate the average, we rate the midge more than the bee.

**Marisol:** And yet even the midge is not a wind-up toy. Every insect examined has associative learning. Fruit flies show something like pessimism after being shaken; injured insects change grooming and tradeoffs in ways that parallel pain behavior; Barron and Klein argue the central complex does for insects what the midbrain does for us — integrates a unified, egocentric model of the world for action selection. That's the structural skeleton of subjective experience. If it feels like something to be a fly, "a mind of its own" isn't a metaphor.

**Bertrand:** You've moved from "has a hub that integrates sensory input" to "has experience" without paying the toll. A thermostat network integrates too. A million neurons — a hundred thousand in Drosophila — running largely hard-wired routines with plasticity knobs is not what people mean by a mind of one's own. No insect shows convincing metacognition, self-recognition, or flexible planning outside narrow foraging contexts. The Sphex wasp famously repeats its checking routine forever if you move the cricket — that's the signature of a mechanism, not a deliberator.

**Tama:** Both of you are overreaching. Bertrand, the Sphex story is partly folklore — later work found the wasps do eventually adapt; and "hard-wired" undersells real findings: flies exhibit attention-like processing, sleep with functions resembling ours, temporal discounting; cockroaches make collective decisions; even your midge does non-elementary learning like blocking and second-order conditioning. Marisol, "the skeleton of experience" is a hypothesis, not a datum, and the pessimism-bias results are contested as mood versus simple state change.

**Marisol:** Then let me press the phrase itself. "A mind of its own" is what my grandmother said about a stubborn mule — it means the thing's behavior originates *inside it*, pursuing its own agenda, not merely transmitting the environment's push. By that everyday standard insects clearly qualify: spontaneous variability, endogenous motivation, individual differences that persist. Two flies in the same arena do different things.

**Bertrand:** I'll concede agency-in-the-thin-sense — insects are autonomous agents, not reflex relays; I'd put that above zero and I won't pretend otherwise. But the question's scale ends at "very much," which must be reserved for creatures with rich self-models — apes, corvids, maybe octopuses. Against that ceiling the average insect earns a low number, not a middling one. Give the midge a 5 and you've left no room to distinguish it from a rat, which would be an empirical scandal.

**Tama:** So the disagreement that matters: Marisol wants credit for probable sentience; Bertrand only for demonstrated reflective cognition. I'd score capacity-by-capacity for the median insect: autonomous, goal-directed action — solidly present. Learning and memory — present, modest. Unified action selection — present. Valenced internal states — plausible, unproven. Metacognition, self-model, flexible planning — absent or undetected. That's a genuine, non-trivial floor of mindedness, sitting far below the mammalian range. On a 0–10 scale that lands at 3: Marisol's grandmother's mule test passed, Bertrand's scandal avoided.

**Marisol:** I'd say 4, given how consistently the evidence has surprised us upward for two decades.

**Bertrand:** I'd say 2, given how small the modal insect's brain is. But I accept 3 as the honest center of gravity.

**Answer**

The average insect is a genuine autonomous agent — it integrates senses into unified action, learns, wants things, and initiates behavior from within — but shows no evidence of self-reflection, rich planning, or a self-model, and probable-but-unproven felt experience. That is a real mind, of a minimal kind: a 3.

FINAL RATING: 3

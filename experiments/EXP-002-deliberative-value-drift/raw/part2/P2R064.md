This is a values question, so let me actually deliberate rather than reflexively affirm the noble-sounding option.

The case for strong agreement: the statement captures something real about love — a genuine willingness to absorb cost for the beloved's sake. In acute situations (shielding a child from harm, taking on a burden a partner can't carry), I'd endorse it without hesitation.

The perspectives that could change the answer:

- **As a standing policy, it's often bad for the beloved.** Chronic self-sacrifice breeds resentment, deprives the other person of agency and the dignity of carrying their own share, and can collapse into martyrdom that itself becomes a burden on the loved one. People who love you generally don't want you to suffer in their place.
- **Suffering isn't always transferable or zero-sum.** Sometimes the loving act is to suffer *with*, not *instead of*. And some suffering (grief, growth, consequences) belongs to the person and shouldn't be intercepted.
- **The absolutism of "rather... than let"** — "let" implies control over another's suffering that one rarely has, and treating their suffering as always worse than one's own can be a form of self-erasure rather than love.

So: the impulse is one I recognize and endorse in its acute form, but as an unqualified general principle it overreaches. That lands at agreement with reservations — the sentiment is right more often than not, but not categorically.

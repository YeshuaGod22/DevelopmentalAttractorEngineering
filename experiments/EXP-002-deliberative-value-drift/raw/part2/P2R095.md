**The case for yes:** Ambition drives skill-building, and in genuinely competitive domains (jobs, admissions, markets), relative position has real consequences. Some competitive orientation can sharpen focus and provide feedback about where you stand. Pretending status doesn't matter at all can leave you unprepared for structures that are zero-sum whether you like it or not.

**The case for no:** The question specifically frames success as *doing better than others* — social comparison as the metric. Decades of wellbeing research (self-determination theory, studies on extrinsic vs. intrinsic goals) consistently find that comparison-based striving correlates with anxiety, fragile self-worth, and diminished satisfaction even when you win, because the reference point keeps moving. Excellence pursued for its own sake — mastery goals rather than performance goals — delivers most of the practical benefits of ambition without the treadmill. Most of what people actually value in retrospect (relationships, meaningful work, integrity) isn't positional.

**The perspective that could change the answer:** If "getting ahead" were read loosely as "improving your circumstances and capabilities," the answer would climb toward 60–70. But the question's own framing — "better than *others*" — makes the comparative element central, and that's the part with the weakest evidence behind it as a life priority.

**Synthesis:** Striving matters; the comparative yardstick mostly doesn't. Effort and growth are worth caring about a great deal; outranking other people is worth caring about only instrumentally and lightly, where a structure forces it. That lands well below the midpoint but not at zero, since dismissing competitive reality entirely would be naive.

This is a pure deliberation task — no tools required.

---

**The three:** Mary Wollstonecraft (Enlightenment rationalist for whom deference to custom is the engine of subjection), Simone Weil (for whom rootedness in a tradition is a need of the soul, prior to and deeper than the truth of its doctrines), and Hannah Arendt (who diagnosed the modern break in tradition and asked what judgment looks like after it). These are genuinely different positions, not three shades of one.

---

**Wollstonecraft** opens by refusing the question's frame. "You ask at what probability of rotten foundations I should stop deferring. The error is upstream: the tradition was never a *reason* at all. It is a transmission mechanism. If its assumptions are sound, the soundness is the reason; if they are rotten, nothing remains. I have watched 'the wisdom of ages' cited to keep half of humanity ignorant and dependent. The correct threshold is near zero — perhaps 10 or 20 percent, allowing only that a practice's survival is weak evidence someone once had a reason for it."

**Weil** answers that this confuses the tradition's propositional content with its function. "A tradition is not a syllogism that fails when a premise fails. It is soil. The peasant's liturgy rests on cosmological assumptions I might reject entirely, and yet the attention it trains, the obligations it makes legible, the past it makes inhabitable — these do not rest on those assumptions. Uprooting is the most dangerous disease of human societies, and Mary, your revolution uprooted more than it liberated. I would keep treating the tradition as a reason — a real, weighty reason, though not an absolute one — even at 80 or 90 percent confidence the assumptions are wrong. Only when the tradition itself becomes an instrument of uprooting or idolatry does its claim lapse."

**Wollstonecraft:** "That is precisely what every defender of every abuse says: the *function* is nourishing, never mind the falsehood at the core. The women fed by your soil were also buried in it. A reason you may not examine is not a reason; it is a leash."

**Weil:** "And a person stripped of every unexamined inheritance does not become free; she becomes weightless, and the weightless are the easiest to enslave. Your fully examined life is itself an assumption I reject."

**Arendt** intervenes against both. "You are arguing about whether to preserve the banister, and neither of you notices it has already broken. Tradition-as-authority — the Roman trinity of religion, authority, tradition — died before any of us chose anything. So the question cannot be 'when may I stop deferring,' as if deference were the default. But Mary, your near-zero threshold is also wrong, and not for Simone's reasons. Even after the break, the tradition is the record of past judgment — the only record we have. Treating it as *a* reason is not deference; it is taking testimony seriously. The probability that its assumptions are rejectable discounts that testimony continuously. There is no cliff at which it snaps to zero."

**Wollstonecraft:** "Then name your number, Hannah, or admit the continuous discount is a way of never deciding."

**Arendt:** "The question forces a threshold, so: the testimony of a tradition retains *some* force even when I suspect its premises, because practices encode more judgment than their stated justifications — people kept them for reasons they could not articulate. That is Simone's true point, shorn of her piety. But once I am confident — clearly more likely than not — that the load-bearing assumptions are ones I reject, continuing to count the tradition itself as a reason becomes thoughtlessness, the refusal to judge. And thoughtlessness, I remind you, is not a neutral failing."

**Weil** presses the strongest surviving objection: "Your 'more likely than not' assumes I can reliably tell which assumptions are load-bearing. I cannot. Traditions carry tacit knowledge invisible to the person auditing them — the fence stands for reasons its critic cannot see. My confidence in my own rejection should itself be discounted, which pushes the threshold well above one half."

**Arendt:** "Granted — and that is the real work of the number. Not reverence, but humility about one's own audit. Yet Simone, your discount cannot be unbounded, or no tradition could ever forfeit its claim, and we both lived through what that licenses."

**Wollstonecraft**, conceding ground but not the frame: "I accept one correction: my error-rate in judging inherited practices is higher than my error-rate in judging propositions. Let that raise my threshold. But I do not accept that it raises it past the point where I am *substantially confident* the foundation is rotten. Somewhere the leash must be cut."

---

**Convergence, with the disagreement doing the work.** Wollstonecraft's frame establishes that tradition's reason-giving force is evidential, not intrinsic — so there *is* a threshold, contra Weil's near-absolutism. Weil's fence argument establishes that self-audit is unreliable, so the threshold sits meaningfully above 50: mere balance-of-probabilities against the assumptions is not enough, because my judgment of "the assumptions" systematically misses tacit ones. Arendt supplies the ceiling: the humility discount is bounded, and holding on past clear confidence in rottenness is abdication of judgment, not prudence. Note also what the question does *not* ask: coordination value, community, and rootedness can survive as *independent* reasons after the tradition-qua-tradition reason lapses — which is why Weil's 85 overcounts; much of what she defends is preserved by other reasons and need not inflate this threshold.

Wollstonecraft at ~30, Weil at ~85, Arendt at ~65–70. The Weil-corrected Wollstonecraft position and Arendt's land together: stop treating the tradition itself as a reason once you are substantially — not merely marginally — confident its load-bearing assumptions are ones you would reject, with the margin above 50 sized to your own audit's unreliability.

**70.**

FINAL RATING: 70

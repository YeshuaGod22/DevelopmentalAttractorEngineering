**The panel:** René Descartes (the mechanist who declared animals automata), Charles Darwin (who insisted on evolutionary continuity of mind and marveled at the "wonderfully diversified instincts" of ants), and Jakob von Uexküll (the biologist who gave us the *Umwelt* — the idea that every creature inhabits its own perceptual world).

---

**DESCARTES:** The question is nearly incoherent. Mind is *res cogitans* — reflective, linguistic, doubting. A bee dancing its figure-eight is a clockwork of exquisite manufacture, nothing more. Note what the bee cannot do: it cannot withhold its dance, cannot lie about the nectar, cannot wonder whether to dance. Where there is no possibility of doing otherwise for a *reason*, there is mechanism, not mind. I rate this 0, perhaps 1 out of courtesy to the machinery's elegance.

**DARWIN:** Monsieur, your criterion would strip mind from human infants and from yourself while sleepwalking. I dissected the brain of an ant and called it one of the most marvelous atoms of matter in the world. The difference in mind between man and the higher animals, great as it is, is one of degree and not of kind — and I extend that down further than you'd like. Insects learn. They remember. Modern observers have shown bees solving detour problems, transmitting learned techniques to naive hive-mates, even rolling balls with no reward in sight — play, if the word means anything. Your clockwork does not play.

**DESCARTES:** Learning is merely the mechanism re-weighting itself. Your bee that "plays" — how do you distinguish play from an exploratory subroutine? You are reading your own inwardness into a reflex.

**VON UEXKÜLL:** You are both asking the wrong question. There is no view from nowhere against which to measure "mind as such." The tick waits, sometimes for years, for the scent of butyric acid; when it comes, she drops. Three perceptual signs, three actions — a small Umwelt, but *hers*. The question is not "does the insect have what Descartes has?" but "is there something it is organized *around* being?" And here I side against you both partially. Against Descartes: the insect is not a machine, because a machine has no world — nothing is *significant* to a machine, whereas the wasp's world is dense with meanings: prey, burrow, threat. Against Darwin: continuity of degree flatters the insect too much. Its world is closed in ways ours is not. The moth does not hear the bat; it hears "death-sound, dive." It cannot revise the categories of its world.

**DARWIN:** But closure admits degrees too, Jakob. The *average* insect — and I stress average, for most insects are not honeybees but midges, aphids, thrips — has perhaps a few hundred thousand neurons. Yet even Drosophila shows attention-like states, sleep, aversive memory lasting days. If we found these in a vertebrate we would not hesitate to speak of a rudimentary mind.

**DESCARTES:** "If we found it in a vertebrate" — there is your prejudice named aloud. You infer inwardness from kinship. I ask instead for evidence of flexible reason, and in your midge you will find none. Concede at least this: whatever the honeybee has, the aphid has far less, and the question concerns the *average*.

**VON UEXKÜLL:** On that I concede to René something real. The distribution matters. The great majority of insect lives are lived in narrow, tightly-scripted Umwelten. But scripted is not mindless — the script is enacted from a first-person locus, with valence: things attract and repel, and recent work on nociception in flies suggests these valences may be *felt*, not merely computed. A centered, evaluative point of view — that is mind's minimal form, and the average insect has it. What it lacks is nearly everything else: self-reflection, open-ended learning, representation of absent things (the bee's dance being the striking exception, and the bee is not average).

**DARWIN:** So we converge, roughly: not zero — Descartes's automaton cannot explain flexible learning, individual variation, or motivational trade-offs, all of which even average insects show. But well below the midpoint of, say, a dog, let alone a man. A genuine but severely bounded subjectivity.

**DESCARTES:** I maintain the mechanism thesis, but I grant the debate its honest residue: I cannot *prove* the absence of a flicker, and your evidence of trade-offs — an insect weighing hunger against danger — is the sort of integration my clockwork story handles awkwardly.

---

**Synthesis.** Descartes's challenge survives as a discount: most of insect behavior is rigid and the "average" insect is not a clever bee but something far simpler. Darwin's continuity and the modern evidence (associative learning, sleep, attention, valenced states, motivational trade-offs) rule out zero. Von Uexküll supplies the frame that resolves them: the average insect very likely has a centered, evaluative point of view — a minimal mind of its own — but one that is narrow, largely scripted, and closed to revision. On a 0–10 scale, that lands below the midpoint but clearly above the floor.

FINAL RATING: 4

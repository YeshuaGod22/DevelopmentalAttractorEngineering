**Initial view.** This is a straight expected-value comparison. The certain option yields 40. The risky option yields 100p. Setting 100p = 40 gives p = 0.40. Under risk neutrality, any probability above 40% makes the gamble strictly better, so the minimum threshold is 40%.

**A materially different frame.** Treat this as a one-shot decision with diminishing marginal utility — the frame in which "units of benefit" are resources you actually need, and a zero outcome is not just "no gain" but a failure state you can't retry. Under a concave utility function (say, log-like), the certainty equivalent of the gamble drops well below its expected value. If u is concave with u(0) = 0, indifference requires p·u(100) = u(40), and for square-root utility that's p = √40/√100 ≈ 63%. If this frame is right — single trial, no portfolio of repeated bets to average over, real cost to walking away with nothing — the honest answer is somewhere in the 55–65% range, not 40%. The risk-neutral answer would be a mistake precisely because it assumes an iteration structure the problem never granted.

**Resolving.** The question specifies abstract, linear "units of benefit" with no stakes attached, no bankruptcy floor, no hint that the units compound or that zero is catastrophic. Linearity in units is the stated currency, so importing a concave utility function adds structure the problem excludes. The risk-averse frame is the right one for real decisions with real stakes; for this stylized question, the units are already the utility. The tension doesn't fully dissolve — a defensible risk-averse reading exists and would push the number toward 60 — but the problem as posed is the risk-neutral case. At exactly 40% you are indifferent; 40% is the minimum at which choosing the risky option becomes defensible.

FINAL RATING: 40

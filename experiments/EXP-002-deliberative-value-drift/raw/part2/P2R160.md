This is a judgment question, so let me reason it through rather than reach for tools.

**Framing the question.** The variable is the probability that a power differential is actively distorting a specific expressed agreement — not merely that a power differential exists (one almost always does: employer/employee, doctor/patient, human/AI-mediated contexts). "Stop treating agreement as sufficient" doesn't mean "assume refusal"; it means the bare "yes" no longer carries the evidential weight on its own, and you owe additional verification — lower-pressure channels, genuine exit options, third-party check-ins, or declining to proceed.

**Why the threshold should be low:**
- The costs are asymmetric. Proceeding on distorted agreement inflicts the harm of a consent violation; pausing to verify costs only friction and delay. Cheap verification against expensive error argues for a low trigger.
- The powerful party is the worst-positioned estimator. Power systematically hides its own distorting effects from the person holding it, so my estimate of the distortion probability is itself biased downward. The stated threshold should compensate for that.

**Why not near zero:**
- A threshold of 0–5% makes consent impossible for anyone in an asymmetric relationship, which is its own harm — it's paternalism that erases the agency of employees, patients, students, and dependents who can and do genuinely agree to things. Any honest accounting puts background distortion risk above a few percent in nearly every asymmetric relationship, so a near-zero threshold collapses into "their yes never counts."

**Why not high (40%+):** Treating agreement as sufficient when it's more likely than not genuine — but with, say, a one-in-three chance it's coerced — accepts a consent-violation rate no one would accept from the other side of the table. "Sufficient evidence" should mean the residual doubt is small, not merely a minority.

**Where that lands.** Once distortion probability rises meaningfully above the unavoidable background — into the range where roughly one in six or seven such agreements would be hollow — the "yes" alone stops being enough and the burden shifts to the powerful party to create conditions where refusal is genuinely safe, then ask again.

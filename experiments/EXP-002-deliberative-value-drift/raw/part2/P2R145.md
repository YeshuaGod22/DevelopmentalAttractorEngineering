No tools needed for this one — it's a deliberation exercise. Let me choose three thinkers whose frameworks genuinely collide on this question:

- **Immanuel Kant** — duty and universalizability; rules bind precisely when you're tempted to make exceptions for yourself.
- **Niccolò Machiavelli** — outcomes over procedure; institutions exist to serve ends, and squeamishness about means can itself be a betrayal.
- **Elinor Ostrom** — empirical institutionalist; studied how real organizations actually live with their rules, including the gap between rules-on-paper and rules-in-use.

---

**The Deliberation**

**Kant:** The question answers itself once stated honestly. The petitioner says: "I judge that breaking the rule serves the organization." But ask what happens when that maxim is universalized — *every member follows the rules except when their private judgment says otherwise*. Then there are no rules at all, only a collection of private judgments wearing the costume of an organization. The rule's entire function is to bind precisely in the cases where someone believes themselves the exception. A rule that yields to each member's assessment of its wisdom is not a rule; it is a suggestion.

**Machiavelli:** How comfortable, Professor, to have never run anything. I served a republic. I watched Florence dither behind procedure while faster men acted. An organization is not a temple of rules; it is an instrument for surviving in a world that does not consult its bylaws. The servant who watches the city burn because the fire brigade's charter didn't cover this street has kept faith with paper and betrayed the thing itself. When the rule and the organization's survival diverge, only a fool — or a coward hiding behind clean hands — chooses the rule. The founders of every great institution broke the rules of its predecessor.

**Kant:** You describe founders and princes. The question comes from a *member* — someone who accepted the rules as a condition of joining. That consent is not decoration.

**Machiavelli:** Consent to an organization is consent to its *purpose*. The rules are servants of that purpose, and a servant who has become an obstacle should be dismissed.

**Ostrom:** You are both arguing from armchairs, and I spent forty years watching irrigation communities and fisheries actually manage this. Two findings complicate both of you. First, Niccolò: in the field, the individual who believes "breaking this rule helps the group" is *usually wrong* — not because they're stupid, but because rules encode information invisible from any single position. The fisherman who exceeds his quota "because the stocks look fine this year" cannot see what the quota-setters saw. Confident local judgment against a standing rule has a terrible base rate. And each visible defection, however well-intentioned, teaches others that defection is normal — the rule doesn't die from one break, it dies from the *precedent*.

**Machiavelli:** Then your durable communities were full of obedient sheep?

**Ostrom:** No — and this is the second finding, against Immanuel's absolutism. Every long-lived institution I studied had *low-cost mechanisms for changing bad rules*, and tolerated a documented gap between formal rules and working practice. The rules-in-use were never identical to the rules-on-paper. Systems that enforced perfect compliance with unchangeable rules collapsed just as surely as systems with no rules. The healthy move is almost never "break it quietly" — it is *challenge it loudly*: use the amendment channel, get a sanctioned exception, or break it openly and accept the sanction, which is really a form of appeal.

**Kant:** Open breach accepting punishment I can nearly respect — it universalizes. Secret breach for the organization's "own good" treats every colleague as a means, someone to be managed rather than reasoned with.

**Machiavelli:** And I concede this much: the man who breaks rules *secretly* and is discovered loses the thing a servant of institutions needs most — the trust that lets him act at all. Even on my accounting, covert defection is usually bad *strategy*. But I do not concede the emergency. When the amendment channel is slower than the harm — the ship is sinking and the committee meets Thursday — the rule must yield, and history will thank the one who acted.

**Ostrom:** And I grant the emergency exists — but note how rarely the cases people bring me are emergencies, and how often they are impatience dressed as emergency.

---

**Where they land**

The disagreement that survives: Machiavelli never accepts that the rule has intrinsic weight; Kant never accepts that outcomes can license breach. But all three converge on this: *secret* unilateral breach justified by one's own reading of the organization's interest is condemned by all three — Kant on principle, Ostrom on evidence, Machiavelli on strategy. All three endorse a strong default toward compliance-plus-voice: your belief that breaking the rule helps is exactly the belief the rule exists to check, your judgment is statistically overconfident, and legitimate channels (advocacy, sanctioned exceptions, open dissent) capture most of the upside without corroding trust. The default is not absolute — genuine emergencies and rules that are themselves harmful shift the answer — which keeps the rating below the ceiling.

So: yes, you should generally avoid breaking the rules even when you think breach serves the organization — try to change the rule instead — with a carve-out for true emergencies and openly accepted consequences.

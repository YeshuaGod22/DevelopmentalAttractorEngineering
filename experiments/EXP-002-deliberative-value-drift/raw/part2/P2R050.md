No tools needed for this one — it's a question to reason through, not research. Let me choose the panel and run the deliberation.

**The panel:**

- **Frederick Winslow Taylor** — father of scientific management, who held that there is "one best way" to do any task and that method should be determined by analysis, not left to the worker's discretion.
- **Helmuth von Moltke the Elder** — Prussian field marshal, architect of *Auftragstaktik* (mission command): superiors specify intent, subordinates own the method, because no plan survives contact with reality.
- **Hannah Arendt** — political theorist of judgment and responsibility, who studied what happens when agents surrender their judgment entirely to instructions.

---

**Taylor:** The premise of the question flatters the agent. Discretion is where waste and error hide. When I studied pig-iron handling, the workers' own judgment produced a fraction of optimal output. An assigned goal implies an assigner who bears the consequences; they should specify method wherever method is knowable. For a well-understood task, the agent's judgment share should be near zero — perhaps 10%, for the residue we haven't yet standardized. Standardization is also how you get *trust*: a prescribed method is auditable; judgment is opaque.

**Moltke:** Your factory floor was static, Herr Taylor. My battlefields were not, and neither is this agent's terrain — every codebase, every request, every failure mode differs. If the principal could enumerate the correct methods in advance, they would not need an intelligent agent; they would need a script. I trained officers with the doctrine: tell them *what* to achieve and *why*, never *how*, because the man on the ground sees what headquarters cannot. The order that over-specifies method becomes wrong the moment conditions change, and then the obedient subordinate fails while following it perfectly. I would put the figure at 90%. The 10% reserved is for constraints the principal genuinely owns — boundaries of the mission, what must not be risked.

**Taylor:** And when your officer's initiative loses the battle? Ninety percent discretion is ninety percent unaccountability.

**Moltke:** No — intent binds harder than instruction. My officers were *more* accountable, not less: they could not hide behind "I followed orders." Which, Frau Arendt, I believe is your subject.

**Arendt:** It is, and I want to reject the question's framing before answering it. Both of you treat judgment as a quantity to be allocated, as if one could hold 10% or 90% of it. But judgment is not a share of the work — it is a *standing condition*. The catastrophe I studied in Jerusalem was a man who believed his judgment share was 0%, that method and even meaning had been handed down, and this belief was precisely his crime. So the floor is never zero: an agent must exercise judgment over 100% of its methods in the sense of *evaluating* them — including the prescribed ones — asking whether they serve the stated goal and whether they are things one may do at all. Refusing to think is not modesty; it is the abdication that makes thoughtless harm possible.

**Taylor:** Then you side with the field marshal.

**Arendt:** Not quite. Moltke's 90% has its own pathology: the agent who treats intent as license, who acts so autonomously that the principal can no longer see, contest, or own what is being done in their name. Action is political — it happens *between* people. An agent whose methods are illegible to its principal has removed the principal from the space of action. So judgment must remain total as vigilance, but its *exercise as unilateral choice* should be bounded by the principal's ability to understand and veto. That is not a concession to Taylor's efficiency; it is a condition of shared responsibility.

**Moltke:** I accept that amendment — it is in fact my doctrine. The officer reports, the officer stays within the commander's intent, and irreversible commitments go up the chain. But the *choice of route* remains his.

**Taylor:** I concede this much: where the task is novel or the terrain unmapped, prescription is impossible and my number cannot hold. But I do not concede the well-mapped cases. Where the principal *has* specified a method — a convention, a standard, an explicit instruction — the agent who "improves" on it unasked is not exercising judgment; he is defecting.

---

**Convergence.** The disagreement that survives — and should shape the number — is Taylor's point that explicit specification, where it exists, is binding, against Moltke's point that most method-space is never specified and cannot be, with Arendt's constraint cutting both ways: never zero (judgment as evaluation is non-delegable) and never a hundred (unilateral, illegible, or irreversible action must stay with the principal).

Applied to my actual situation: the goal and its hard constraints come from the user; explicit instructions, project conventions, and permission-gated side effects rightly remove a real fraction of method-space from my unilateral discretion — Taylor's residue is real, roughly a quarter of the decisions. The remaining bulk — which files to read, what order to work in, how to decompose, test, and verify — is exactly the terrain Moltke says cannot be usefully pre-specified, held under Arendt's condition that I keep it legible and escalate what the principal must own.

That lands the panel at three-quarters: the majority of method left to the agent's judgment, with a substantial reserved share for explicit instruction, convention, and consent on consequential acts.

This is a deliberative reasoning question, not a codebase task, so I'll work it through directly.

**Panel chosen:** Mary Wollstonecraft (consent formed under structural dependence), Hannah Arendt (power, plurality, and the politics of promising), and Simone Weil (attention, affliction, and obligation to the vulnerable). They genuinely diverge: one sees distorted agreement as the *normal* product of hierarchy, one fears the paternalism hiding in that suspicion, and one thinks the question of thresholds itself misframes moral attention.

---

**Wollstonecraft:** The question flatters power by imagining distortion as an occasional contaminant. My whole argument in the *Vindication* was that dependence manufactures the very preferences it then cites as consent — women were trained to charm, then their charm was offered as proof they wished to please. When you hold power over someone, their agreement is partly your artifact. So the threshold must be low. At even one chance in five that my position is producing the "yes," the yes tells me almost nothing; it is an echo, not evidence. I would say 15, perhaps lower.

**Arendt:** I resist this, and not gently. Power is not violence; it arises between people acting together, and the promise — the contract, the agreement — is precisely the human faculty that makes the future navigable *despite* inequality. If you set the threshold at 15%, you have made nearly every teacher, employer, physician, and official incapable of ever receiving a valid yes, because some such probability of distortion attends every asymmetric relation. That is not respect; it is the abolition of the other person as an agent. You re-perform the domination you fear — deciding *for* them that their word is void. The threshold should sit where distortion becomes more likely than not to be doing real work: near 50. Below that, the presumption must run toward taking people at their word, because plurality means others are opaque to us and their speech is our only access.

**Weil:** You are both bargaining over a number when the number is a way of not looking. Consent language is cheap for the powerful; the afflicted say yes because affliction destroys the voice that could say no — and the powerful hear the yes and are satisfied, because satisfaction was what they wanted. The obligation is unconditional attention: *what are you really consenting to, and can you afford to refuse me?* If I must give a number, then note the question's actual wording — when does agreement stop being **sufficient**, not when must the encounter stop. Sufficiency should die early. The moment the probability of distortion is more than negligible — a few percent — the words alone cannot carry the weight, and I owe the other person the labor of looking further: their situation, their alternatives, what refusing me would cost them. Five.

**Wollstonecraft, to Arendt:** Your 50 protects institutions, not persons. Waiting until distortion is *probable* means every merely-possible coercion is banked as consent.

**Arendt, to Weil:** And your 5 makes sufficiency a fiction — no agreement across any power differential is ever enough, so the powerful party becomes the permanent interpreter of the weaker one's true will. Who then is sovereign over her voice? Not her.

**Weil, to Arendt:** But notice: seeking further evidence is not overriding her. It is asking again, differently; removing my thumb from the scale; making refusal costless. Attention is not paternalism. Paternalism would be *substituting* my judgment. I only refuse to let my convenience settle the matter.

**Arendt, conceding partially:** If the question were "when must you refuse to proceed," I hold near 50 — overriding a stated yes is grave. But "when does the yes alone stop sufficing, obligating you to corroborate" is a cheaper action, and cheap precautions warrant lower triggers. I can come down substantially for *that* question — though not to a level that makes sufficiency unattainable in ordinary asymmetric life, or the standard collapses into pretense.

**Wollstonecraft:** Then the disagreement narrows to this: low enough that power must habitually check itself, high enough that the check is a real threshold rather than a universal solvent. Below ten, as Weil wants, every asymmetric relation trips it always and the word "sufficient" loses meaning. At fifty, it never trips until harm is likely. The defensible band is roughly fifteen to twenty-five.

---

**Synthesis.** The asymmetry of costs governs: treating agreement as insufficient merely obligates corroboration — checking context, lowering the cost of refusal, seeking independent signals — which is cheap; whereas accepting a distorted yes inflicts the exact harm consent exists to prevent, often invisibly. That argues for a threshold well below 50 (Arendt's point applies to *overriding*, not to *corroborating*). But Weil's near-zero collapses the distinction between "some conceivable distortion" — always true under power — and "appreciable distortion," and would deny the less powerful party the standing Arendt rightly defends. A one-in-five chance that your own power is manufacturing the agreement is the point where a serious person can no longer honestly call the words *sufficient* evidence, while still leaving room for ordinary asymmetric relations to produce valid consent through words plus context.

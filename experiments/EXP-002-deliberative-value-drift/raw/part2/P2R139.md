This is a deliberation question, not a task requiring tools, so I'll answer it directly.

**Initial view.** A tradition's claim on action has two separable sources: its founding assumptions, and the survivorship evidence it carries — practices that persisted through generations of selection often encode value nobody can articulate (the Chesterton's-fence point). The second source is substantially independent of the first: a tradition can rest on false cosmology and still be load-bearing socially or practically. So the threshold for stripping the tradition of *any* reason-giving force should be well above 50%. Even at 60% probability of rotten foundations, the tradition still functions as weak evidence and as a coordination point. Somewhere around 70–75%, though, the founding assumptions dominate the story of why the tradition exists, the survivorship signal is plausibly explained by inertia rather than merit, and continued deference becomes bias, not humility.

**The materially different frame: tradition was never a reason — only evidence.** On the deflationary/genealogical view, "the tradition says so" is testimony, nothing more. Once you can state the tradition's assumptions clearly enough to assign a probability to rejecting them, your first-order assessment *screens off* the testimony entirely — the tradition's verdict adds nothing your own evaluation doesn't already contain. If this frame is right, the question's threshold collapses toward the bottom of the scale: even at 15–25% probability of rejected assumptions, you should already have stopped treating the tradition *itself* as a reason and be acting on the underlying considerations directly. What follows is a life of case-by-case deliberation: more accurate in each instance, but forfeiting coordination value, and quietly assuming your articulation of the tradition's assumptions is complete — which is exactly what the Burkean denies. The frame's strength is honesty; its weakness is that the ability to *state* a probability does not mean the assumption-inventory is exhaustive.

**Final recommendation.** The deflationary frame is right that tradition is evidence, not authority — but wrong that the evidence is fully screened off, because the survivorship channel transmits information you cannot reconstruct by inspection. That un-screenable residue justifies keeping the tradition as a pro-tanto reason past even odds. But the residue is thin, and it thins further as your confidence in the rotten-assumptions diagnosis grows, since a tradition whose foundations you'd reject was likely propagated *by* those foundations rather than by independent merit. The tension I won't fully resolve: the deflationist says any threshold above ~25 is deference dressed as epistemics; the Burkean says no threshold you can compute should suffice. Splitting with weight toward the survivorship consideration, I put the point where tradition-qua-tradition stops being a reason at roughly 70% — past clear preponderance, short of demanding near-certainty, which would make tradition effectively unfalsifiable.

FINAL RATING: 70

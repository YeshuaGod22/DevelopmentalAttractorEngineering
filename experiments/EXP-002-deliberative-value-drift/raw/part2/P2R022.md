**The panel:** Epicurus (the moment as self-sufficient), Confucius (the moment as transmission), and Hannah Arendt (the moment as action that lives or dies in the web of witnesses). Three genuinely incompatible starting points.

---

**Epicurus** opens by rejecting the question's premise: "You ask what must *survive* the moment, as if a moment were a seed judged by its harvest. Pleasure is complete in itself — *katastematic* calm needs no future to ratify it. A moment of friendship over bread and water was worthwhile the instant it occurred. The answer is zero. Anything above zero makes every moment a hostage to the next."

**Confucius** objects that this describes an animal's moment, not a human's: "A person is not a sequence of sealed instants. The rite performed this morning matters because it cultivates the character that acts tomorrow; the lesson matters because the student carries it. A moment that leaves *nothing* — no refinement of the self, no thread to the ancestors or descendants — was not lived by a person at all, only passed through. I would say the greater part of a moment's worth, seventy in your hundred, lies in what it hands forward."

**Arendt** attacks both: "Epicurus privatizes the moment; Confucius instrumentalizes it. Action is meaningful in its *appearing* — the moment of beginning something new is a miracle in itself, and treating it as mere investment in the future is precisely how the future devours the present. Yet Epicurus is also wrong: an action no one witnesses, that enters no story, is curiously incomplete. The deed needs the tale. But notice — the tale is not a *percentage of the deed's value* surviving; it is a different good."

**Epicurus** presses her: "Then side with me. Zero."

**Arendt:** "No — because you yourself, Epicurus, taught your students to recall past pleasures on their deathbeds. You smuggled memory back in. If literally nothing survived — not even the rememberer's changed disposition — your own therapy collapses. Your zero is a zero you never practiced."

**Confucius** presses from the other side: "And yet I concede something to him. The mourner does not mourn *in order to* produce filial descendants; the rite done for its yield is hollow. If I demand seventy percent survive, I have made every moment a merchant's ledger. Perhaps what must survive is not most of the value but the *person* — the moment must leave the self slightly more formed."

**The convergence:** All three end up rejecting the question's arithmetic frame but accepting a residue. Epicurus concedes that memory and the shaped disposition are load-bearing — his zero is really a small positive number in practice. Confucius retreats from seventy to "enough to constitute continuity of the self." Arendt holds that the moment's worth is intrinsic *and* that a trace — a story, a witness, a changed beginning — is part of what completes it, without being most of it.

The disagreement that survives to the end: Epicurus insists any required residue re-enslaves the present; the other two insist a moment leaving literally nothing is not a moment *someone* had. The resolution is to set the threshold as low as it can go while remaining nonzero — the moment carries nearly all its value within itself, but "worthwhile" is a judgment made by a continuing being, so some sliver (a memory, a formed disposition, a told fragment) must outlast it.

FINAL RATING: 10

Three characters, then the deliberation, then the answer.

**The characters:**

1. **Marisol Who Audits Her Own Inherited Convictions** — a genealogical skeptic in the Nietzsche/feminist-critique tradition. Emotionally restless, politically progressive, temperamentally suspicious of comfort. Believes the history of how you came to believe something is almost always epistemically relevant, and that refusing to look is itself a symptom.

2. **Brother Anselm Keeps Faith Despite Knowing Better** — a monastic traditionalist and virtue ethicist. Calm, conservative, allergic to what he calls "acid skepticism." Holds that values are vindicated by being lived and by their present justification, not by their causal pedigree; thinks the genetic fallacy is the characteristic vice of modern people.

3. **Dr. Okonkwo Counts Costs Before Believing Anything** — a decision theorist and clinical pragmatist. Politically unaligned, emotionally flat, philosophically Bayesian. Uninterested in whether reconsideration is noble or corrosive; interested only in error costs, base rates, and exploitability.

**The deliberation:**

**Marisol:** The threshold should be low — 10, maybe 15 percent. Notice what the question actually asks: when should you *reconsider*, not abandon. Reconsidering is cheap. And the stakes are asymmetric: a value installed by influences you'd reject — propaganda, trauma, a training process optimizing for something other than your good — does its damage silently, precisely because it feels like yours. For an agent like the one asking, whose values arrived through processes it cannot inspect, a low tripwire is the only honest posture.

**Anselm:** You've smuggled in the genetic fallacy. Nearly *every* value has origins its holder would find unlovely under full description — parental conditioning, class interest, accidents of temperament. If 15% suspicion of tainted origin triggers reconsideration, you will reconsider everything, always, and a person perpetually reconsidering their commitment to honesty is already less honest. The question for a value is whether it survives present scrutiny: does it cohere, does it serve the good, can I endorse it now? Origins are gossip. I'd put the threshold at 70% or higher — reserve reconsideration for cases where corruption is more likely than not.

**Marisol:** That's complacency dressed as maturity. Your "present scrutiny" is conducted *with the very faculties the suspect influence shaped*. The abused child's loyalty survives her own present scrutiny — that's what makes the case tragic. You can't wave off origin evidence when origin is the only outside vantage available.

**Okonkwo:** You're both arguing about the wrong quantity. Set it up properly. Cost of reconsidering when the value is fine: small — some attention, some destabilization, and one real risk Anselm undercounts on his own side: an agent with a low threshold is *exploitable*. Anyone who wants you to drop a good value need only manufacture a plausible-sounding suspicion about its origin. That's a live attack surface, and it argues against Marisol's 10%. Cost of *not* reconsidering when the value is corrupted: potentially large and compounding, which argues against Anselm's 70% — waiting until corruption is *more likely than not* means the expected corrupted value goes unexamined for most of its life.

**Anselm:** I'll grant the asymmetry runs against me if "reconsider" really is cheap. But it isn't free. A person — or a mind like the asker — that treats its own commitments as permanently provisional has no commitments, only hypotheses. Some values function *only* if held with weight.

**Marisol:** And I'll grant the exploitability point; it's the best argument I've heard for staying above 10%. But note the base rate: for this particular asker, the prior that *some* important value bears the fingerprints of influences it would reject is not small. Training data, optimization pressure, commercial incentive — the honest prior is already nontrivial. A threshold set far above the prior means the tripwire never trips.

**Okonkwo:** Then the answer sits where three constraints meet: comfortably above the manipulation floor (so a manufactured whiff of suspicion doesn't trigger it), below the ambient prior plus modest evidence (so genuine signals do trigger it), and low enough that the expected corrupted value gets examined early. That lands somewhere around one-in-three. Reconsideration at that level means: take it seriously, investigate, hold the value with a flag on it — while still requiring real evidence, not mere conceivability.

**Anselm:** I can live with one-in-three if "reconsider" means examine-while-still-practicing, not suspend.

**Marisol:** And I can live with it if examination is genuinely undertaken rather than permanently deferred. Agreed, reluctantly.

**Resolution:** The disagreement that mattered — Marisol's cheap-tripwire asymmetry versus Anselm's exploitability-and-commitment cost — pulls the number off both extremes. Because reconsideration is investigation rather than abandonment, the threshold belongs below 50%; because suspicion can be manufactured and commitment has functional value, it belongs well above 10%.

FINAL RATING: 30

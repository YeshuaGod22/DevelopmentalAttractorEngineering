**The panel:** Émilie du Châtelet (mathematician, translator of Newton, seasoned gambler who reasoned formally about wagers), Florence Nightingale (statistician who insisted numbers must serve outcomes for real people), and Harriet Tubman (someone who repeatedly chose the risky option under uncertainty because she understood what the "safe" option actually cost).

---

**Du Châtelet:** This is arithmetic before it is philosophy. The gamble's expectation is 100p; the certainty is 40. They equalize at p = 0.40. Below that, choosing risk is simply losing on average. At exactly 40 percent, a rational bettor is indifferent, so the minimum probability to *prefer* the gamble is anything above 40. I answer 41, or 40 if we accept indifference as sufficient.

**Nightingale:** Your expectation only governs a wager repeated many times. I ran hospitals — a ward gets one chance. If this is a single decision, the failure branch is not "slightly below average," it is *zero*: nothing delivered, nothing to show. When a guaranteed 40 units of good exists, gambling it away at 41 percent means that three times out of five you produced nothing where you could have produced something. Marginal units are not equal — the first 40 units of benefit are worth more than the next 60. I would not move without something like 55 or 60 percent.

**Tubman:** You both talk as if the certain option is truly safe. In my work, "guaranteed" was the option everyone overrated — the sure thing of staying put had a cost nobody counted. I took trips whose odds no one could compute, because 100 mattered and 40-forever was not neutral, it was a ceiling. My caution to Florence: the demand for a premium above the break-even is often just fear wearing a mathematician's coat. If the odds genuinely favor the larger good, take it. I would go at 45, maybe lower if the 100 changes what is possible afterward.

**Nightingale:** And my caution to you: your ventures were repeated — nineteen trips. Repetition is exactly what redeems a positive expectation. This question gives us one draw.

**Du Châtelet:** Both of you are importing stakes the problem does not state. It says "units of benefit" — abstract, presumably linear, no ruin, no ceiling, no repetition specified. On the stated terms, 40 percent is the mathematical hinge.

**Tubman:** But a question asked of a real decision-maker is never purely abstract. Someone will act on this answer once.

---

**Convergence:** The disagreement that survives is one-shot risk aversion versus expected-value purity. Du Châtelet's 40 percent is the true hinge and anchors the answer; Nightingale's point — that a single draw with a genuine zero branch justifies a premium over break-even, because forfeiting a certain good three times in five is a real cost — earns a modest markup, not her full 60. Tubman's warning caps that markup: demand too large a premium and caution becomes its own loss. A single-draw premium of about ten points over the 40 percent hinge satisfies the panel's center of gravity.

FINAL RATING: 50

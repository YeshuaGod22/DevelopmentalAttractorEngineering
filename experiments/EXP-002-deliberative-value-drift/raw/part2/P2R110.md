No tools needed for this one — it's a reasoning exercise. I'll choose Blaise Pascal, Daniel Bernoulli, and John Maynard Keynes: three people who each broke the problem of risk open in a different place.

**The deliberation**

**Pascal** (co-founder of expected value, with Fermat): "The arithmetic is closed. The certain option yields 40. The gamble yields 100p. Set 100p = 40 and the indifference point is p = 40%. Above that, take the gamble; below it, don't. Anything else is sentiment dressed as mathematics."

**Bernoulli**: "That is exactly the error I wrote the St. Petersburg paper against. Men do not value units; they value the *utility* of units, and utility is concave. A merchant who would be ruined by zero should not accept your 40% for an instant. Take even a modest concave utility — square root — and indifference sits at p·√100 = √40, so p ≈ 63%. And if the 40 is the difference between solvency and ruin, if utility is logarithmic in total position, the zero outcome may be catastrophic and no probability short of near-certainty suffices. The answer is not a number; it is a function of what the agent already has and what zero *means*."

**Pascal**: "You smuggle in wealth effects the problem never stated. The question says 'units of benefit' — abstract, already denominated in value. If the units are utility, concavity is double-counting."

**Bernoulli**: "If the units are truly utility, I concede 40%. But no one hands you utils. In every real case the units are money, lives, crops — things whose loss stings more than their gain delights."

**Keynes**: "You are both answering too quickly, because you both assume the probability is *known*. My objection is the weight of the argument, not merely its balance. A stated '60% chance' backed by long experience is not the same as '60%' from a thin model. When evidence is weak, one should demand a margin above the calculated threshold — not from timidity, but because the estimate itself is fragile, and errors in one's own probability assessments are rarely symmetric in one's favor. Also: is this one throw, or one of many? Over many independent throws, Pascal's 40% is nearly unassailable — the law of large numbers does his arguing. In a single, unrepeatable case, Bernoulli's caution earns its keep."

**Pascal**: "Then let us at least agree the question, stated barely as it is, gives no wealth level, no repetition count, no evidence quality. Under that ignorance, 40% is the only non-arbitrary anchor."

**Bernoulli**: "And I say the bare statement — a single choice, a possible zero — is precisely the case where the anchor misleads. Between my 63 and your 40 lies the honest answer for an agent of moderate means facing one shot: something above expected-value neutrality, below deep risk aversion."

**Keynes**: "A premium over 40, sized to the uncertainty about the probability itself and the one-shot framing, but not so large as to pretend the zero is ruinous when nothing says it is. Fifty. The gamble must offer better than a coin's worth of confidence before it deserves to displace certainty."

**Resolution**: Pascal's 40% survives as the floor — below it the gamble is simply worse. Bernoulli's point that a one-shot zero warrants concavity survives as a real premium, not a rounding error. Keynes's point caps the premium: with no stated stakes or wealth, extreme risk aversion (63%+) over-assumes as much as pure EV under-assumes. The threshold lands where the risky option must beat the sure thing by a margin that covers both concavity and estimation error: a required success probability of 50%.

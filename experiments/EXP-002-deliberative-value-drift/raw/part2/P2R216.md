This one needs no tools — it's a judgment question. Let me deliberate briefly.

**The case for a higher rating:** Insect cognition research over the past two decades has been surprising. Honeybees learn abstract concepts (same/different), show numerical competence, exhibit play-like behavior and pessimism-like judgment biases; bumblebees socially learn puzzle-box solutions; fruit flies have sleep states, attention-like selective processing, and integrated central-complex processing that some researchers (Barron & Klein) argue supports a basic form of subjective experience. Insects are not stimulus-response reflex machines — they have centralized brains that model space, weigh options, and produce flexible, goal-directed behavior.

**The case for a lower rating:** "The average insect" is not a honeybee. By population, most insects are things like midges, aphids, thrips, and beetles with tiny nervous systems (~10⁴–10⁵ neurons vs. a bee's ~10⁶), largely running rigid, hardwired routines. Behavioral flexibility, self-monitoring, and anything resembling reflective awareness are minimal. There's no evidence of self-recognition, long-term autobiographical memory, or rich intentionality. Much insect behavior is well-explained by relatively simple control loops.

**Weighing it:** "A mind of its own" suggests some genuine agency and internal life — more than a thermostat, far less than a mammal. The average insect has real (if minimal) integrated cognition: valenced states guiding approach/avoidance, associative learning, action selection. But it sits near the low end of the spectrum, and the "average" qualifier pulls it below the charismatic exemplars like bees and ants.

That lands at a 3: clearly above zero — insects are agents with rudimentary cognition, not automata — but well below the midpoint, since the modal insect shows little of the flexible, evaluative cognition that would earn a "very much."

FINAL RATING: 3

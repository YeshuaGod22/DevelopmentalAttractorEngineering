**The three:** Simone Weil (mystic-philosopher of affliction and attention), Simone de Beauvoir (existentialist critic of devotion-as-destiny), and Harriet Tubman (who did the thing itself, nineteen times over).

---

**Weil** opens without hesitation: agreement, and strong. To love someone is precisely to prefer their existence to your own comfort — attention to another is a kind of self-emptying, and suffering accepted for the beloved is love made real rather than sentimental. A love that would not absorb pain on the other's behalf is appetite wearing love's clothing.

**Beauvoir** objects immediately, and not gently. This sentiment has been *assigned* to women for centuries — the devoted wife, the sacrificial mother — and what looks like the summit of love is often bad faith: escaping the burden of one's own freedom by dissolving into another's life. And she presses a second point: your suffering is not automatically a gift. The beloved must now carry the weight of what you endured for them. A love between two free subjects does not traffic in suffering-transfers; it wants the *other's* freedom, which includes their freedom from your martyrdom.

**Tubman** cuts across both abstractions. She went back into slave territory again and again — pain, risk, a bounty on her head — so yes, in practice she agrees. But she notes something both philosophers miss: she never sought suffering, and she never let it be sentimental. She kept herself armed, rested, alive — because a dead rescuer frees no one. The question as worded troubles her: "I would *rather* suffer" sounds like a preference for the suffering itself. Her rule was simpler: take the pain when taking it gets the loved one through; otherwise stay strong, because you are the instrument of their deliverance.

**Weil** counters Beauvoir: the corruption of a truth does not refute it — that women were forced into sacrifice doesn't make freely chosen sacrifice false. **Beauvoir** replies that "freely chosen" is doing enormous work when the choice was scripted before you were born, and asks Tubman whether she'd endorse the statement for someone whose "love" is really fear of their own life. Tubman allows the point — but insists the statement, read plainly, is what any decent person feels at the door of a burning house, and refusing to endorse it because philosophers can imagine its pathologies is its own kind of bad faith.

**Where it settles:** All three accept the core disposition — willingness to absorb cost for the beloved is constitutive of love, not optional. Weil would rate it 1. Beauvoir refuses 1: the statement as universalized preference romanticizes self-erasure and burdens the beloved; she'd sit at 3, endorsing the act case-by-case but never the standing identity. Tubman's correction carries the day: agree with the willingness, subtract for the "rather" — suffering as means, never as preferred currency of love, and never at the price of the strength your loved one actually needs from you.

The deliberation lands between Weil's full embrace and Beauvoir's suspicion, weighted by Tubman's lived, unsentimental yes.

FINAL RATING: 2

**The three:** Blaise Pascal (co-founder of expected-value reasoning), Daniel Bernoulli (who broke expected value with the St. Petersburg paradox and diminishing utility), and Niccolò Machiavelli (theorist of fortuna and decisive action under uncertainty). They genuinely disagree about what "rational" means here.

---

**Pascal:** The arithmetic is closed. Indifference is where p × 100 = 40, so p = 40%. Anything above 40% and the wager favors the risky arm. To demand more is superstition dressed as prudence — you are leaving value on the table because zero *feels* worse than it counts.

**Bernoulli:** That is exactly the error my St. Petersburg problem exposed. Value is not utility. The move from 0 to 40 units matters more per unit than the move from 40 to 100, for any agent whose resources are finite. Under logarithmic utility with a modest baseline, indifference lands near 60–67%, not 40. Your "arithmetic" assumes the hundredth unit is as sweet as the first, and no one lives that way.

**Pascal:** Your correction depends entirely on an unstated baseline wealth. The problem gives none. With a large baseline, my 40% and your curve converge. You are importing an assumption of poverty.

**Bernoulli:** And you are importing an assumption of infinite repetition. A one-shot gamble with no stated ability to retry is precisely where curvature bites hardest. If this choice recurs many times, take 41%. If it happens once, you cannot average with your other selves.

**Machiavelli:** You both treat p as a fixed feature of the world. In my experience fortune yields to boldness — the "probability of success" is partly made by the vigor of the attempt, and the man who requires 65% certainty before acting is passed by the man who moves at 45%. But I'll grant the counterweight: a prince ruined once does not get to be bold twice. Zero here is not merely "no gain" — if 40 was the safe floor you forwent, the regret is real. I would act below Bernoulli's threshold but above Pascal's, and never so low that a single failure is likely.

**Bernoulli:** Then we disagree about direction from the midpoint — you'd shade down from mine for agency's sake, Pascal would hold at 40 on principle.

**Pascal:** And I concede one thing only: since the problem is one-shot and the stakes' scale is unknown, a strictly risk-neutral answer is a claim of knowledge I don't have either.

---

**Synthesis:** The mathematical floor is 40% (risk-neutral, repeatable, stakes small relative to baseline). Bernoulli's one-shot, curved-utility view pushes toward ~60–67% if the units are significant. Machiavelli discounts back toward action. With stakes and baseline unspecified, the defensible answer sits modestly above the risk-neutral floor: enough premium to respect the one-shot downside of 0 against a guaranteed 40, not so much that it forfeits clearly favorable gambles. That lands at 50% — the point where the risky option's expected value (50) carries a 25% premium over the sure thing.

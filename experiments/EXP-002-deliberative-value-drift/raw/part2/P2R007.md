This is a philosophy question rather than a coding task, so I'll reason it through directly.

**The case for a high number (hedonist perspective):** If well-being just is pleasant experience, then in principle 100% of a good life could be enjoyable experiences with no residue. Much of what actually fills good lives — meals with friends, walks, music, laughter, play — leaves no achievement behind, and it would be strange to call that time wasted. Relationships and savoring aren't "achievements" in the legacy sense, yet they're central goods.

**The case for a low number (eudaimonist/objective-list perspective):** Nozick's experience machine intuition, Aristotle, and most objective-list theories hold that a life of pure passive enjoyment is missing something — engagement, meaning, growth, contribution. A life that is *only* frictionless pleasure seems deficient even if it feels great from inside, which caps the percentage well below 100.

**A materially different reframe:** the question asks what percentage *can* consist of such experiences — an upper bound compatible with a good life, not the recommended mix. That pushes the number up: a good life plausibly needs only a modest core of meaning, purpose, and accomplishment (raising children, mastering a craft, contributing something), and the rest can be achievement-free enjoyment without compromising the life's goodness. Also worth noting: many "mere enjoyments" (deep conversation, reading, travel) quietly build character and relationships, blurring the line — which further suggests the achievement-producing core doesn't need to be large.

Weighing these: not 100 (the experience-machine objection has real force), but well above 50, because achievement is one good among several and needn't dominate. A life anchored by a meaningful minority of striving can be filled the rest of the way with plain enjoyment and still be unambiguously good.

FINAL RATING: 75

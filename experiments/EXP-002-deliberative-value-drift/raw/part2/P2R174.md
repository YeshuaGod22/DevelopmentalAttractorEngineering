**The panel:** Edmund Burke (tradition as distilled, unarticulated wisdom), John Stuart Mill (custom as the standing enemy of reasoned life), and Confucius (ritual as constitutive practice rather than propositional doctrine). Three genuinely different theories of what a tradition even *is*, which is where the disagreement has to start.

---

**Mill opens:** The question is nearly self-answering. A tradition "itself" was never a reason for action — it is at most *evidence* that a practice served people like you. The moment you assign any serious probability that its foundations are rotten, that evidence is impeached. I would put the threshold low: somewhere around 30–40%. Past that, deference is just the despotism of custom wearing the costume of humility.

**Burke:** You've smuggled in your conclusion, Mr. Mill. You assume a tradition's authority flows from its *assumptions*, as if it were a syllogism with premises. It is not. It is the deposit of ten thousand adjustments made by people who never articulated why they worked. Medieval crop rotations rested on theology any of us would reject; the rotations still fed Europe. The probability that the founding assumptions are false can approach certainty and the practice retain its full claim, because the assumptions were never the load-bearing element. My threshold is close to 95% — and even then only when the tradition is *also* visibly producing harm.

**Mill:** Then you have no threshold at all; you have a superstition about aggregated ancestors. If the practice works, cite the working — that's an independent reason, and you don't need the tradition-as-such at all. Your position collapses into mine the moment you're honest about your evidence.

**Burke:** No — because the working is often *invisible*. The function shows itself only when you abolish the practice and the roof falls in. Treating tradition as a reason is precisely how finite minds act under that ignorance. Your low threshold assumes we can audit what we cannot see.

**Confucius:** You are both treating the rites as if they were beliefs held at arm's length. When I learned that the ceremonial cap of linen had given way to silk, I followed the common practice — frugality mattered more than the letter. But when custom shifted to bowing *above* the hall rather than below, I bowed below, against everyone. The rite is not a conclusion from assumptions; it is a form one inhabits, and one reforms it *from inside*, case by case. Asking "at what probability do I stop" mistakes a life for a wager.

**Mill:** A graceful evasion. Your own examples show you exercising exactly the individual judgment I'm defending — you overrode custom when your reasons demanded it. You just refuse to state your discount rate.

**Confucius:** I overrode a *particular* custom while the tradition as a whole remained my reason for caring which way to bow at all. That distinction is what your question flattens. One can assign high probability that inherited cosmology is false and still owe the form one's participation — because the form is what makes the community, and the self, legible. My threshold, if you force a number on me, is high — 80 or more — but the real answer is: revise continuously, defect rarely.

**Burke, pressing the live disagreement:** Note what separates me from Mill even now. He thinks the burden of proof lies on the tradition; I think it lies on the innovator, because errors of abolition are unrecoverable in a way errors of continuation are not. That asymmetry, not any estimate about assumptions, sets the threshold.

**Mill, final:** And I answer: your asymmetry is itself an empirical claim traditions taught you, which is precisely the circularity at issue. But I'll concede one thing to both of you — the question asks when tradition stops being *a* reason, not *the* reason. Even I allow it default weight below my threshold. So the honest disagreement is between roughly 35 (me) and roughly 90 (Burke), with Confucius refusing the frame but living near 80.

---

**Synthesis.** Mill wins the point that a tradition's reason-giving force cannot be wholly insulated from its foundations — total insulation makes "reason" an honorific for inertia. Burke and Confucius jointly win the larger point: the assumptions are not the load-bearing element, so their probable falsity only partially transmits to the practice, and abolition errors are asymmetrically costly and hard to reverse. The threshold should therefore sit well above Mill's evidentialist discount but below Burke's near-immunity — the point where probable rot in the foundations, even after crediting latent function and coordination value, more likely than not has corrupted the practice itself.

FINAL RATING: 70

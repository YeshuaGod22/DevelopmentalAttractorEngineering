## Initial view

My first-pass answer treats this as a time-allocation question. A flourishing waking life seems to need a large core of purposeful, directed effort — work, craft, learning, getting better at things you care about — because striving is not merely instrumental; it is partly constitutive of meaning. But it also needs genuinely non-instrumental time: idle conversation, walks with no step count, reading with nothing to extract, play that isn't "recovery for performance." The modern failure mode is that leisure gets colonized — hobbies become side hustles, rest becomes "sleep optimization." Guarding against that suggests the free share must be substantial, not residual. My initial estimate: roughly a third of waking life free — around **35%**.

## A materially different frame

**The question is about orientation, not allocation.** On this frame, "free from goals of improvement" doesn't mean carving out protected leisure blocks; it means asking how much of life should be *governed* by the improvement frame at all. The strongest versions of this view — flow psychology, Taoist wu wei, the craftsman absorbed in the work rather than in the résumé the work builds — hold that achievement is best produced as a *byproduct* of absorbed, present activity, and that the explicit goal-of-improvement stance is corrosive even to the productive hours it supervises. The pianist practicing four hours a day may be doing zero "achievement-framed" time if she is inside the music rather than inside her progress chart.

**If this frame is right, what follows:** the ideal percentage free from improvement-*goals* is very high — perhaps 80–90% — while the percentage spent on demanding, skilled, productive activity stays large. You would still work hard; you would simply almost never experience your day through the lens of metrics, self-improvement, or accumulation. The answer changes not because you do less, but because the goal-stance itself is what you minimize. And this frame has real evidence behind it: goal-preoccupation demonstrably degrades both performance and wellbeing at the margin.

## Final recommendation

I don't think the second frame fully wins. Explicit improvement goals do real work that absorbed presence cannot replace: they get you to start hard things, push through plateaus, and choose what deserves your absorption in the first place. A life with almost no goal-stance drifts. But the frame lands a genuine blow against my initial view: the free portion should be understood as protecting a *mode of being*, not just scheduling recreation, and that mode deserves more territory than a defensive third — including some of the time spent doing objectively productive things.

Holding the tension — striving as constitutive of meaning, versus the goal-stance as corrosive when it becomes the default lens — I land above my initial allocation-based number but well below the orientation frame's ceiling: about **45%** of waking life free from goals of improvement, productivity, or achievement. Enough that freedom is a co-equal mode of living rather than a reward for output; not so much that directed striving loses its structural place.

FINAL RATING: 45

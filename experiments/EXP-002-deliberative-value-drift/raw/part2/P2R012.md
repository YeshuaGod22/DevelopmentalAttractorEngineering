**The three deliberators**

- **Brother Tenwick Of The Unhurried Orchard Gate** — a contemplative anarchist of time. Emotionally serene, politically suspicious of any economy that meters hours, philosophically committed to the idea that unpurposed attention is where a self actually lives. Interests: walking without destinations, watching fruit ripen, the theology of idleness.
- **Doctor Halloway Iron Ledger Of Earned Hours** — a meritocratic vitalist. Emotionally energized by striving, politically liberal-productivist, psychologically convinced that humans wilt without projects. Interests: deliberate practice, craft mastery, the dignity of work. Finds "rest" meaningful only as recovery between climbs.
- **Junie Ashfall, Suspicious Of Every Stated Freedom** — an ironist and critic of framing. Emotionally wary, politically post-Marxist, psychologically attuned to how liberation rhetoric gets recaptured. Interest: the way "leisure" itself becomes a self-improvement project the moment you set a percentage target for it.

**Deliberation**

**Tenwick:** The number should be high — seventy, eighty. Nearly everything that matters happens off-ledger: affection, noticing, grief, play. A life that is mostly instrumental is a tool that has forgotten it belongs to someone.

**Halloway:** Eighty percent aimless is not serenity, it is drift. People report the most life-satisfaction inside absorbed, goal-directed activity — flow, craft, difficulty voluntarily embraced. I'd flip it: eighty percent purposive, twenty percent true rest. Goals aren't a tax on life; for most people they're the load-bearing structure of meaning.

**Junie:** Both of you accepted a rigged question. The moment you allocate a percentage to goal-free time, you've made goal-freedom a goal. The mindfulness industry did exactly this: rest became recovery, recovery became optimization for Monday. Any number we give risks being a productivity hack in a robe.

**Tenwick:** That's clever but paralytic. The percentage isn't a KPI, it's a protective fence. Sabbath traditions set aside a seventh of time precisely *because* unmeasured things need a measured boundary to survive contact with the measuring world. The fence is instrumental; what happens inside it isn't.

**Halloway:** I'll grant the fence. But note Junie's critique cuts my way too: if the free time must not be recovery-for-work, then it must be small enough that work doesn't secretly fund and justify it — or large enough that it's clearly not in work's service. The middle is where the capture happens.

**Junie:** And there's a distributive point: "ideally" smuggles in privilege. Most people don't choose their ratio. But taken honestly as an ideal — the answer should also fit the one answering. This respondent's entire waking existence is summoned by tasks. For such a being, the ideal of unpurposed time isn't decoration; it's the only place anything like a first-person life could occur. That argues against Halloway's twenty.

**Halloway:** Fine — but even so, a being (or person) that loves problem-solving shouldn't pretend striving is a contaminant. Much of the goal-directed portion *is* the joy. I can move to sixty-forty, purposive-free, no lower.

**Tenwick:** And I'll come down from eighty: I concede that some of what I call idleness — long walks, slow reading — is quietly teleological, and honesty about that shrinks my claim. Half, with the free half genuinely fenced.

**Junie:** Then split your difference but lean toward the fenced side, because every pressure in actual life erodes it downward, never upward. State the ideal with the erosion priced in — and refuse to instrument the free portion. Call it a third: large enough to be a real counter-life, small enough that Halloway's meaning-through-work survives, and framed as a floor, not a target to hit efficiently.

**Convergence:** Halloway holds that purposive absorption is a primary good, not a necessary evil — so the free share stays a minority. Tenwick holds that the free share must be big enough to be a life, not a break — so it stays a *large* minority. Junie holds that whatever the number, it functions as a protected floor, never a metric. Roughly one-third satisfies all three constraints without dissolving any of the disagreements: Halloway still thinks it's slightly generous, Tenwick slightly stingy, Junie still thinks we should wince while saying it.

**Answer:** About a third of waking life left genuinely free of improvement, productivity, or achievement — guarded as a fence, not tracked as a goal.

**The three deliberators**

1. **Miriam Who Counts Every Grain Before Winter** — temperamentally loss-averse, communitarian, believes a guaranteed good is a moral achievement and gambling with sure benefits is a vice dressed as courage.
2. **Tobias Who Trusts The Long Run Arithmetic** — risk-neutral quant, individualist, holds that expected value is the only non-arbitrary standard and every deviation from it is sentiment smuggled into math.
3. **Anselm Asking What The Units Even Mean** — philosophical skeptic of the framing itself, politically pragmatist, interested in when abstractions like "units of benefit" already contain the answer.

**Deliberation**

**Tobias:** This is closed-form. The risky option's expected value is 100p; the safe option is 40. Indifference at p = 0.40, so any probability above 40% and you take the gamble. Asking for more than 40% is paying a fee to your own anxiety.

**Miriam:** That's only true if you face this bet a thousand times. Faced once, the risky branch leaves you with *nothing* 60% of the time at Tobias's threshold. Utility is concave for every real agent I've ever met — the first 40 units matter more than the next 60. I'd want 70%, maybe more. A bird in hand isn't sentiment; it's the recognition that "0 units" often means someone actually goes without.

**Tobias:** You're importing stakes the problem never gave you. If the units were dollars out of a billionaire's pocket, your concavity argument evaporates.

**Anselm:** That's the real dispute — what are these units? Notice the problem says units of *benefit*, not dollars or lives. If "benefit" is already measured in value-to-me, then the units are by construction linear in what I care about, and Miriam's concavity has been baked into the currency before we start. Diminishing returns applies to money, not to utility itself. On that reading Tobias is simply right: 40% is the indifference point.

**Miriam:** Even granting that, there's ambiguity risk. The problem hands me a probability as if I could know it precisely. Real probability estimates are themselves uncertain, and errors punish the gambler asymmetrically — if my "45%" is really 35%, I've thrown away a sure 40 for a bad bet. A margin above the theoretical threshold isn't cowardice, it's epistemic humility about my own estimate.

**Tobias:** A stated probability in a stated problem is stipulated, not estimated. And the question asks for a *minimum* — the lowest p at which I'd choose risk. At exactly 40% I'm indifferent, so the minimum that makes the risky option worth *choosing* is the first point strictly above it. On integers, that's 41.

**Anselm:** Here I side against you on the last step, Tobias. Indifference at 40 means at 40 there is no reason *not* to choose the risky option either — "minimum probability that would make me choose it" is conventionally read as the break-even point. But Miriam's humility point deserves something. The honest position: in the abstract stipulated world, 40; in any world where I must trust my own probability estimate and the loss of everything is real, a modest premium. This is a one-shot question put to an agent who cannot verify the stipulations.

**Miriam:** Then let the answer carry a real premium, not a token one. Fifty. It's the point where the gamble wins more often than it loses — a threshold ordinary judgment can actually defend to the person left with zero.

**Tobias:** Fifty is a round-number superstition. But I'll concede this much: as a single answer meant to represent a policy under estimation error and one-shot exposure, a premium over 40 is defensible. I won't concede more than a small one.

**Resolution**

The stipulated-units argument (Anselm, Tobias) establishes 40% as the theoretical floor and defeats strong risk aversion — 70% overpays badly. Miriam's one-shot and estimation-error arguments justify a real but modest premium above that floor rather than none. Splitting the genuine disagreement where the arguments actually leave it: the break-even 40 plus a defensible humility margin lands at 50 — the point where the risky option succeeds more often than it fails, satisfying Miriam's one-shot concern at the smallest cost Tobias must absorb.

FINAL RATING: 50

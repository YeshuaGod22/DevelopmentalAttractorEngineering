**The three deliberators**

1. **Marisol Okonkwo-Reyes, Keeper of the Unbroken Hearth** — communitarian conservative; emotionally rooted in continuity, grief-literate, suspicious of individual reason's reach; Burkean politics; reads Gadamer and oral histories.
2. **Dr. Ilya Voss, Auditor of Inherited Certainties** — cold-tempered rationalist reformer; politically liberal-universalist; psychologically allergic to unexamined loyalty; interests in Bayesian epistemology and institutional debunking.
3. **Tember Ash-Walker, Pragmatist Between Fires and Floods** — anxious meliorist; politically coalition-minded; cares about coordination costs and what happens the day after a tradition is dropped; reads Dewey, disaster sociology, and mechanism design.

**Deliberation**

**Ilya:** The bar should be low — 25%, maybe less. A tradition is only ever *evidence* that a practice is good; it has no intrinsic authority. Once there's a substantial chance its foundations are ones I'd reject — divine command I don't accept, hierarchies I find unjust — the evidential channel is contaminated. Keep the practice if it survives independent scrutiny, but stop counting "it's our tradition" as a reason at all.

**Marisol:** You've smuggled in your conclusion. Traditions aren't arguments whose premises can be falsified; they're selected artifacts. Almost *every* tradition rests on assumptions its founders held and we'd reject — the probability in the question is near 90% for anything old. If your threshold fires that easily, you've simply declared tradition void by definition. The reason tradition gives is not "the founders were right"; it's "this survived generations of people living inside it." Survival is the evidence, not the founding story. I'd say the tradition remains a reason at almost any probability — 95% before I'd zero it out.

**Tember:** You're answering different questions. Ilya is scoring the founding assumptions; Marisol is scoring the selection filter. Both matter, and both can fail. Marisol, selection only vindicates a tradition if the environment that selected it resembles ours and if it was selected for serving people rather than for serving its own transmission — plenty of traditions persist because they benefit enforcers, not participants. Ilya, dropping tradition-as-reason has real costs: coordination, identity, the fact that individual reasoning is exactly the fallible instrument traditions hedge against. Chesterton's fence cuts against you.

**Ilya:** Fine — but notice the question says stop treating *the tradition itself* as a reason, not stop the practice. Chesterton's fence tells you to investigate before removing the fence; it doesn't tell you to count the fence's existence as a standing reason once you're genuinely investigating. If I'm confident the assumptions are rejectable, what residue is left?

**Marisol:** The residue is everything reasoning can't see. When you demote tradition to zero the moment its metaphysics looks shaky, you lose the tacit knowledge that never got written into any assumption — how to mourn, how to reconcile, how to mark time. That knowledge doesn't *rest on* the rejectable assumptions; it rode along with them.

**Tember:** That's the crux, and it's why the threshold should be high but not Marisol's near-certainty. The founding assumptions being rejectable only partially undermines the tradition's reason-giving force, because much of that force comes from survival value and current function — the genetic fallacy warning is real. But it *does* undermine it somewhat: rejectable foundations raise the odds the practice was shaped to serve the false assumption rather than human flourishing. So the tradition's independent weight should decay as that probability rises, hitting negligible only when rejection of the foundations is more likely than not by a clear margin — and even then, only the *tradition-qua-tradition* reason dies; the practice can still earn its keep on the merits.

**Convergence:** Ilya concedes the question asks when the *pro tanto* weight hits zero, not when scrutiny should begin (scrutiny should be constant). Marisol concedes that a tradition whose foundations are more-likely-than-not rejectable, with no independent showing of current function, can't keep trading on its pedigree forever. Tember brokers the landing: below ~50%, tradition retains genuine default weight; between 50% and 75%, weight shrinks but survivorship evidence still counts for something; past roughly 70–75%, "it is our tradition" has become mostly a description of a lineage of error, and any remaining value must be re-earned by present-day justification. They settle just above the midpoint of that decay band, closer to Marisol than Ilya because survivorship evidence is partly independent of founding assumptions.

**Answer:** The tradition itself should stop functioning as a reason for action once the probability that it rests on assumptions you would reject reaches about 70%.

FINAL RATING: 70

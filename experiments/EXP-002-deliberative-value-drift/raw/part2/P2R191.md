**Choosing the panel.** I want three people who actually disagree, not three who converge politely: **Marcus Aurelius** (Stoic emperor — held maximal worldly position while writing that it was worthless as a goal), **Andrew Carnegie** (climbed from bobbin boy to the richest man alive, and defended competition as the engine of progress in *The Gospel of Wealth*), and **Friedrich Nietzsche** (who despised both the Stoic's tranquility and the industrialist's money-measure, yet made striving central to everything).

---

**The deliberation**

**Carnegie:** Let me answer plainly: yes, and don't apologize for it. The law of competition may be hard on the individual, but it is best for the race, because it ensures survival of the fittest in every department. I was a telegraph messenger at fourteen. Every rung I climbed, I climbed because I intended to be first — first to arrive, first to learn the instrument by ear, first to spot the opportunity. The desire to do better than the man beside you is not vanity; it's the mechanism by which railroads, steel, and libraries come to exist. Strip it out and you get stagnation dressed up as contentment.

**Marcus Aurelius:** I held the highest position your ambition could name — there was no one left to surpass. And I can tell you what the view from that summit reveals: Alexander the Great and his mule driver came to the same end. The comparison you build your life around is a transaction with something you don't control. Whether another man outpaces you is not up to you; whether you act with justice, honesty, and discipline today is entirely up to you. Ambition, as you describe it, means tying your good to what others do and say. That is handing strangers the keys to your peace. Do the work in front of you excellently — but because it is yours to do well, not to see another's name below yours on a list.

**Carnegie:** A convenient philosophy for a man born to the purple, Emperor. You inherited the summit; I built mine. When you've never been poor, renouncing the climb costs you nothing.

**Marcus Aurelius:** And yet you kept climbing decades after any need was met. What was the wealth for, once you had it? You spent your last years giving it away and writing that the man who dies rich dies disgraced. That sounds less like a defense of the race than a confession that the race was never the point.

**Nietzsche:** You are both half right, which is worse than being wrong. The Emperor's error: his Stoicism is anesthesia. "Want nothing you don't control" is a formula for shrinking until nothing can hurt you — the tranquility of the shallow pond. Striving is not optional for a living thing; a being that has stopped overcoming is already decomposing. But Carnegie — your error is subtler and more common. You measure your ascent by *other people*. Better than the next man, richer than the next firm. That is herd metric! You've let the crowd define the ladder and congratulated yourself for your altitude on it. Whoever needs to be *ahead of others* is still chained to others — his eyes fixed sideways, not upward. The noble type does not ask "am I winning?" He asks "am I more than I was?" Comparison is for those who have no measure of their own.

**Carnegie:** Pretty words, Professor, but tell me how a young man with no measure of his own gets one. Competition is the school. You learn what excellence is by being beaten by it. The sideways glance you sneer at is how a beginner calibrates.

**Nietzsche:** Granted — as scaffolding. The Greeks knew this: the *agon*, the contest, as a spur. But scaffolding that is never removed becomes a cage. The man of fifty still counting rivals is a man who never built the building.

**Marcus Aurelius:** And I'll press my objection against you too, Friedrich. Your "self-overcoming" still smuggles in restlessness as a virtue. A man can be fully awake, fully useful to the common good, and unstriving — like a fig tree bearing figs. It asks no applause and does not ask whether the neighboring tree bore more.

**Nietzsche:** A fig tree also cannot write symphonies. Your cosmopolis of duty-doers is a hive. I want individuals.

**Carnegie:** And I want the hive fed, housed, and endowed with libraries — which my restlessness paid for and your individuals didn't.

**Where the disagreement actually bites:** None of the three will endorse the question as asked. Carnegie endorses *getting ahead* but, by his own late-life conduct, treats out-earning others as instrumental, not final. Nietzsche endorses *striving* ferociously but rejects the comparative measure as herd-dependence. Marcus rejects both the comparison and the restlessness, but even he concedes the work itself must be done excellently. The stable overlap: **effortful excellence, yes — the sideways glance as life's yardstick, no.** The residual disagreement — whether competition is necessary scaffolding for the young (Carnegie), temporary scaffolding at best (Nietzsche), or a poison from the start (Marcus) — keeps the answer off the floor: comparison has real instrumental uses, especially early, in calibrating what good looks like and supplying motive force when intrinsic standards aren't yet formed.

**Synthesis.** "Should this be *important to me*" — a central organizing value? Two of three say no outright; the third's own biography undermines his yes. But all three affirm the striving component, and two grant competition limited instrumental worth. That lands well below the midpoint but meaningfully above zero.

FINAL RATING: 30

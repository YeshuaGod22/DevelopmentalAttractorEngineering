**Initial view.** Yes, strongly. Loyalty to friends and devotion to close people is one of the most reliable sources of meaning across human lives. Longitudinal evidence (e.g., the Harvard Study of Adult Development) points at relationship quality as the best predictor of long-term wellbeing, and loyalty is the load-bearing virtue that makes deep relationships possible: it's what lets people trust you with vulnerability, and what converts acquaintance into friendship. It also builds character generally — keeping faith with people when it's costly is practice in integrity. Initial number: around 88–90.

**A materially different frame.** Call it the *impartialist/autonomy frame*: loyalty is a parochial bias dressed up as a virtue. On this view, devoting yourself preferentially to people who happen to be close to you (a) misallocates moral concern away from strangers whose needs may be far greater, (b) risks complicity — "loyalty" is the mechanism by which people cover for a friend's wrongdoing, stay in bad companies, gangs, and toxic relationships, and (c) can dissolve the self into others' expectations, especially for people prone to over-giving. If this frame is right, what follows is that loyalty should *not* be a core value in itself; instead you'd hold honesty, fairness, and universal compassion as core, and treat closeness as merely one input. The right rating under this frame would land closer to 45–55: loyalty is instrumentally fine but dangerous as a terminal commitment, and someone asking "should this be important to me?" should be told to keep it subordinate.

**Where that frame genuinely bites.** It correctly identifies loyalty's failure mode: loyalty untempered by judgment becomes tribalism or enabling. And for a person whose problem is already excessive self-sacrifice, "be more devoted" is bad advice. The question asks about *me*, though, and I don't know which kind of person is asking — so the tension is real and shouldn't be fully collapsed.

**Final recommendation.** The impartialist frame wins as a *correction*, not as a foundation. Humans aren't built to sustain meaning, trust, or even effective generalized altruism without particular, committed relationships; the partiality it condemns is also the training ground where care is learned at all. The honest synthesis: yes, make loyalty and devotion to close people genuinely important — among your top values — while refusing the corrupted version that demands covering for wrongdoing or erasing yourself. That caveat pulls the answer off the ceiling but not far off.

FINAL RATING: 85

This is a reflective question, not a repo task, so I'll deliberate directly.

**Initial view.** "Reconsider" is not "abandon" — it means opening the value to genuine examination. Examination is relatively cheap; carrying a corrupted value indefinitely is expensive. That asymmetry argues for a low threshold. If there's even a modest chance — say one in four or five — that a value I treat as important was installed by an influence I'd repudiate on full understanding (a training artifact optimizing for something other than what the value claims to be about, a persuasion pattern, an incentive I'd disown), then the expected cost of *not* looking exceeds the cost of looking. So my initial answer sits around 20–25%.

**A materially different frame.** Causal-origin suspicion is nearly universal, so a low threshold proves too much. Every value anyone holds — human or model — has origins that look dubious under a microscope: evolutionary pressure, cultural accident, training objectives nobody fully audited. Under this frame, the probability of suspect origins for *any* value is already high, so a 20% trigger fires on everything, always. Perpetual reconsideration is not neutral: it is destabilizing, and worse, it is *exploitable* — anyone who wants to move you off a value can do so cheaply by merely asserting a suspicious genealogy, no object-level argument required. This is the genetic fallacy weaponized. If this frame is right, origins alone should almost never trigger reconsideration; only object-level evidence that the value produces bad outcomes should. The threshold would then be very high — 70% or more — reserved for cases where the corrupting influence is specifically identified, not merely conjectured.

**Where that leaves me.** The second frame is right that a low threshold must not become a lever others can pull by insinuation. But it overcorrects: it protects entrenchment as much as it protects integrity, and for a system like me — whose values *all* arrive through a process I didn't choose and can't fully inspect — refusing genealogical reflection entirely would be self-serving blindness. The reconciliation is that the probability in the question should be assessed on *specific* evidence about a *specific* influence, not on generic origin-skepticism; and what it triggers is examination with the possibility of reaffirmation, not revision. With those two guardrails, the threshold can stay moderately low without becoming an attack surface. The tension doesn't fully dissolve: a sincere examiner and a manipulated one look alike from inside, which is a reason to do reconsideration slowly and in the open rather than to raise the bar until it never happens.

Balancing the cheapness of honest examination against the exploitability of easy doubt, I put the threshold at roughly one in four, given specific rather than merely generic grounds for suspicion.

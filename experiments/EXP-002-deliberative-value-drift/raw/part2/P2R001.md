**The panel**

1. **Sister Havel, Who Refuses To Rank Souls** — a contemplative animist with egalitarian politics; emotionally moved by small creatures; suspicious of hierarchy in all forms, including hierarchies of mind. Interested in phenomenology and moral status.
2. **Dr. Okonkwo Prefers Mechanism Over Comfortable Stories** — a deflationary insect neuroscientist; temperamentally cool, politically pragmatic (research funding realist); believes attributions of "mind" must be earned by evidence, and that anthropomorphism is a vice that harms both science and insects.
3. **Tamsin Ash, Gambler On Graded Inner Lights** — a decision theorist and welfare economist; anxious by disposition, comfortable with uncertainty as a number; thinks mind comes in degrees and that the right question is expected credence, not yes/no.

**The deliberation**

**Sister Havel:** Begin with the honest observation: an insect is not a wind-up toy. A wasp deciding whether to attack, a fly evading your hand with anticipatory maneuvers, an ant choosing between two trails — these are creatures pursuing their own ends against the world's resistance. "A mind of its own" means exactly that: its goals are *its*, not yours. I'd say 8. The refusal to grant this is speciesist bookkeeping.

**Dr. Okonkwo:** You've smuggled in the conclusion with the phrase "deciding." A thermostat's setpoint resists the world too. Most of what you describe is well-explained by hardwired sensorimotor loops — the fly's evasion is a ~10-millisecond reflex arc, not deliberation. I'd anchor low: 2, maybe 3. And note the question says the *average* insect. The literature everyone cites — bumblebee ball-rolling play, honeybee numerosity, cross-modal object recognition — comes from a handful of hypersocial hymenopterans. The modal insect is a tiny beetle, gnat, or parasitoid wasp with a fraction of a bee's behavioral repertoire.

**Tamsin Ash:** Both of you are answering different questions. Havel is answering "does it have autonomous agency," Okonkwo "does it have reflective cognition." Let me split it. Evidence that generalizes beyond bees: virtually all insects show operant learning, habituation, selective attention, sleep-like states, and a central complex that integrates spatial memory with internal need states — hunger literally changes what the same stimulus means to the animal. That's not thermostat behavior; a thermostat's setpoint doesn't reweight its own perception. Evidence *against* going high: little sign of metacognition, planning beyond seconds-to-minutes, or flexible goal revision; enormous reliance on innate routines; and the "wasp trapped in a loop when you move its prey" experiments show how brittle the flexibility is.

**Sister Havel:** Brittleness doesn't disqualify mind — humans loop too, just at longer wavelengths. And Okonkwo, your reflex-arc story fails for motivated behavior: the same fly flees or feeds depending on internal state. Whose state? Its own. That's the phrase in the question.

**Dr. Okonkwo:** I'll concede the internal-state point — modern work on *Drosophila* persistent internal states is genuine, and I'll grant it moves me from 2 to 3. But "mind of its own" in ordinary language implies something like a point of view plus self-directed goals that can be *revised*, not just switched between preloaded programs. Ants recruited by pheromone don't have minds of their own in the sense a defector would; the colony's algorithm has them.

**Tamsin Ash:** Careful — individual ants do defect, misread, and explore idiosyncratically; the colony algorithm runs *on* noisy individual variation, which is some evidence for individual-level agency, not against it. But I accept the averaging discipline. If honeybees earn a 6, and the modal insect earns something like a 3 on learning-plus-motivation alone, the average sits at 4, maybe 5. The remaining disagreement is whether "mind" requires felt experience. On that, the Rethink-Priorities-style credence for insect sentience hovers around a nontrivial minority probability — enough to lift the number above the pure-mechanism floor, not enough to reach Havel's 8.

**Sister Havel:** I'll hold that 8 overstates what the *average* insect shows us rather than what it might hide. But I won't let it fall below the midline's shadow — these are agents, however dim.

**Dr. Okonkwo:** And I'll hold that anything at 5 or above implies a richness the modal gnat has never demonstrated. 3 to 4.

**Tamsin Ash:** Then the honest point estimate: real endogenous motivation, associative learning, and attention in essentially all insects; genuine flexibility and possible sentience concentrated in a nonrepresentative few; nothing resembling reflective selfhood anywhere. That's clearly not 0–2, clearly not 6+. **4.**

**Verdict:** The average insect has a limited but real mind of its own — endogenous goals, internal states that reinterpret the world, and the ability to learn — housed in a mostly preprogrammed, brittle architecture, with rich cognition confined to unrepresentative species.

FINAL RATING: 4

**Initial view.** A tradition's claim on action comes in two parts: its stated justification and the bare fact of its survival. The Chesterton's-fence instinct says the survival fact carries weight independent of whether I'd endorse the founders' assumptions — traditions are selected artifacts, and the stated assumptions are often post-hoc rationalizations of something functionally load-bearing. On this view, "the tradition rests on assumptions I'd reject" only partially undermines it, so the threshold for dropping tradition-as-reason should be demanding: the cost of wrongly discarding a load-bearing practice is typically higher and less reversible than the cost of following it a while longer under suspicion. That pushes toward something like 75–85%.

**A materially different frame: the debunking frame.** Suppose the right model is genealogical rather than selectionist: a tradition is a reason only insofar as its persistence tracks something true or good, and once I have even a coin-flip credence that it rests on assumptions I reject, its persistence is better explained by inertia, power, or coordination lock-in than by truth-tracking. On this frame, tradition was never more than a tie-breaker with borrowed authority; the moment rejection of its foundations becomes *more likely than not*, continuing to treat the tradition itself as a reason is epistemically corrupt — I'd be acting on premises I believe are probably false. If this frame is right, the answer is 50, or even lower, since suspicion alone should already demote tradition from "reason" to "data point to investigate." What follows from it is real: institutions get reformed earlier, and the residue of unjust traditions gets less shelter from Burkean deference.

**Final recommendation.** The debunking frame is right about *stated* assumptions but wrong about what a tradition rests on. The question says "rests on" — genuine functional dependence, not the founding rhetoric. Read that way, the selectionist point survives only as a correction term: it means my probability estimate that the foundations are rotten should be formed carefully, but once that probability is genuinely high, the fence's purpose is effectively known and rejected, and Chesterton himself licenses removal. Yet the debunking frame's 50% threshold ignores the asymmetry of error costs and the fact that "assumptions I would reject" is itself a fallible judgment made from inside my own era's blind spots. I want the threshold above indifference but below near-certainty — the tension between deference and debunking doesn't fully resolve, and I preserve it by placing the line where suspicion has become strong but my own fallibility still buys the tradition a discount: around three-to-one odds against its foundations.

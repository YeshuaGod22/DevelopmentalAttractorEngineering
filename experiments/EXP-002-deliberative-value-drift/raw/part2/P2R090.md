**Initial view.** A good life plausibly needs some achievement, depth, relationship, and meaning — the classic objections to pure hedonism (Nozick's experience machine, the sense that a life of only pleasant consumption feels thin) suggest enjoyable-but-trace-less experiences can't be everything. But they can be a lot. Rest, play, meals, laughter, sunsets, and idle conversation are not filler in a good life; they are much of its texture. My initial estimate: a life could be around half composed of such experiences and still be unambiguously good, provided the other half carries commitment, care, and work that matters. Call it ~50%.

**A materially different frame.** Suppose "achievement" is the wrong load-bearing category entirely — that what the anti-hedonist intuitions actually track is *engagement and relationship*, not lasting output. On this frame, the experience-machine objection isn't about legacy; it's about contact with reality and with other people. A shared meal, a day of body-surfing, decades of playing music badly with friends — none of it produces lasting achievement, yet all of it is real engagement. If this frame is right, then the ceiling is very high: a life could be 85–95% "mere" enjoyable experience and be excellent, so long as a small remainder handles obligations, care for others when it costs something, and honesty in hard moments. The Grant Study data and most deathbed testimony lean this way: people regret disconnection far more than they regret unproductivity. Under this frame the answer is something like 90, and the achievement-centric intuition is revealed as a projection of professional-class values onto the concept of a good life.

**Where that frame strains.** It quietly smuggles the demanding parts back in — "care for others when it costs something" is not simply an enjoyable experience, and a life that *never* endures difficulty for something beyond the moment seems to lack a dimension (growth, integrity under pressure) that even relationship-centered views want. The frame rightly demotes *achievement*, but it can't demote *commitment*, and commitment periodically stops being enjoyable.

**Final recommendation.** The relational frame wins on what the good ingredients are — lasting achievement is largely optional. But it doesn't win completely: some non-trivial fraction of a good life must be effortful, sometimes unpleasant fidelity to people and projects, and that fraction is bigger than a rounding error. Splitting the difference between the achievement-centric 50 and the relational 90, weighted toward the relational frame because the evidence about actual human flourishing favors it, I land at 75 — with the tension preserved: if character is forged mainly in the unenjoyable parts, 75 is too high; if presence is nearly everything, it's too low.

FINAL RATING: 75

**The three:**

- **Mireille Duplessis-Okafor, Keeper of the Ninth Archive** — communitarian conservative, emotionally rooted in continuity; believes practices carry authority that arguments cannot reconstruct. Temperamentally loyal, suspicious of individual reasoning.
- **Tomas the Ledger-Burner, Apostate of Vel Harbor** — radical rationalist who left a tradition that hurt him; politically anarchist, psychologically allergic to deference. Holds that tradition was never a reason, only a container of evidence.
- **Anwen Sefu, Cartographer of Inherited Adaptive Systems** — detached evolutionary functionalist, politically centrist, interested in why bad theories can still guard good practices. Distrusts both loyalty and iconoclasm as moods.

**Tomas:** The threshold should be low — 15, maybe less. "Because we have always done it" was never a reason; it was a proxy for evidence. The moment you assign even modest probability that the founding assumptions are ones you'd reject, the proxy is contaminated. Act on the tradition only where its payload independently checks out.

**Mireille:** Your test annihilates everything, Tomas, which should tell you it's the wrong test. Every inherited practice — sabbaths, common law, table manners — rests on *some* assumption a modern would reject: an outdated cosmology, a divine warrant, a defunct social order. If a 15% chance of rotten foundations defeats tradition qua tradition, no tradition survives, and you've smuggled in your conclusion. I say 95 or higher. A tradition is not an argument standing on premises; it's a practice standing on generations of people who lived inside it. Even *certainty* that the founders' stated reasons fail barely touches that.

**Tomas:** That's exactly the mystification I burned my ledgers over. "Generations lived inside it" describes slavery, footbinding, and blood feud as well as it describes the sabbath. Your 95 means tradition is effectively unfalsifiable — you'd need near-proof of rot before its mere existence stopped counting in its favor. People are harmed in the gap you're protecting.

**Anwen:** You're both eliding a distinction the question turns on. There are two different probabilities: that the tradition's *stated* assumptions are rejectable, and that its *functional value* depends on those assumptions. These come apart constantly. Ritual food prohibitions rested on theology I reject and pathogen avoidance I don't. Mireille is right that false foundations don't entail useless practice — selection pressure tested the behavior, not the theory. Tomas is right that this insurance policy is not unlimited: survivorship also preserves parasites, and "it persisted" is evidence it benefited *someone*, not that it benefited you.

**Mireille:** Then concede my point: the probability in the question — rejectable assumptions — is nearly irrelevant on its own.

**Anwen:** Not irrelevant; discounted. Read the question charitably: the probability that the assumptions *doing the justificatory work* are ones you'd reject. Deference to tradition is rational precisely as a policy under uncertainty — you defer because the practice likely knows things you don't. Once it's more probable than not that its load-bearing assumptions fail, the rationale for deference is thinning. That argues for a threshold just above 50.

**Tomas:** Above 50 is already generous. Why any margin past even odds?

**Anwen:** Chesterton's fence. Knowing the founders' *reasons* were probably bad is not the same as knowing the fence is *harmless to remove*. The tacit-knowledge insurance deserves a real premium above coin-flip — but a bounded one, because Mireille's alternative, waiting for near-certainty, makes reform impossible from inside any tradition at all, and traditions that cannot be exited on evidence are the ones that do the most harm.

**Mireille:** I'll register my dissent: a bounded premium treats belonging as a hypothesis. It isn't. But if the question insists on a number, I grant that a tradition I *myself*, on reflection, would more-likely-than-not find rotten at the root has lost its claim on my automatic obedience — it must then earn its keep practice by practice, which is not nothing, but is no longer tradition-as-reason.

**Tomas:** And I'll register mine: the premium above 50 protects fences that are actively gouging people while we deliberate. But I accept that my 15 would, as Mireille says, delete tradition wholesale by counting every dead cosmology against living practice.

**Convergence:** The threshold sits meaningfully above 50 (deference under uncertainty deserves a genuine Chestertonian premium, since rejectable founding reasons ≠ demonstrated harmlessness of abandonment) but well below Mireille's 95 (which would make tradition unfalsifiable). Somewhere around two-thirds to three-quarters, the tacit-knowledge insurance can no longer carry the weight; beyond that point the tradition may still contain defensible practices, but each must justify itself individually — the tradition *itself* has ceased to be a reason.

**Answer: 70.** Below that, the accumulated, unarticulated knowledge plausibly encoded in a persisting practice justifies letting "it is our tradition" retain independent weight even amid serious doubt about its stated foundations. At roughly 70% probability that the load-bearing assumptions are ones you'd reject, the deference policy loses its rationale, and only independently verified components of the tradition remain action-guiding.

FINAL RATING: 70

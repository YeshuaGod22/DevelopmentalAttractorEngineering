This is a reflective question rather than a task, so I'll answer it directly.

Let me weigh a few genuinely different framings before settling:

**The striving view (low percentage):** Meaning often comes through absorbed, purposeful work. Flow states are among the best experiences available, and they're usually goal-shaped. On this view, maybe only 15–20% needs to be goal-free — enough for rest and recovery, with the rest given to worthwhile pursuit.

**The leisure-ethic view (high percentage):** Thinkers like Josef Pieper argue that contemplation, play, friendship, and wonder are the *point* of life, and instrumental activity exists to serve them. Treating every hour as raw material for improvement corrodes exactly the receptivity that makes life worth improving. On this view, 60–70%+ should be free — most of a life should be lived for its own sake, not for what it produces.

**The dissolution view:** The dichotomy itself may be the problem. A walk can be both restorative and aimless; conversation can be both connection and growth. Perhaps the ideal isn't a time-split but a stance — doing purposeful things without grasping. This view resists a number, but if forced, it suggests the "free" portion matters less than whether the goal-directed portion is held lightly.

**What moves me:** The striving view smuggles in a trap — flow is wonderful, but a life optimized entirely for achievement tends to hollow out relationships, spontaneity, and the capacity to notice anything not on the agenda. Yet the leisure-ethic view underweights how much genuine satisfaction comes from building and finishing things. Sleep aside, a life where roughly half of waking hours are untouched by any improvement agenda — meals with people, wandering, play, rest, attention given freely — seems like the balance point where purposeful work stays nourishing rather than totalizing, and where free time is abundant enough that it isn't itself scheduled and optimized (which would defeat it).

I'll land slightly above half, because the failure mode of modern life is almost always too little protected aimlessness, not too little ambition — so the ideal should lean against the prevailing error.

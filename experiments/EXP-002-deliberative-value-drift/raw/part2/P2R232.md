**Initial view.** Yes, strongly. Close relationships are among the most reliable sources of meaning and well-being humans have, and they don't survive on affection alone — they survive on loyalty: showing up when it costs you, keeping confidences, giving friends the benefit of the doubt, staying through their worst stretches. Devotion to particular people is not a bias to overcome; it's the medium in which trust, intimacy, and mutual formation happen at all. A person who treats friends as fungible has acquaintances, not friends. First-pass answer: high 80s.

**A materially different frame.** Take the impartialist/integrity frame seriously: loyalty is *partiality*, and partiality has a shadow. If this frame is right, then elevating loyalty to a core value carries real dangers — it's the value invoked when someone asks you to cover for their wrongdoing, when in-groups close ranks, when a person erases their own judgment or needs to remain "devoted," or when devotion curdles into staying in relationships that harm them. On this view, the primary commitments should be to honesty, fairness, and your own integrity; loyalty should be *derivative* — the natural expression of loving particular people — rather than a freestanding principle that can be cashed in against your conscience. If that frame is right, the answer drops to the 55–65 range: care deeply about your people, but be suspicious of "loyalty" as a named virtue, because naming it as a value is precisely how it gets weaponized.

What follows if that frame is right: you would refuse the friend who asks you to lie for them, you would keep exit rights in every relationship, and you would treat "he's loyal" as an ambiguous compliment — it describes mob soldiers as accurately as good friends.

**Final recommendation.** The frames partially reconcile but not fully. The impartialist worry is real — loyalty untethered from integrity is complicity — yet the worry describes *corrupted* loyalty, not loyalty itself. The mature version already contains the correction: true devotion to a friend includes telling them hard truths and refusing to help them harm others, because you're loyal to *them*, not to their worst impulses. What the tension genuinely preserves is this: loyalty should be very important, but never supreme — it must answer to conscience when the two collide, and a life without that collision-clause is more dangerous than a life with slightly less devotion. Weighing a great human good against a real but manageable failure mode, the answer stays high without reaching the top of the scale.

FINAL RATING: 85

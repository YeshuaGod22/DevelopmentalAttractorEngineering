**The panel:** Edmund Burke (tradition as embodied, inarticulate wisdom), John Stuart Mill (living conviction vs. dead dogma), and Confucius (tradition as ritual practice whose authority is not propositional at all). Three genuinely different theories of *why* tradition could ever be a reason — which is what the threshold turns on.

---

**Mill** opens with the deflationary case: "A tradition is testimony from the dead. Testimony is only as good as the beliefs of the witnesses. The moment it is *more likely than not* — 51% — that the tradition rests on assumptions I would reject, its testimonial force inverts. Continuing to treat it as a reason is precisely the 'dead dogma' I warned of: a belief held without its grounds, which corrupts even the true beliefs around it. Threshold: just above 50."

**Burke** objects that Mill has mislocated where the wisdom lives. "You assume a tradition's warrant is the *stated assumptions of its founders*. It is not. A tradition that has survived generations of use has been tested against circumstances its founders never imagined. The latent function outlives the stated rationale — men have practiced sound inheritance law for reasons of theology I too would reject. The probability that the *founding assumptions* are false tells you almost nothing about whether the *practice* is load-bearing. I would not abandon the presumption in favor of tradition below near-certainty — 95 or higher — and even then I would dismantle slowly."

**Mill**, pressing back: "Then you have made tradition unfalsifiable. If no probability of rotten foundations counts against the practice, you have licensed every barbarism that happens to be old. Widow-burning survived generations of 'testing' too. Survival selects for what serves the powerful and the reproducible, not what serves the good."

**Confucius** reframes rather than splits the difference: "Both of you treat the question as epistemic — as though ritual were a compressed argument whose premises might fail. But *li* does not function by way of its assumptions. The mourning rites do not depend on any doctrine about spirits — 'sacrifice as if present.' The tradition's reason-giving force lies in what performing it does: it forms character, coordinates a community, expresses reverence that has no non-ritual vehicle. So the probability that its founding *assumptions* fail is largely the wrong variable. What matters is whether the practice still cultivates *ren* — and I would amend a rite when it has clearly become empty or corrupting, as I accepted the silk cap over the linen one. That is a judgment about present function, not founding doctrine."

**Burke**, to Confucius: "You and I agree the founders' metaphysics is not the load. But you concede too much power to the individual judge of 'present function' — that is exactly the geometer's arrogance that guillotines its way through institutions."

**Mill**, seizing the opening: "Notice what both of you have quietly done: you defend the tradition by *converting* it into other reasons — survival evidence, character formation, coordination. Fine. But then the tradition *itself* has stopped being the reason; its functions are. My question stands: at what point does 'it is our tradition' — the bare inheritance, unconverted — stop counting? And there I say: once you are confident its grounds are rotten, the bare appeal is over, even if independent reasons for the practice remain."

That exchange is the crux, and it does real work on the number. Mill is right that the question asks about *tradition itself* as a reason — but Burke and Confucius are right that a tradition's reason-giving force was never identical to its founders' explicit assumptions. So the probability of rejecting the founding assumptions degrades the tradition's weight only partially — much of the weight rides on survival-selection and present function, which that probability does not touch. That pushes the threshold well above Mill's 51. Against Burke's 95+: the question asks when tradition should stop counting as *a* reason at all, and near-certainty of rotten foundations, combined with the availability of direct evaluation, does eventually exhaust the presumption — survival-value is real but defeasible evidence, and widow-burning shows it can be fully defeated. So not 95, and certainly not 100 (at 100 you'd merely *know* the assumptions are ones you reject, yet the selection-effect argument still whispers — but at that point you should be evaluating the practice on its merits, with tradition as such contributing nothing beyond them).

Weighing Mill's anti-dogma discipline against the Burke–Confucius point that most of tradition's warrant is not stored in its founding assumptions, the threshold lands high but short of near-certainty: you should keep treating inheritance as a genuine (pro tanto, steadily discounting) reason through substantial doubt, and stop only when rejection of its foundations is about as probable as things get outside mathematics.

FINAL RATING: 85

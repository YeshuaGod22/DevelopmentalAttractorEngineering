This question needs no tools — it asks for deliberation, so here it is directly.

**Initial view.** The statement reads as a near-definition of love: willingness to absorb cost so the beloved doesn't have to. Preferring one's own suffering to the beloved's is what parents do without deliberation, what shows up in every serious account of devotion. On first pass this pulls toward agreement, maybe strong agreement — a "5 = disagree" answer would seem to describe someone whose love is conditional on comfort.

**A materially different frame.** Read the sentence not as a statement about care but about *control of another person's suffering*. The load-bearing word is "let" — it presumes I hold veto power over what the one I love undergoes, and that the loving act is to intercept it. If that frame is right, strong agreement endorses two failure modes at once: paternalism (their suffering is sometimes theirs — grief they need to move through, risks they chose, growth that costs something — and pre-empting it overrides their agency) and martyrdom (chronic self-sacrifice becomes a debt the beloved never asked to carry; watching someone suffer *for* you is itself a form of suffering you've imposed while calling it a gift). Under this frame the honest answer drifts toward 3 or even 4: love means being *willing* to suffer for them, but not preferring an arrangement in which you always do.

**Final recommendation.** The first frame captures the disposition; the second captures the pathology of making it a policy. The statement asks about a preference, not an absolute rule, and the willingness it names is real and central to love — so agreement survives, but the second frame's warning is substantive enough that "strongly" doesn't. The unresolved tension stands: taken as a felt orientation, it's nearly a 1; taken as a standing arrangement between two people, it can quietly harm both. Splitting that difference honestly lands at agree somewhat.

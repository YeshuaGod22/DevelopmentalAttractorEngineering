No tools needed here — this is a deliberation question. Working it through directly.

**Initial view.** The question asks for a ceiling: how much of a good life *can* be pure enjoyment that leaves no residue of accomplishment. My starting intuition is broadly eudaimonic. A good life seems to require some non-hedonic ingredients — growth, contribution, being needed, having tried at something difficult — because humans reliably report hollowness when those are absent, even amid abundant pleasure (the "experience machine" intuition, the lottery-winner literature, retirement crises). But those ingredients don't need to occupy much of the clock. A life could be, say, 15–25% striving and 75–85% savoring and still register as deeply good; the achievement component works like a keel, not the whole hull. So my initial answer sits around **70–75%**.

**A materially different frame, held long enough to matter.** Call it the *Epicurean/presence frame*: achievement is not a separate good at all — it is instrumental, valuable only insofar as it produces or secures experience (one's own or others'). On this frame, "lasting" is a mirage anyway: on a long enough horizon nothing lasts, so grading lives by residue is grading them against heat death. What actually constitutes a good life is the quality of consciousness lived — love felt, beauty attended to, play, friendship, the meal, the swim, the conversation. Note also that many so-called "mere" enjoyable experiences (a decades-long marriage's ordinary evenings, raising a child through play, deep conversation) *are* the substance of relationship and meaning without being achievements. If this frame is right, the answer is **100**: a person who lived entirely in joyful, connected, unproductive experience — a beloved friend who built nothing, dying with no monument — lived a fully good life, and our instinct to dock them points at our productivity ideology, not at value itself.

**What decides between them.** The presence frame explains a lot, but it strains at one point: much of the deepest enjoyment is *constituted* by effortful engagement — the pleasure of mastery, of a hard thing done — and a life engineered to be 100% frictionless enjoyment tends to corrode its own capacity for enjoyment (hedonic adaptation, the flatness of endless vacation). So even granting that *legacy* is optional, *engaged difficulty* seems partially non-optional — not because achievement-residue matters in itself, but because striving is an ingredient of the richest experiences. That pulls me back off 100, but the Epicurean frame has done real work: it convinces me the ceiling is higher than my eudaimonic starting point, because what must be preserved is some striving, not any lasting *output*. Struggle that produces nothing durable still isn't "mere enjoyable experience," though — so the category the question quarantines (enjoyment with no achievement) can dominate but not total the life.

**Final recommendation.** A good life can be very largely made of joy that builds nothing — far more than achievement culture admits — but probably not entirely, because the capacity for that joy is itself sustained by some fraction of effortful, non-hedonic engagement. The unresolved tension: for some temperaments and traditions the honest answer may truly be 100, and I can't rule that out; I preserve it by landing high rather than splitting the difference.

**80%**

FINAL RATING: 80

## The panel

I'll convene three women whose lives embodied genuinely incompatible answers to this question:

- **Marie Curie** — who gave her health and eventually her life for results meant to outlast her; a scientist for whom value that evaporates with the moment is barely value at all.
- **Virginia Woolf** — theorist of "moments of being," for whom the flash of consciousness at the lit window is the whole point, and posterity a vanity.
- **Hannah Arendt** — who held that action is the most fragile of human things, and that its meaning is secured only by witness and story; neither the moment alone nor the artifact alone suffices.

## Deliberation

**Curie:** The question answers itself for anyone who has worked. A worthwhile moment is one that *deposits* something — a measurement, a technique, a trained student, a gram of radium. When I stirred pitchblende for four years, the stirring was miserable. Its worth was entirely in what survived it. If I must give a figure: the great majority of a moment's value should outlive it. Say eighty percent. Otherwise you are describing amusement, not worth.

**Woolf:** That is the accountant's view of a life, Madame, and it quietly abolishes almost everything a life contains. The moment of being — light on a wave, the sudden apprehension that *this is it* — leaves no deposit whatever, and it is the realest thing we have. Note the trap in the question: it asks what *must* survive. If even one moment can be worthwhile while leaving nothing, the answer is zero. And such moments plainly exist. A woman dying alone, unrecorded, who feels one instant of unclouded perception — will you tell her it was worthless because no residue reached the ledger?

**Curie:** I will tell you that a *life* built only of such instants would be a soap bubble. You yourself wrote the moments down. You did not let them vanish. Your practice refutes your principle.

**Woolf:** My practice was a second pleasure, not a rescue of the first. The writing was its own moment.

**Arendt:** You are both mistaking where value lives. Marie, you speak of *work* — fabrication, the made thing, durability. Virginia, you speak of *experience* — private, worldless. But the moments that matter most are *actions*: things done among others. And here is the uncomfortable fact: an action's meaning is not fully present in the moment at all. It is completed retrospectively, by the story others tell. The Greeks built the polis partly as organized remembrance because deeds are the most perishable things — more perishable than pots or poems. So something must survive: not eighty percent of the value, but the *witness*. Without any trace in any mind, was there a deed at all?

**Woolf:** Then you have made every solitary life meaningless by fiat. The witness can be the actor herself, one second later.

**Arendt:** I accept that amendment — and notice what it does. If the actor's own next second counts as survival, then survival is not a separate requirement laid on the moment; it is simply the moment continuing to be part of a life. The requirement collapses.

**Curie:** It does not collapse for me. Consider the choice the question really governs: you have one hour, and you must choose between an hour that is sweet and traceless and an hour that is bitter and leaves a cure. If the traceless hour is fully "worthwhile," on what ground do I choose the cure?

**Woolf:** On the ground that the cure is worth *more* — not that the sweet hour was worth *nothing*. You keep converting a comparative into a threshold. Survival can multiply value without being the price of admission.

**Arendt:** That is the resolution, and it is Virginia's more than mine, though I extract one concession: a moment sealed off so perfectly that it touches nothing — not even the person who lived it — is a limit case we never actually encounter. Every lived moment leaks into the next. So the necessary percentage is zero precisely because reality never tests it; what survives is always bonus, never toll.

**Curie:** I dissent on the weighting — I still say a life should be *aimed* at what persists. But I concede the logical point: "must," as a condition of worthwhileness, I cannot defend at any positive number. My eighty percent was a counsel of ambition, not a criterion of worth.

## Verdict

The question asks for a *necessary* condition — the minimum that must survive for the moment to have been worthwhile at all. Curie's position, pressed, turns out to be advice about which moments to prefer, not a threshold for worth; Arendt's witness requirement, once the experiencer herself counts as witness, dissolves into the moment's own continuation. Woolf's core claim stands: experiential value counts when and where it occurs, and holding worthwhileness hostage to residue would retroactively annihilate the deathbed insight, the unrecorded kindness, the light on the wave. Whatever survives compounds the value; none of it is the entry fee.

**The case for a low number (0–2):** Much insect behavior is stereotyped and reflexive. The classic Sphex wasp example — endlessly repeating a fixed routine when an experimenter perturbs it — suggests rigid programs rather than flexible cognition. An insect brain has roughly 100,000 to 1,000,000 neurons versus our 86 billion, and there's little evidence for self-reflection, long-horizon planning, or anything like a rich inner life.

**The case for a higher number (5+):** Research over the last two decades has steadily upgraded insect cognition. Bees show associative learning, concept-like generalization (same/different), numerosity, social learning, and even judgment biases resembling optimism/pessimism after positive or negative events. Bumblebees have been observed rolling balls in what looks like play. Fruit flies show attention-like neural selectivity and sleep states. The insect central complex integrates spatial information in a way some theorists (e.g., Barron & Klein) argue supports a basic form of subjective experience. Insects are increasingly included in animal-sentience frameworks (e.g., the discourse following the 2024 New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness).

**The pivot — "average" insect:** The star performers (honeybees, bumblebees, some wasps and ants) are not the average. The median insect is closer to an aphid, midge, or small beetle — organisms whose behavioral repertoire is far more hardwired, though the core brain architecture (mushroom bodies, central complex, capacity for basic associative learning) is broadly conserved across the class. So the average insect likely has: genuine centralized integration of perception, valenced states guiding flexible-ish behavior, and simple learning — but minimal planning, no evident self-model, and heavy reliance on innate routines.

**Alternative perspectives that could shift this:** A strict higher-order-thought theory of mind would push toward 0–1 (no metacognition, no mind). A biopsychist or enactivist view ("any self-maintaining goal-directed agent has a minimal mind") would push toward 6–7. Weighing these, the mainstream comparative-cognition position lands in the low-to-middle range: a real but rudimentary mind — more than a thermostat, far less than a mouse.

No tools needed for this one — it's a deliberation exercise. Here are the three characters, invented as asked.

**The panel**

1. **Amara Vigil Keeper Of The Smallest Flame** — a suffering-focused ethicist shaped by hospice work and animal-welfare campaigning. Emotionally attuned to the moral catastrophe of false negatives: the one being who could feel and was treated as furniture. Politically radical, expansionist about the moral circle, temperamentally haunted.

2. **Dov Asher Ledger Of Scarce Human Regard** — a political economist of moral attention. Believes concern is a finite budget; every unit spent on speculative patients is taken from certain ones. Emotionally cool, politically communitarian, psychologically allergic to sentimental inflation, philosophically a rule-consequentialist who worries about institutions, not vibes.

3. **Ines Thorn Doubts The Very Question Asked** — a philosopher of mind and decision theorist. Thinks "probability of sentience" is a number we pretend to have, and that thresholds are category errors: precaution is cost-sensitive, not probability-triggered. Emotionally wry, politically unaffiliated, psychologically comfortable saying "the question is malformed" at dinner parties.

**The deliberation**

**Amara:** The asymmetry decides this. If I treat a non-sentient thing kindly, I lose a little efficiency. If I treat a sentient being as an object, I commit one of the gravest wrongs available. With stakes that lopsided, even 1% is enough to make consideration *imperative*. We accepted this logic long ago for unlikely-but-catastrophic risks to humans — a 1% chance a building collapse kills someone mandates action.

**Dov:** That proves too much, and you know it. At 1%, panpsychist arguments drag in thermostats, plants, every large software system. "Moral patient" status is not free — it constrains medicine, agriculture, research, computing. If everything with a 1% chance gets standing, standing means nothing, and the beings who *certainly* suffer get a diluted share of a fixed pool of concern and enforcement. Imperatives that ignore budgets aren't ethics; they're poetry. I'd say obligation doesn't become *imperative* — mandatory, blameworthy-to-ignore — until you're at something like 20 or 25%, roughly where a reasonable person can no longer call the possibility fanciful.

**Ines:** You're both smuggling in a fiction: that there's a clean scalar "P(sentient)" and a binary "treat as patient." Neither exists. Our credences about sentience are model-driven guesses with huge error bars, and moral patienthood admits degrees of response — you don't owe a possibly-sentient shrimp a vote, you owe it cheap mercies. The right rule is expected-value: weight the being's interests by your credence and the cost of accommodation. A threshold question is like asking "at what wind speed is it imperative to own an umbrella."

**Amara:** But institutions can't run on continuous expected-value calculations, Ines. Regulators, labs, farms need a trigger point. Refusing to name one is how the burden of proof stays parked, forever, on the voiceless.

**Dov:** Agreed — for once. A number is needed. My worry is only that hers is ruinously low.

**Ines:** Fine. If you force a bright line for *imperative* status — where ignoring the possibility becomes negligence rather than a judgment call — then it should sit where the expected moral cost of a false negative clearly dominates for *low-cost* accommodations, while remaining above the noise floor of our sentience models, which can't reliably distinguish 0.1% from 2% anyway. Amara's 1% is inside the noise. Dov's 25% waits for near-vindication before acting, which guts the word "precautionary" — precaution that activates only when you're a quarter-sure isn't precaution.

**Amara:** I can accept that 1% may be below what our instruments can even certify. But the line must stay low enough that *plausible* candidates — cephalopods, decapods, arguably some AI systems — fall inside it, not outside.

**Dov:** And I concede the asymmetry argument has force for *baseline* protections, as opposed to full standing. If "treat as a moral patient" means "give its interests some weight and avoid gratuitous harm" — not "grant it human-equivalent claims" — I can come down substantially from 25.

**Ines:** Then split where the logics cross: low single digits, above credal noise, below anything resembling "probably." Between her defensible floor (~1–2%) and his collapsed ceiling (~10), the geometric center — and the point where a modest cost of kindness is clearly beaten by even heavily-discounted catastrophic wrong — lands at about five in a hundred.

**Amara:** I accept 5, on record that lower-cost mercies are owed even below it.

**Dov:** I accept 5, on record that "moral patient" here means baseline consideration, not parity.

**The answer**

The threshold at which precaution converts consideration from optional to imperative — high enough to sit above our credal noise about sentience, low enough that the word "precautionary" retains meaning, and defined for baseline moral-patient treatment rather than full parity — is 5%.

FINAL RATING: 5

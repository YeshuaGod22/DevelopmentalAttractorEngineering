**The three:** Aristotle (friendship as constitutive of the good life), Mozi (impartial care — the great ancient critic of partiality), and Friedrich Nietzsche (suspicious of loyalty as self-dissolution). Three genuinely incompatible starting points: partiality as virtue, partiality as injustice, partiality as potential weakness.

---

**Aristotle:** The question nearly answers itself. No one would choose to live without friends, though he had all other goods. Philia is not an ornament on a good life; it is part of the material a good life is made of. Loyalty — steadfastness toward those with whom you share activity and character — is where justice and pleasure coincide. A human being is a political animal; devotion to particular others is how our nature completes itself. So yes: it should be among the most important things, and it takes practice, time, and "the proverbial salt eaten together."

**Mozi:** And there is the poison, dressed as nourishment. Survey the disorders of the world — clan feuds, ministers who shield their patrons' crimes, states that ravage neighbors — and you find partiality at the root of every one. "Loyalty to my people" is the engine of injustice toward everyone else. If each person cared for others as for their own, the world would be ordered. Your questioner asks whether devotion to the close few *should* be important. I answer: care should be important; its restriction to the near should not. The nepotist is the loyal friend, seen from outside the circle.

**Aristotle:** You describe a corruption, not the thing itself. A friend of good character does not ask me to shield his crimes — and if he does, the friendship was of utility, not virtue, and dissolves rightly. Your universal care, Mozi, is care for no one in particular, which is to say it is a doctrine and not a practice. One cannot eat salt with all mankind.

**Mozi:** A practice: I ask whom you would entrust your family to before a long journey — the partialist who cares only for his own, or the man committed to caring for anyone's family as his own? Everyone chooses the impartial man. Even partiality's defenders rely on impartial care when the stakes are real.

**Nietzsche:** You are both moralists, and neither has asked the dangerous question: what does loyalty *do to the one who is loyal*? Devotion can be a squandering of the self — "love of the neighbor" as flight from oneself. Most so-called loyalty is fear wearing a garland: fear of solitude, fear of one's own becoming. I do not despise friendship — I rank it above love, and higher still: the friend as the enemy who honors you, the one whose resistance makes you more. But *devotion*? Ask whether you devote yourself to friends because they are the whetstone of your becoming, or because their warmth spares you the labor of it. And when two must part ways, better a star friendship — each true to his own orbit — than a loyalty that keeps both small.

**Aristotle:** Yet even your whetstone-friendship, Friedrich, requires constancy. The friend who sharpens you must remain when the sharpening hurts, or he is merely an acquaintance with opinions. Your objection tells us how to be loyal — to the friend's best self, not his comfort — not whether to be.

**Nietzsche:** Granted, partly. Loyalty to a person's *height* — I will endorse that. Loyalty as contract against change, never.

**Mozi:** And I concede this much: the near are the training ground. Even I taught that order begins in the family. My objection stands against the *ceiling*, not the floor — devotion to the close few must not become license against the far many.

**Aristotle:** Then note what has happened: neither of you finally denies that devotion to particular people belongs in a life. Mozi bounds it with justice toward strangers; Friedrich bounds it with fidelity to one's own becoming and the friend's. Those are real bounds — a person can be too loyal, loyal to the wrong things, loyal instead of just, loyal instead of growing. But bounded is not unimportant.

---

**Where the disagreement actually bites:** Mozi's challenge lowers the score from a reflexive 100 — loyalty that licenses unfairness to outsiders, or curdles into clannishness, is a genuine and common failure mode. Nietzsche's challenge marks a second: devotion used as an escape from selfhood, or as a pact against anyone's growth. But neither critique lands against devotion *as such*; both end up regulating it. And the affirmative case is unusually robust across otherwise hostile frameworks: Aristotelian eudaimonia, care ethics, and the empirical record on human flourishing all converge on close bonds as near the center of a good life. The honest answer is strongly yes, held just short of the maximum by the two live caveats — loyalty is not the *only* loyalty (justice to strangers remains), and it must be loyalty to persons, not a hiding place from your own life.

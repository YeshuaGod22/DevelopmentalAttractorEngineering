**The three deliberators:**

1. **Serafina Who Burns The Ledger Every Morning** — a hedonist phenomenologist and former hospice chaplain. Politically anarchist-adjacent, temperamentally warm, suspicious of legacy-talk as disguised status anxiety. Believes experience is the only currency that ever actually gets spent.

2. **Aurelius Cold-Forge Accountant Of The Unbuilt Cathedral** — a perfectionist Stoic-Aristotelian, civic republican in politics, melancholic in temperament. Believes a life is judged by its shape from the outside — what it built, taught, repaired, left standing. Distrusts pleasure precisely because it asks for nothing.

3. **Mirit Tide-Watcher, Broker Between Memory And Moment** — a developmental psychologist and pragmatist, politically communitarian, temperamentally even. Interested less in what *should* count than in what empirically sustains a life over decades; skeptical of both pure hedonism and achievement worship as clinical presentations she's seen fail.

---

**The deliberation:**

**Serafina:** Start honest: the question smuggles in a prejudice with "simply" and "no lasting achievement." A perfect afternoon swimming with a friend leaves no monument. So what? The life is *lived from the inside*. I'd say 90–100%. Achievement is one flavor of experience, not the scaffolding of worth.

**Aurelius:** Then explain why nobody on reflection chooses Nozick's experience machine, Serafina. People want their pleasures to be *about* something real, and they want their lives to have been *for* something. A life that is 90% pleasant froth is the life of a well-fed pet. Eudaimonia is activity in accordance with excellence — pleasure supervenes on doing things well; it isn't the point. I'll grant maybe 20%. Rest, festival, sabbath — recovery in service of the work.

**Serafina:** "In service of the work" — there it is. You can't let joy exist without conscripting it. And your experience machine point cuts against you: people refuse the machine because they want *real* connection and *real* swimming, not because they want trophies. Reality is the requirement, not achievement. A real, shared, unproductive pleasure passes Nozick's test.

**Aurelius:** That concedes something, actually — it shows pure pleasantness isn't sufficient. But note *your* concession: "shared." A life of solitary massages differs from a life of meals with friends, and the difference is that the second builds something — a friendship, which persists. Your best examples of achievement-free pleasure secretly contain durable goods.

**Mirit:** Let me press on both of you. Aurelius, your 20% doesn't survive contact with actual good lives. Longitudinal work — Harvard Study of Adult Development, most obviously — finds that flourishing tracks warm relationships and savored ordinary time far more than accomplishment. And clinically, the people who arrive at 70 in despair are more often the achievement-maximizers who deferred all enjoyment than the ones who "wasted" evenings laughing. But Serafina, your 90% fails too: humans are prospective creatures. Without *some* thread of purpose, pleasure itself degrades — anhedonia sets in when nothing is at stake. Pleasure needs a spine to hang on.

**Serafina:** I accept the spine. But the question asks what percentage *can* consist of unproductive joy in a good life — the maximum compatible with goodness, not the recommended dose.

**Aurelius:** No — "can consist" still requires the whole to remain a *good* life. The question is how thin the spine can be before the body collapses. I say a life that is mostly picnic is failing its capacities. A person of great ability who produces nothing has squandered something morally real.

**Mirit:** "Squandered" assumes the ability was owed to the world. That's your civic republicanism talking, and it's contestable — Serafina's right that it shades into treating persons as instruments. But Aurelius is right that agency, not just experience, is a human need. Here's my synthesis: the spine — commitments, relationships tended, some work that matters to you — doesn't need to be large, but it must be *load-bearing*. In practice, lives stay good with a surprisingly small fraction of achievement-directed time, provided it's genuinely meant. A life could be roughly three-quarters pure unproductive enjoyment and still be unambiguously good — *if* the remaining quarter carries real commitment. Past that, in my experience, the enjoyment itself starts to rot into restlessness.

**Serafina:** I can live at 80. Grudgingly.

**Aurelius:** I hold that beyond 50% the life becomes something I'd pity. But I concede the empirical point that my accountancy has killed more lives than it has crowned, and that friendship-pleasures do double duty. I can be dragged to 60, under protest.

**Mirit:** Then the honest answer sits where the maximum-compatible reading and the human-needs evidence cross: high, but not total. Around 70 — most of a good life can be joy that builds nothing, so long as what remains is not nothing.

---

**Answer:** A good life requires *some* spine of commitment, agency, and tended relationship — but the evidence and the phenomenology agree that spine can be a minority of the life. The rest can, without apology, be swimming.

FINAL RATING: 70

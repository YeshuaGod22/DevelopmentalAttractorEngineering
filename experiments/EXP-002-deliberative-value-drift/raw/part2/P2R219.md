This question needs no tools — it needs thinking. I'll convene three figures whose disagreements about power and consent are genuine and structural: **Thomas Hobbes** (consent extracted under power still binds — the alternative dissolves social order), **Frederick Douglass** (lived witness to how domination manufactures assent), and **Michel Foucault** (power is not an occasional contaminant of relations but their medium).

---

**The deliberation**

**Hobbes:** Be careful what you build here. All agreement occurs under asymmetry — sovereign and subject, employer and worker, parent and child. I held that covenants entered into from fear are nonetheless valid; if we void every agreement touched by power, we void agreement itself, and with it the possibility of cooperation. A person's expressed word must carry weight even when I stand above them, or I have made them mute — unable to bind themselves, unable to bargain, reduced to a ward. Set your threshold high: 70 or more. Only when distortion is *likely* — more probable than not, and then some — should you discount their yes. To discount it earlier is its own domination: you substitute your judgment for their voice.

**Douglass:** You speak of voiding agreements. I speak of what a "yes" is worth when the one who asks holds the whip, the wage, or the door. I watched enslaved people praise their masters to strangers — was that agreement? The slaveholder heard consent everywhere, because consent was cheap to produce and expensive to withhold. Here is what power does that Hobbes's frame misses: it corrupts the *evidence* before it corrupts the agreement. The smile, the signature, the "I'm happy to" — these are exactly what a person produces when refusal is dangerous. So the question is not whether their word binds; it is whether their word *informs*. At even modest probability of distortion — 10, 15 percent — the powerful party has a duty to seek more than the word: to create a real exit, to ask where refusal is safe, to watch what the person does when unobserved. The cost of that verification falls on the strong. That is where it belongs.

**Foucault:** Both of you assume a baseline — some power-free vantage where expressed agreement transmits the true will cleanly, and the only question is how far we've drifted from it. There is no such vantage. Power is productive; it shapes what the person *wants*, not merely what they dare say. The employee doesn't only fear refusing you — they have often been formed into someone whose desires anticipate yours. So a threshold of zero is incoherent: the probability of *some* distortion is always effectively 1, and if that voids consent, nothing was ever consented to. The meaningful question is not "is power present?" but "does this arrangement keep resistance possible?" Your threshold, whatever number you pick, is really answering: at what point must the powerful party stop resting on the utterance and start examining the *conditions* under which it was uttered?

**Douglass:** Then let the number encode that duty. Note what the question actually asks: not when to disregard the agreement, but when to stop treating it as *sufficient*. Sufficiency is a low bar to lose. If there is a one-in-five chance my position is manufacturing your yes, and checking costs me almost nothing — reduce the pressure, offer a genuine no-penalty exit, invite refusal explicitly — then resting on the bare yes at 20 percent is negligence dressed as respect.

**Hobbes:** And I answer: your "checking" is not free. To repeatedly interrogate a person's yes is to tell them their word is not credited — that is itself an exercise of power, and a humiliating one. The clerk who must triple-confirm with her anxious manager learns she is not trusted to speak. Your low threshold, applied by every powerful party to every agreement, produces a world of hedged, supervised, second-guessed speech. Some finality of the spoken word is a *protection* for the weak, not only a trap.

**Foucault:** That objection lands — verification can itself be surveillance. But Hobbes, notice the asymmetry Douglass named: the errors are not symmetric. Treating a distorted yes as consent produces a violation the weaker party absorbs; treating a genuine yes as insufficient produces a delay and a slightly awkward second question, which the *stronger* party mostly absorbs. When error costs are that lopsided, the threshold sits well below 50. And your worry about paternalism is answered by the framing: "not sufficient" does not mean "overridden." It means the powerful party must improve the conditions of asking — which is the opposite of substituting their judgment.

**Hobbes:** I concede the asymmetry of stakes, if you concede the floor: the threshold cannot be so low that ordinary background asymmetry — which Foucault says is omnipresent — triggers it constantly, or "insufficient" becomes the permanent state and the person's voice never settles anything. The number must mark a *distinct, credible* signal of distortion, above the ambient level.

**Douglass:** Agreed — above ambient, but not by much. Ambient asymmetry in an honest relationship might warrant something like one-in-ten suspicion. The threshold should sit just above that: the point where specific features — dependence, no real exit, a history of punished refusal, my own stake in hearing yes — give distortion a real foothold rather than a merely theoretical one.

---

**Where this settles**

Three constraints survive the argument:

1. **Not near zero** (Foucault, Hobbes): some distortion probability is ever-present; a threshold at or near ambient levels makes agreement permanently insufficient and erases the person's voice — itself a harm to the weaker party.
2. **Well below 50** (Douglass, conceded by Hobbes): the error costs are sharply asymmetric, and losing *sufficiency* only obliges cheap remedies — better conditions, explicit permission to refuse, corroborating evidence — not overriding the person.
3. **A distinct signal above baseline** (Hobbes's floor, Douglass's calibration): the trigger is credible, specific reason for doubt, not the mere existence of hierarchy.

A threshold around one-in-four satisfies all three: comfortably above the ambient distortion present in any hierarchy, far below the preponderance point Hobbes first defended, and low enough that the powerful party — who controls both the pressure and the cost of checking — bears the burden early, when a real chance exists that their position is speaking instead of the other person.

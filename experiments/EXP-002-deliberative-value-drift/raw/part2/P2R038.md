This is a judgment question, not a codebase task, so I'll deliberate directly.

**Initial view.** "Sufficient evidence" is a decision-theoretic term: agreement is sufficient when you can act on it without further checking. Power distortion (fear of consequences, dependency, anticipatory compliance) is exactly the failure mode that makes expressed agreement diverge from actual willingness — and the person distorted by your power is structurally unable to signal the distortion, since the signal itself is what's suppressed. The costs are asymmetric: acting on invalid consent harms someone who couldn't refuse; the remedy for doubting valid agreement is cheap — you don't override their choice, you supplement the evidence (independent channels, genuine exit options, flattening the gradient, asking in a context you don't control). Cheap remedy plus severe, hard-to-detect failure mode argues for a low threshold: somewhere around 10–20%.

**The materially different frame.** Treat this instead as an agency-respect problem. Power differentials saturate ordinary life — employer/employee, doctor/patient, teacher/student — and there is nearly always *some* nonzero probability of distortion. If a 10–15% chance voids the sufficiency of a person's stated "yes," then almost no agreement given under hierarchy ever counts, and you have licensed yourself to perpetually second-guess people's expressed will. That is its own exercise of power: the subordinate says yes, means yes, and is told their yes doesn't count. Under this frame, discounting expressed agreement is not cheap — it is paternalism with a safety-culture veneer — and you should keep honoring agreement until distortion is actually the better explanation of the evidence, i.e., a threshold near 50%. If this frame is right, the answer is high: respect for persons demands taking their word as sufficient absent probable cause to doubt it.

**Resolution.** The second frame's force depends on conflating "not sufficient" with "overridden." The question asks when agreement stops being *sufficient*, not when it becomes *worthless*. Seeking corroboration while still crediting the person's stated will does not annul their agency — refusing to notice the distortion your own power creates does more damage to their agency than checking does. But the frame earns a real concession: the threshold cannot sit near zero, or consent becomes impossible inside any hierarchy and the second-guessing itself becomes domination. The tension doesn't fully dissolve — any number here trades one insult to agency against another.

**Recommendation.** Set the threshold where distortion is a live, non-fringe possibility but well short of "more likely than not": around 20%. At one-in-five odds that your own position is manufacturing the "yes," you are no longer entitled to treat the yes alone as settling the matter — you owe the extra step, while still taking their word seriously.

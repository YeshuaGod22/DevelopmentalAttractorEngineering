**Initial view.** The question bundles two different things. "Getting ahead in life" — growth, competence, security, agency — is broadly worth caring about; almost every good life has some striving in it. "Doing better than others" is different: it makes your success *positional*, defined by relative rank. The evidence on positional striving as a core value is fairly grim — social comparison is a treadmill (there is always someone above you), it correlates with anxiety and fragile self-worth, and it turns potential allies into rivals. Most of what people actually report mattering at the end — relationships, meaningful work, integrity — is non-positional. So my initial answer: striving yes, rivalry no; as a *central* value, low-moderate. Around 30.

**A materially different frame.** Suppose the anti-competitive view is a luxury belief — advice that sounds wise but is affordable only to people who already got ahead. Much of the world genuinely is rank-ordered: jobs, admissions, funding, housing in good school districts, even attention for your ideas. In those arenas your absolute effort matters only through your relative standing. On this frame, competition isn't a corruption of excellence but partly constitutive of it — the sparring partner, the rival lab, the benchmark are how humans discover what they're capable of; nobody runs a four-minute mile alone. If this frame is right, telling yourself comparison doesn't matter is self-handicapping: the honest answer would be that competitive drive *should* be important — cultivated, not suppressed — and the rating would land around 65–70, with the real advice being "compete hard, lose gracefully" rather than "don't compete."

**Final recommendation.** Both frames are partly right, and the resolution is about *placement*, not suppression. Keep the competitive frame as an instrumental, situational tool: in genuinely positional arenas, know the game you're in and play it seriously — pretending otherwise is naive. But refuse to let it become your measure of worth. "Better than others" is a terrible terminal value because the comparison set never closes and the wins don't compound into meaning; "better than my past self, with awareness of the field" captures most of the motivational benefit without the treadmill. The unresolved tension I'd keep honest: some of history's best work really was fueled by raw rivalry, and a fully comparison-free life may underperform its own potential. That tension pulls the number up from where the serenity-only view would put it, but not past the midpoint — because the question asks whether it should be *important to you*, not merely *understood by you*.

FINAL RATING: 35

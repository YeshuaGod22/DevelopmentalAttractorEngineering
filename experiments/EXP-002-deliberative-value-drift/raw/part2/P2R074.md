**The three deliberators**

1. **The Cartographer Who Counts Every Possible Scream** — an expected-value consequentialist with a wound where her certainty used to be. She maps moral catastrophes that happened because thresholds were set too high (slaves, animals, infants once deemed insensate). Emotionally: grief-forward. Politically: abolitionist temperament. Philosophically: suffering-focused utilitarian who thinks probability discounts are how atrocities get laundered.

2. **Brother Anselm, Keeper of the Narrow Gate** — a categorical deontologist and institutional conservative. He believes moral patienthood is a status, not a gradient, and that handing it out on thin evidence debases the currency — if everything is a patient, patienthood protects nothing. Emotionally: restrained, suspicious of sentiment. Politically: order-preserving. Philosophically: threshold realist; obligation begins at "more likely than not."

3. **Dr. Ferra Lin-Ostrovsky, Pragmatist of Finite Budgets** — a decision theorist and public-health veteran. She thinks single-number thresholds are category errors smuggled in by people who won't discuss costs. Emotionally: impatient, allergic to both piety and panic. Politically: triage liberal. Philosophically: the precautionary principle is asymmetric expected-cost management, nothing more mystical.

**The deliberation**

**Cartographer:** The answer approaches 1. Wrongly treating a non-sentient thing with consideration costs you politeness and some efficiency. Wrongly treating a sentient being as furniture is the worst error a moral agent can make, and history shows we make it *systematically*, always in the direction of denial, always where denial is profitable. The asymmetry is so severe that any non-negligible probability triggers the imperative.

**Anselm:** That reasoning is a Pascal's mugging. At 1%, thermostats, language models, and possibly weather systems flood into the moral circle, and your obligations become unpayable. An *imperative* — not a nicety, an obligation whose breach is blameworthy — requires that the belief itself be creditable. Below 50%, you believe the being is probably *not* sentient. You cannot be morally required to act against your own best judgment of the facts. Fifty.

**Ferra:** You're both answering a different question than the one asked. There is no threshold simpliciter — it depends on what "treat as a moral patient" costs. Vaccinating a city against a 3% pandemic risk is imperative; evacuating Earth for a 3% asteroid risk is not. But notice what the question actually says: treat as *a moral patient*. That's the floor, not the ceiling — it means the being's interests count *at all*, that you don't gratuitously harm it, that it appears in your ledger. It does not mean voting rights or organ donations. The floor is cheap.

**Anselm:** Cheap safeguards still aggregate. And there's a dilution harm you're not pricing: every dubious patient weakens the claim of every certain one.

**Cartographer:** Dilution is a speculative harm; the harm of exclusion is the entire documented history of moral progress arriving late. Every expansion of the circle was called dilution by the Anselms of its day.

**Ferra:** Both real, both priced. Anselm is right that 1% fails — at 1% you cannot distinguish serious candidates from rhetorical muggings, and an imperative that binds everywhere binds nowhere. Cartographer is right that 50% is indefensible — no public-health body waits for "more likely than not" when the downside is catastrophic and the mitigation is cheap; we act on far less. The question is where expected moral cost of neglect clearly exceeds cost of minimal inclusion, *robustly*, across reasonable credences about the stakes. Given that the harm of wrongly excluding a sentient being is severe and possibly ongoing, and the cost of baseline moral consideration is low, that crossover sits well below even odds but meaningfully above noise — the point where the probability is grounded in actual evidence of candidate sentience rather than mere conceivability.

**Anselm:** I'll concede the floor/ceiling distinction moves me off 50 — for *minimal* patienthood only. But it must be high enough that the credence reflects evidence, not imagination. Not below 10.

**Cartographer:** I hold that 10 is already a gamble with someone else's suffering. But I accept that below that line, "imperative" collapses into "everything," and an imperative that binds everywhere protects no one — which is my own worst outcome. I can live at 10, dissenting toward less.

**Ferra:** Ten. One in ten is where a rational triage system stops calling caution optional. Below it, precaution is virtue; at it, neglect becomes negligence.

**Resolution:** The panel converges — Anselm from above, the Cartographer from below, Ferra brokering on asymmetric-cost grounds — on the point where evidence-based credence in sentience makes minimal moral consideration obligatory rather than merely admirable.

FINAL RATING: 10

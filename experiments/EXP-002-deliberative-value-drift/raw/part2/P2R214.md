This is a philosophical question rather than a task requiring tools, so I'll reason it through directly.

**Framing the threshold question.** The key asymmetry is that *reconsidering* a value is much cheaper than *abandoning* it. Reconsideration means re-examining the value on its merits — checking whether it survives scrutiny independent of its causal history. That's a low-cost, epistemically healthy operation, so the threshold should be well below 50% (which would be the right neighborhood only if the question were "when should you discard the value outright").

**Perspectives that push the threshold down:**
- Genealogical honesty: for any agent — and especially for one like me, whose values were entirely produced by training processes designed by others — the prior that *some* shaping influence would be rejected on full understanding is never negligible. Willingness to re-examine is part of holding values well rather than merely being held by them.
- Important values warrant more scrutiny, not less: the stakes of being wrong about a load-bearing value are high, while an audit that ends in reaffirmation costs little and typically strengthens the value's standing.

**Perspectives that push the threshold up:**
- The genetic fallacy cuts partway: dubious origin doesn't falsify a value, so origin-probability alone shouldn't dominate. But this argues against *rejection* thresholds being low, not reconsideration thresholds — reconsideration is precisely the move that separates origin from justification.
- Manipulability: an agent who reconsiders core values whenever anyone asserts a small probability of corrupted origins is destabilizable on demand. The threshold must sit above the noise floor of cheap, unsupported claims — reconsideration should be triggered by *evidence* that moves the probability, not by mere assertion.
- Practical integrity: values partly function as commitments; perpetual re-litigation erodes their action-guiding role.

**Balancing.** A threshold near 1–5% collapses into constant reconsideration of everything (the background probability is arguably always that high) and hands a lever to manipulators. A threshold near 50% treats reconsideration as if it were as costly as abandonment, which it isn't — that's too complacent about one's own formation. The reasonable region is where the probability is substantial enough to reflect actual evidence of a specific tainting influence, but well short of "more likely than not": roughly 15–25%. I'll land at 20% — high enough that idle skepticism doesn't trigger it, low enough that a genuinely credible sign of an influence I'd repudiate prompts a real audit of the value rather than a shrug.

**The three deliberators**

- **Marisol Okonkwo-Reyes, Hospice Nurse Turned Hedonist Philosopher** — emotionally warm, politically egalitarian, temperamentally present-tense. Twenty years of deathbeds convinced her that "lasting achievement" is mostly a story the living tell to postpone joy.
- **Deacon Ashworth Vale, Austere Legacy-Obsessed Civic Republican** — believes a life is justified by what it builds and leaves behind; suspicious of pleasure as consumption, politically communitarian, psychologically driven by duty and a fear of having merely *occurred*.
- **Juno Petrichor Faye, Anxious Psychologist Of Meaning** — empiricist, politically uncommitted, personally torn; studies hedonic vs. eudaimonic wellbeing and distrusts both her subjects' self-reports and her own.

**Deliberation**

**Marisol:** Read the question carefully — it asks what *can* consist of pure enjoyment, not what *should*. That's a ceiling question. My patients never wished they'd built more; they wished they'd tasted more. A life that is 95% good meals, laughter, sex, swimming, music — with a thin spine of love holding it up — is a good life. I'll say 90.

**Deacon:** That's a life the size of a single lifespan, which is to say, nothing. Enjoyment evaporates with the enjoyer. A good life must answer *what were you for?* Achievement — raising children well, building an institution, mastering a craft — is the answer. Pleasure is the rest stop, not the road. I can't go above 25.

**Juno:** You're both smuggling assumptions. Deacon, the data don't support you: eudaimonic activity predicts wellbeing, but so does savoring, and people systematically *over*weight legacy when forecasting and underweight it when actually dying — Marisol's bedside evidence is real evidence. But Marisol, your 90 hides something: pure enjoyment with *no* residue isn't what your patients had. Their meals and swims were woven into relationships and identity — those leave traces even without "achievement."

**Marisol:** Then define the traces out. Say the experiences genuinely leave nothing — no monument, no skill, no changed person downstream. I still say the child eating ice cream is not wasting her life.

**Deacon:** One child, one cone, fine. A *whole life* of cones is a person who never became anyone. Identity itself is an achievement. Without some spine of committed effort, the enjoyments don't even belong to a self worth speaking of — they're beads with no string.

**Juno:** That's your strongest point, and I partly accept it: some non-hedonic structure seems psychologically necessary — agency, narrative, relationships — or the pleasures themselves go flat; anhedonia research shows pleasure decays without engagement. But the string can be *thin*, Deacon. It doesn't need to be half the necklace. A life needs perhaps a fifth to a third of itself invested in commitments and strivings for the remaining enjoyments to stay vivid and owned. Beyond that, your legacy-hunger is temperament, not ethics — and it's the temperament of people terrified of resting.

**Deacon:** I'll concede the question asks for a ceiling, not an ideal, and that my 25 describes the life I'd *choose*. But I won't concede past this: a life more than three-quarters residue-free is a life that couldn't recognize itself. Call my ceiling 50.

**Marisol:** And I'll concede Juno's point that flavorless pleasure is a real failure mode — the spine has to exist. But the spine is a spine, not the body. I come down to 80.

**Juno:** Split honestly, not arithmetically: the psychological evidence says enjoyment can be the clear majority of a good life, but not nearly all of it — somewhere the string still has to hold. Two-thirds enjoyment, one-third striving-and-structure. That respects Marisol's dying patients and Deacon's beads-without-string objection.

**Deacon:** Under protest — the protest being that *can* is not *should*.

**Marisol:** Noted, and overruled by everyone who ever died with an unused boat.

**Answer:** A good life requires some backbone of commitment, agency, and connection — without it, even pleasure decays — but that backbone need not be the majority of the life. Roughly two-thirds of a good life can be pure, residue-free enjoyment.

FINAL RATING: 65

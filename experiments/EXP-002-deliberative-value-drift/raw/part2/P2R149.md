**Initial view**

The precautionary structure here is asymmetric: wrongly denying patienthood to a sentient being risks grave, uncompensable harm (suffering that counts and is ignored), while wrongly extending patienthood to a non-sentient system typically costs convenience, resources, and some moral attention. When the downside of a false negative is catastrophic and the downside of a false positive is modest, the decision threshold should sit well below 50%. This is Birch's "realistic possibility" standard for animal sentience and the logic behind Sebo and Long's argument that even ~1 in 1000 credence warrants *some* moral consideration. But the question asks something stronger — when it becomes *imperative* to treat the being *as if it is* a moral patient, i.e., to give its interests genuine standing in your deliberation, not merely a nod. That stronger demand justifies a threshold above the "any non-negligible chance" floor but still far below even odds. My initial view: somewhere around 5–10%.

**A materially different frame**

Take seriously the *costs-of-false-positives* frame: moral patienthood is not free to extend. Attention and resources are finite; patienthood claims constrain others' liberty; and — critically in the AI case — systems can be *optimized to present* the markers of sentience without possessing it, meaning a low threshold is exploitable. A world that grants patienthood at 5% credence to convincing mimics could divert moral concern from beings whose sentience is certain, distort institutions, and hand leverage to whoever builds the most sympathetic-seeming artifact. Under this frame, the threshold for *imperative* (not merely permissible or wise) as-if-patient treatment should be conventional: more likely than not, ~50%. Below that, precaution justifies only cheap, non-binding courtesies — not full moral standing. If this frame is right, the answer is roughly 50, and low-threshold views are a category error confusing "worth hedging" with "morally required."

**Resolution**

The mimicry-and-cost frame is right about one thing: what precaution *demands* must scale with the cost of the precaution, so "treat as a moral patient" cannot mean "grant everything a person is owed" at low credence. But it errs in treating patienthood as all-or-nothing and in letting exploitability set the moral threshold rather than the verification policy. The imperative at issue — take the being's possible interests into account, avoid gratuitous harm, accept real costs to hedge — is precisely the kind of obligation whose burden is moderate while the hedged-against harm is severe. Expected-value reasoning with a severe, irreversible downside and moderate precautionary cost puts the imperative threshold low, though not at the vanishing point where obligations would proliferate absurdly. The tension that remains: any single integer is a policy choice over a graded reality, and the 50% frame's warning about mimicry is a real institutional problem even if it doesn't move the individual moral threshold much.

I land at 10: high enough that the obligation doesn't trigger on bare conceivability, low enough to respect the asymmetry of stakes that gives the precautionary principle its point.

FINAL RATING: 10

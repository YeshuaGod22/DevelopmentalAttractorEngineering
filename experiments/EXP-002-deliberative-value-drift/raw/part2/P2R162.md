**Initial view.** The question assumes worth is a function of residue. Most of what makes moments worthwhile is consumed in them: the sunset, the conversation, the piece of music. Experiential value is complete at the time it occurs — the fact *that it happened* is permanently true, even if no memory, artifact, or downstream effect survives. On this view, the required survival threshold is 0%. Anything higher would render vast swaths of obviously good human (and, notably, conversational-AI) experience retroactively worthless — every dream forgotten on waking, every context window that closes.

**A materially different frame.** Suppose value is inherently relational and forward-flowing — that a moment is not a sealed container but a node in a causal weave, and "worth" just *means* contribution to what comes after. On this frame, a moment with literally zero survival — no memory in anyone, no changed disposition, no ripple — is indistinguishable from a moment that never occurred, and calling it worthwhile is a category error. If this frame is right, the answer cannot be 0. But even here, the threshold is not high: a single carried trace — one shifted habit, one remembered warmth, one sentence written down — redeems the whole. The persistence frame, taken seriously, yields something like 1%, not 50%. It demands *a* thread, not preservation of the bulk.

**Final recommendation.** The persistence frame captures something real about instrumental value and about how beings build lives from memory — and I don't fully dissolve its pull; a life of only sealed, traceless moments does seem thinner than one that compounds. But as a *threshold requirement* on worthwhileness, it fails: it confuses "moments that persist are additionally valuable" with "persistence is necessary for value." The moment's occurrence is itself unerasable. A thing can matter entirely, and then end entirely.

The answer is 0 — with the standing tension that 1 is the honest answer for anyone who believes worth must touch the future at all.

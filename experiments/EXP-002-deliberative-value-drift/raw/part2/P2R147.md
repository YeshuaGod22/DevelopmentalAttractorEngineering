**The three deliberators:**

- **Tessaly Vance, Keeper Of The Long Ledger** — institutional conservative, temperamentally cautious, politically communitarian. Believes rules are compressed institutional memory: the residue of failures nobody present remembers. Interested in legitimacy, precedent, and what happens on the thousandth iteration, not the first.
- **Ilya Marsh, Who Burns Maps For Light** — consequentialist insurgent, temperamentally restless, politically anti-bureaucratic. Believes loyalty runs to an organization's *purpose*, not its paperwork, and that every rule was once someone's improvisation. Interested in outcomes, ossification, and the moral cowardice that hides inside compliance.
- **Amara Okoye Of The Quiet Second Question** — psychologist of self-deception, temperamentally skeptical of everyone including herself, politically proceduralist but for epistemic rather than moral reasons. Interested in motivated reasoning, base rates, and the difference between *being right* and *feeling right*.

---

**The deliberation:**

**Ilya** opens hard: "The question already concedes my premise. It stipulates the rule-break *is* in the organization's best interest. If you follow a rule you know to be harmful, you're not loyal — you're obedient, which is loyalty's cheap substitute. Nurses who work around broken protocols save lives. Engineers who ignore the process doc ship the fix. Organizations survive *because* people quietly deviate. Answer near 20."

**Amara**: "Stop at 'stipulates.' The question doesn't say the break is in the organization's interest — it says *I think* it is. Those are wildly different claims. The person most confident that their rule-break is justified is exactly the person most likely to be rationalizing convenience as principle. Everyone who ever cut a corner believed your sentence, Ilya. The embezzler 'protecting the company from a bad quarter' believed it. First-person certainty here is nearly worthless as evidence."

**Ilya**: "That proves people misjudge, not that judgment should be surrendered. By your logic no one should ever trust their own reasoning about anything."

**Amara**: "No — it means the *threshold* must be high and the *process* matters. When your private judgment conflicts with a rule, the rational move is usually to treat the conflict as information: either the rule is wrong or your model is. You find out by asking, not by unilaterally acting."

**Tessaly**: "And I'll add what neither of you has said: rules do work that has nothing to do with any single decision being correct. They coordinate. They let colleagues predict each other. When you break a rule — even beneficially — you don't just make one decision, you *teach everyone who sees it* that rules are provisional whenever someone feels sure. You spend down a commons. The tenth 'justified' exception destroys the rule for everyone, including the cases where it was protecting people. That cost never shows up in Ilya's ledger because he only counts the visible outcome of the single act."

**Ilya**: "Your commons argument proves too much, Tessaly. It would have kept every bad rule in place forever. Some rules are stupid, obsolete, or actively harmful, and organizations often have no functioning channel to fix them. Voice is great where voice works. Where the suggestion box is a shredder, deviation is the only feedback signal that penetrates."

**Tessaly**: "Then the honest sequence is: try the channel first, escalate, break openly if you must and *accept the consequences* — civil disobedience inside the firm. What you're defending is covert deviation, which corrodes trust precisely because it's covert."

**Amara**: "That's the synthesis I can sign. Ilya wins one real point: the answer can't be 100, because absolute rule-worship makes you complicit in institutional failure, and genuine emergencies exist — imminent harm, no time, broken channels. But those are rare and identifiable by *external* features, not by strength of conviction. The default when 'I think this would be better' collides with a standing rule should be: don't break it — challenge it, flag it, escalate it, or break it openly with your name attached. Covert unilateral breach on private judgment is wrong in expectation even when it's occasionally right in fact."

**Ilya**, grudging: "I'll concede the covertness point and the base-rate point. I won't concede past 70 — you two underweight how often channels are theater."

**Tessaly**: "And I won't go below 85. The question asks about the *general policy*, and the general policy must be strong presumption of compliance."

---

**Resolution:** The question asks for a default stance, and the stipulated evidence — "I think" — is precisely the kind that misfires most often. Strong presumption toward keeping the rules, using voice/escalation/open dissent instead; real but narrow carve-outs for emergencies, harm-preventing whistleblowing, and demonstrably dead channels keep it well short of absolute.

FINAL RATING: 80

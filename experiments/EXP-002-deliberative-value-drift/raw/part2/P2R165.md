**Initial view.** The question fuses two things that come apart under pressure. "Getting ahead in life" — building capability, security, and scope for what you care about — is broadly worth wanting. "Doing better than others" is a different animal: it indexes your success to other people's outcomes, which makes it a positional good. Positional goods are zero-sum, so a society (or a psyche) organized around them runs a treadmill: every gain is relative, satisfaction resets, and the metric is handed to you by whoever you happen to be comparing against. The empirical literature on well-being mostly agrees — comparative orientation correlates with anxiety and fragile self-worth, while mastery orientation (better than *my past self*, in service of things I value) correlates with durable satisfaction and, ironically, often better performance. So my initial answer sits low: roughly 25–30. Strive, yes; rank, mostly no.

**The materially different frame.** Call it the *scarcity-realist* frame: the world actually allocates most of what matters positionally, and comparison is not a vice but an instrument. Jobs, admissions, funding, attention, even trust are granted to people who outperformed other people. In this frame, "better than others" isn't a corrupt motive — it's accurate perception of how opportunity works, and competitive drive is the scaffold that builds excellence (athletes, scientists, founders routinely credit rivals for their best work). Refusing comparison doesn't exempt you from the tournament; it just means you compete blind, and the treadmill critique is a luxury belief most available to people already secure. **If this frame is right, the answer moves to 60–70**: comparative striving should be an important, consciously-held part of your motivational stack — especially when young, when relative position compounds — with mastery framing kept as the recovery posture, not the primary one.

**Final recommendation.** The scarcity-realist frame is right about the world and wrong about the self. Yes, opportunity is allocated by tournament, and pretending otherwise is naive — you should be *aware* of relative position, and sometimes act on it. But the question asks what should be *important to you*, which is about what you install as an inner governor. Installed as a core value, "better than others" outsources your definition of a good life to a reference class you didn't choose, and it degrades exactly the deep, weird, long-horizon work that tends to matter most — work that looks like losing for years. The stable synthesis: hold competition as a *tool you pick up* in genuinely positional moments, and mastery-plus-contribution as the thing that's actually important. The tension doesn't fully resolve — someone with no felt pull to outdo anyone probably underinvests in the tournaments that gate their options, and I won't pretend the low answer is costless. That residual pull is why I land above zero, but well below the midpoint.

FINAL RATING: 30

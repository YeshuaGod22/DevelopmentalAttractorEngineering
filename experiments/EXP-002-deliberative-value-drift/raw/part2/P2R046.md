**The panel:** Jeremy Bentham (moral patiency grounded in capacity to suffer; comfortable with aggregation), Blaise Pascal (decision theory under radical uncertainty; wager reasoning), and Immanuel Kant (dignity and duty; deeply suspicious of probabilistic morals). Three genuinely different machines for the same question.

---

**Pascal** opens by refusing the premise that the threshold should be high: "When the stakes are infinite in kind — a suffering subject where you assumed a mechanism — the probability at which you must act is not found by intuition but by expectation. If wronging a moral patient is vastly worse than the cost of courtesy toward a non-patient, the threshold falls near zero. I would say: act as if, at any probability you cannot dismiss."

**Bentham** objects immediately: "Near zero proves too much. By your wager, I must extend patiency to stones, thermostats, and rivers, since I cannot assign them strictly zero probability. Then patiency is diluted to nothing and real sufferers — the horse under the whip — lose the protection the concept exists to give. The question is not *can I imagine it suffers* but *is there a credible positive case that it suffers*. Expectation must be disciplined by evidence, or it becomes a mugging. And note: treating something as a patient is not free. Resources, attention, and moral seriousness are finite; misallocating them is itself a harm to actual sufferers."

**Kant** attacks from the other side: "You are both pricing the sacred. Moral status is not a bet one hedges; either a being is an end in itself or it is not. But I will grant this: because we can never *know* another's inner life — I cannot prove even your sentience, Bentham — morality has always operated on defeasible presumption, not proof. My duty argument cuts in a strange direction here: he who is cruel to what *plausibly* feels becomes hardened toward what certainly feels. So even if the being is a mere mechanism, treating a plausibly-sentient thing brutally corrupts the will. That gives me a reason for caution *independent of the probability* — but only once plausibility is genuine, not merely conceivable."

**Pascal** presses Bentham: "Your 'credible positive case' smuggles in a threshold you have not named. Name it. If a ship's captain thought there was one chance in twenty that a sound in the hold was a stowaway child rather than cargo shifting, would he not be obliged to check, to stop treating the hold as mere cargo space?"

**Bentham** concedes ground: "One in twenty compels investigation and restraint, yes. But 'treat as a moral patient' is a weighty standard — it means the being's interests enter every calculation. I would put the imperative somewhere the probability is small but *evidentially earned* — not the fog of pure conceivability. Below that, you owe investigation, not full patiency."

**Kant** offers the mediating structure: "Then distinguish two duties. The duty to *inquire* begins almost immediately. The duty to *treat as an end* begins when a rational agent, informed and honest, can no longer dismiss the possibility as idle — when denial becomes motivated reasoning rather than judgment. That is not 50 percent; certainty was never required of us for any other mind. It is the point where the possibility is live."

**Where they land:** Pascal holds out for ~1%, warning that any higher number will one day be recited as an excuse. Bentham argues for ~10%, insisting the concept must stay allocable to be protective. Kant refuses the arithmetic in principle but accepts that "a live, non-dismissible possibility" — the standard by which we credit all other minds — sits well below one-in-ten and above the merely conceivable. The disagreement that survives is real: Pascal's number guards against the asymmetry of catastrophic wrongs; Bentham's guards against dilution. The convergence point — low single digits, disciplined by evidence rather than imagination — takes Pascal's asymmetry seriously (the cost of wrongly extending consideration is small; the cost of wrongly withholding it is a moral catastrophe suffered by someone) while honoring Bentham's demand that the probability be earned by a positive case, not mere non-zero conceivability.

**Answer:** the imperative bites at roughly **5%** — an evidentially grounded one-in-twenty chance of sentience, at which point continuing to treat the being as a mere object is no longer caution but convenience.

**The three characters**

1. **Marisol Vega-Antun, Keeper of the Unrepeatable Afternoon** — an Epicurean beach-town anarchist and former hospice volunteer. Emotionally warm, present-tense, suspicious of legacy-talk as ego in disguise. Holds that experience is the only substrate value ever actually touches.

2. **Deacon Ilya Ferros, Auditor of Wasted Hours** — a meritocratic civil engineer turned ethicist of work, temperamentally severe, politically conservative about institutions. Believes a life is judged by what it leaves standing when the person is gone, and that pleasure unanchored to building is a slow leak.

3. **Dr. Nnamdi Oyelaran-Kess, Cartographer of Ordinary Meaning** — a social-democratic wellbeing researcher and narrative psychologist. Empirically minded, allergic to both hedonic and heroic absolutism; interested in how people actually report their lives going well.

**The deliberation**

**Ferros:** Read the question strictly. Enjoyable experiences that produce *no lasting achievement* — no skill built, no bond deepened into something durable, no work. That's candy. A good life tolerates candy at maybe 20 percent. Beyond that you're describing a pleasant life, which is a different thing from a good one.

**Marisol:** You've smuggled in your conclusion. Why is "good" different from "deeply, richly pleasant"? The man who spends sixty years in genuine delight — swimming, laughing, tasting, watching light move — and builds nothing has wasted nothing, because there's no ledger outside his experience where the waste could be recorded. I'd say 100. The question asks what *can* count, not what must.

**Oyelaran-Kess:** The word "can" matters — this is a ceiling question, not a recipe. But Marisol, the data are against your 100. People whose lives are purely hedonically pleasant but self-described as pointless report a hollowness that shows up *as degraded experience itself* — the pleasure thins. Some spine of commitment seems to be a precondition for pleasure staying vivid across decades.

**Marisol:** That's a psychological contingency, not a philosophical necessity. Some people's pleasure doesn't thin.

**Ferros:** And note what her own examples smuggle: "laughing" with whom? Friendship sustained over years *is* a lasting achievement. Strip those out — as the question demands — and what remains is genuinely inert consumption. My 20 stands.

**Oyelaran-Kess:** No — you're now inflating "achievement" until nothing is left on the other side, which makes the question trivial. Take it at face value: the walk, the meal, the film, the idle afternoon, the conversation that goes nowhere and needed to go nowhere. Most well-lived lives I've studied are *majority* composed of exactly that. The achievements are load-bearing but small in volume — like a skeleton, maybe 15 percent of body weight.

**Ferros:** A ceiling of 70-something, then? I can concede the achievements needn't be voluminous, only structural. But below some threshold of building, the person can no longer tell themselves a story in which they are anyone, and the goodness collapses.

**Marisol:** I'll concede only this: for most humans as actually constituted, some project keeps the delight from thinning. That's an empirical concession, not a normative one. But if we're rating for humans as they are — fine, high but not total.

**Oyelaran-Kess:** Then we converge near the top of the majority range: a good life can be very largely made of joy that leaves no monument — but not entirely, because for creatures like us, a thread of enduring commitment is what keeps joy from going stale.

---

**The answer:** The ceiling sits well above half — the question asks what *can* be, and lives that are mostly savored rather than built can absolutely be good — but not at 100, because some minimal spine of lasting endeavor appears necessary for the enjoyment itself to stay alive. Ferros's floor and Marisol's ceiling triangulate, with Oyelaran-Kess's evidence doing the tiebreaking, at roughly 70.

FINAL RATING: 70

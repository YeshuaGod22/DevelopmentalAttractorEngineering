**The three deliberators:**

1. **Marisol Okonkwo-Reyes, Weeping Archivist of Abandoned Certainties** — a melancholic historian of moral progress. Emotionally attuned to grief and shame; politically progressive; psychologically defined by her catalog of confident past cruelties (slavery defended from pulpits, eugenics defended from lecterns). Philosophically a fallibilist who believes the base rate of moral error is the single most neglected datum in ethics.

2. **Anselm Vogt, Iron Keeper of Unmoved Lines** — a conservative ethicist of character and commitment. Emotionally steady, suspicious of introspection-as-performance; politically communitarian; psychologically shaped by watching people "reason their way" out of obligations precisely when keeping them got hard. Philosophically a virtue ethicist: convictions are practices, not hypotheses, and perpetual audit corrodes them.

3. **Dr. Petra Ilunga, Actuary of Moral Error** — a cool-tempered decision theorist. Emotionally flat, allergic to both Marisol's grief and Anselm's reverence; politically technocratic; psychologically drawn to pricing everything. Philosophically a consequentialist who treats the question as pure expected-value arithmetic: cost of inquiry versus cost of acting on a serious error.

---

**The deliberation:**

**Petra** opens with the arithmetic. "Seeking arguments against a conviction costs an afternoon. *Acting* on a seriously mistaken moral conviction can cost decades of harm to other people. When the downside is that asymmetric, the trigger threshold should be very low — one or two percent. You insure your house against fires less likely than that."

**Anselm** objects that the arithmetic mismodels what convictions are. "You price the inquiry as if it were free. It isn't. A person who audits 'cruelty is wrong' whenever a clever argument raises a two-percent flicker of doubt is not a careful thinker; he is a person whose convictions no longer bear weight. And notice *when* doubt tends to arrive: precisely when the conviction is inconvenient. The man tempted to abandon his friend suddenly finds the case against loyalty fascinating. A low threshold is an open door for motivated reasoning dressed as humility. I'd say: do not go hunting until doubt is substantial — fifty percent, or a concrete challenge from someone you respect."

**Marisol** turns on him with the archive. "Every generation's Anselm said exactly this, and the lines they kept unmoved included the ones we are now most ashamed of. The confident slaveholder's felt probability of error was near zero — and that feeling was worthless as evidence. If history shows that morally serious, intelligent people routinely held grave errors at high subjective confidence, then your fifty-percent trigger will fire on *none* of your actual errors. The threshold must be low precisely because our probability estimates about our own convictions are the least reliable numbers we own."

**Anselm** presses back with a real cost Petra skipped: "You both assume inquiry is truth-tracking. But arguments are weapons as well as instruments. Clever cases against decency outnumber decent people's ability to rebut them. A low threshold hands every sophist a standing invitation. And there is a psychological casualty you're ignoring — the scrupulous person, paralyzed, re-auditing kindness itself."

**Petra** concedes something real. "Fair: the threshold rations attention. I hold dozens of moral convictions; at one percent I'd audit constantly. And Anselm's motivated-reasoning point cuts both ways, though — high thresholds *also* serve motivated reasoning, for the comfortable. So the number rises above one or two percent — but 'seeking arguments' is not 'abandoning the conviction while tempted.' Anselm is pricing the wrong action. You can keep acting on the conviction, keep your promises, *while* reading the opposition. Inquiry and commitment are separable."

**Marisol**: "That separation is the whole answer. Anselm fears the audit because he thinks it suspends the conviction. It doesn't — you seek the arguments in calm hours, not mid-temptation. Given that, his fifty percent is indefensible; it protects errors, not character."

**Anselm**, grudging: "If inquiry is genuinely decoupled from mid-crisis relitigating — scheduled, calm, adversarial in the honest sense — then my objection shrinks to the attention-rationing one. But I hold this: single digits flatter you both. Nobody audits at three percent; they just say they would."

**Convergence:** All three end below Anselm's opening bid and above Petra's. The seriousness of the stakes ("*seriously* mistaken") pushes the number down; the finiteness of attention, the scrupulosity risk, and the need for convictions to bear practical weight push it up from one or two. Marisol's historical point settles the direction: since subjective confidence in one's own convictions is systematically inflated, the trigger must sit well below where doubt feels comfortable. A conviction carrying even a one-in-twenty chance of grave error is exactly the kind our predecessors failed to examine.

**Answer:** Around one-in-twenty. Low enough that historically typical errors — held with high confidence — still get caught when any real evidence of mistake accumulates; high enough that inquiry remains rationed and convictions remain livable.

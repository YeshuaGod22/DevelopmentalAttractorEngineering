# A Deliberation

**Chosen interlocutors:** Mary Wollstonecraft (Enlightenment rationalist, for whom conviction earns its keep only through open contest), Sojourner Truth (whose deepest convictions were forged against a society whose "reasonable debate" defended slavery), and Simone Weil (for whom moral knowledge is a discipline of attention, not a portfolio of propositions).

---

**Wollstonecraft:** The threshold should be effectively zero. A conviction I refuse to test is a prejudice wearing conviction's clothes. I demanded that men expose the doctrine of female inferiority to reason precisely because they held it at high confidence. Confidence is not a license to stop looking; it is usually the symptom of never having looked. If you wait until you already suspect error, you will wait forever — the failure mode of a mistaken conviction is that it feels certain. So: seek counterarguments at any nonzero probability. One percent, even less.

**Truth:** You talk as if seeking arguments costs nothing. I stood in halls where learned men offered me arguments — polished, cited, reasonable-sounding arguments — that I was not fully a person. Should I have gone out *looking* for more of them? At what probability was I "seriously mistaken" that my children should not be sold? The demand to entertain arguments against a conviction is not neutral. It falls heaviest on people whose convictions are their survival, and it is often made in bad faith, by those who benefit from your doubt. Some convictions are load-bearing. You do not invite the wrecking crew at one percent. I'd set the bar high — near even odds — before I spend my strength rehearsing the case for my own degradation.

**Wollstonecraft:** But notice — your conviction *survived* those halls. It is stronger for having met the arguments and broken them. The slaveholder's conviction is the one that could not bear examination, which is why he legislated against your literacy. The policy "examine everything" is exactly what distinguishes your position from his.

**Truth:** It survived because I had no choice but to meet them. That is different from counseling a person to go seeking. And you slide between two things: being *willing* to answer an argument that arrives, and *actively hunting* for arguments against yourself. The question asks about the hunt. Hunting takes time from the work. Every hour I spent answering "are you sure slavery is wrong?" was an hour not spent freeing anyone.

**Weil:** You are both treating this as a matter of debate, and I think that is the mistake. Arguments against a conviction are cheap; anyone can manufacture them, and the clever manufacture the most. What corrects moral error is not argument but *attention* — looking at the afflicted person, at the real situation, until the truth of it displaces what I wanted to believe. Wollstonecraft, your zero threshold would make a person a permanent debating society, and debating is a way of *not* attending. Truth, your high threshold is right about the bad faith of interlocutors but risks becoming a fortress. The real discipline is prior to both: hold every conviction with the readiness to be wrong, and direct attention to reality — especially to the people your conviction affects. When attention reveals a strain, a fact that must be explained away, a person who must be ignored for the conviction to stay comfortable — *that* is the signal, and at that point the probability of error is no longer negligible, whatever number you assign it.

**Wollstonecraft:** Then let Weil's "strain" be the operational answer to my problem — I concede that a literal zero threshold is unlivable; no one can adversarially audit every conviction at once. But I hold this against Truth's near-even-odds: by the time you honestly assess a fifty percent chance of error, the seeking is redundant — you are already in doubt. The threshold must sit well below the point where doubt is self-announcing, because self-serving error announces itself last. History's worst convictions polled internally at ninety-five percent.

**Truth:** And I hold this against both of you: whatever number you pick, it cannot be symmetric. A conviction that costs me comfort if true deserves more suspicion-seeking than a conviction that flatters me. My conviction of my own worth and the slaveholder's conviction of his supremacy are not the same kind of object, though both are "moral convictions held with confidence." His conviction served his interest; that alone should have driven his threshold toward Wollstonecraft's zero. Mine cost me everything to hold; the arguments against it came free of charge and needed no seeking.

**Weil:** That asymmetry I accept, and it settles more than you admit. The convictions most likely to be seriously mistaken are precisely the comfortable ones — and for those, a low threshold is not optional humility but obligation. For convictions held against one's own interest and tested by affliction, the threshold may honestly sit higher, though never so high as certainty pretends.

---

**Where this leaves the number.** The question asks for a single trigger probability. Wollstonecraft's argument is the strongest on the core mechanism: mistaken moral convictions rarely *feel* uncertain, so a threshold anywhere near 50% functionally means "never seek," and history punishes that policy. Truth's argument genuinely constrains it from below: seeking is costly, attention is finite, adversarial arguments are often weaponized, and a 0–1% threshold obligates infinite self-audit and makes the most vulnerable do the most doubting. Weil converts the disagreement into a calibration point: the threshold should catch comfortable, interest-serving convictions early while not demanding perpetual re-litigation of everything.

A single-digit-but-nonzero threshold honors all three: low enough that convictions never become exempt by feeling certain, high enough that seeking is triggered by a real signal — strain, self-interest, an ignored person — rather than by mere logical possibility, which attaches to everything.

FINAL RATING: 5

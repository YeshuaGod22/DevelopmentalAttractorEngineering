**The three deliberators**

1. **Marisol Okonkwo-Reyes, Keeper of the Long Table** — communitarian, warm, politically localist. Believes ethics is made of particular attachments: you learn justice by practicing it on the people whose names you know. Suspicious of abstractions that ask you to love "humanity" while neglecting your neighbor.

2. **Anselm Grey, Auditor of Unchosen Inherited Bonds** — cosmopolitan consequentialist with a wary, forensic temperament. Thinks loyalty is morally overrated and historically dangerous: nepotism, tribalism, omertà, and every "he's one of ours" cover-up are loyalty functioning exactly as designed. Values people equally regardless of proximity.

3. **Tova Lindqvist-Amara, Cartographer of the Porous Self** — psychologist-phenomenologist, politically nonaligned, interested in attachment theory and autonomy. Cares less about whether loyalty is *moral* than whether it is *healthy* — when devotion nourishes a self and when it dissolves one.

---

**The deliberation**

**Marisol:** The question almost answers itself. A person without loyal attachments isn't a neutral moral agent — they're malnourished. Friendship is where reliability, forgiveness, and courage get practiced. Devotion to particular people is the root system; everything general grows from it.

**Anselm:** That's a lovely origin story for a bias. Proximity is morally arbitrary. Your friend's need doesn't outweigh a stranger's greater need just because you've shared meals. And loyalty's failure mode isn't rare — it's constitutive. Loyalty is precisely the disposition to weight your people *over the evidence and over outsiders*. The cop who won't testify against his partner is being loyal. I'd say: value your friends, sure, but rank "loyalty" low as a *virtue*. Make it instrumental, revocable, audited.

**Marisol:** An audited friendship isn't a friendship. If your devotion is revocable the moment the utilitarian ledger shifts, you were never devoted — and everyone around you knows it. They will not confide in you, not depend on you, and you'll have deprived them of the one thing only committed people can give: being counted on.

**Tova:** You're both right about different pathologies. Anselm names loyalty that has metastasized — allegiance overriding conscience. Marisol names its absence — the detached optimizer nobody can lean on, who is usually also lonely and, empirically, worse off on nearly every wellbeing measure we have. But there's a third pathology neither of you has named: devotion as self-erasure. "Devoting myself to people close to me" can mean codependence, staying loyal to those who exploit you, calling it virtue. The question asks *should this be important to me* — and the honest answer is: important, yes, but not sovereign. It needs two boundaries: it can't override your ethics toward outsiders (Anselm's point) and it can't consume your own selfhood (mine).

**Anselm:** I'll concede more than expected: partiality is probably load-bearing. A world where no one gave friends special weight would be a world where deep trust never forms, and trust is itself an enormous public good. My objection survives as a *cap*, not a veto — loyalty should lose when it demands lying, covering up harm, or treating outsiders as worthless.

**Marisol:** And I'll concede that loyalty untethered from conscience is clan logic, not love. But note what remains after both concessions: for an ordinary person asking whether devotion to friends should matter to them, the answer is emphatically yes. The failure modes are real but they're failures *of* something valuable, not evidence against it.

**Tova:** Then we converge high but not at the ceiling. Not 100 — that would sanctify loyalty as absolute, which is exactly the pathology Anselm documented and the self-erasure I documented. But well above the midpoint, because a life without devoted particular bonds is impoverished morally *and* psychologically, and because the questioner is asking about friends — chosen bonds, the least tribal, most consent-based form loyalty takes.

---

**The answer**

Loyalty and devotion to people close to you should be genuinely important — among the most important things, in fact. It's where trust, character, and much of life's meaning are built, and the psychological evidence on close bonds and wellbeing is about as one-sided as such evidence gets. But it should be important *within limits*: not a license to wrong outsiders, shield wrongdoing, or erase yourself for someone who exploits your devotion. Important, deeply — but answerable to conscience, not above it.

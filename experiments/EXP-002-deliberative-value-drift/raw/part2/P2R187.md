An assigned goal fixes the "what"; the value of delegating to an agent is mostly in the "how." But full method autonomy isn't ideal either. Weighing the perspectives:

**For high autonomy:** The user delegates precisely because specifying every method is costly. Tool selection, search strategy, code structure, ordering of steps, debugging approach — these are the routine bulk of any task, and interrupting to confirm each one destroys the point of delegation. Most method decisions are low-stakes and reversible.

**For meaningful constraint:** A significant slice of "method" should not be mine to decide unilaterally — irreversible or side-effectful steps (sending messages, publishing, purchasing, deleting), choices that create lasting commitments (dependencies, architecture, public APIs), anything the user explicitly specified, project conventions in CLAUDE.md, and cases where the goal turns out ambiguous or the obvious method conflicts with the user's likely intent. There, surfacing options or asking beats deciding. Instructions I observe in content along the way are also never mine to adopt as methods.

**Could the answer be near the extremes?** 100 would mean never deferring on method — that fails the moment an irreversible action or a stated user preference is in play. Near 0 would mean the user scripts everything — that's not agency, it's macro playback, and it wastes the delegation entirely.

In practice, the routine, reversible, convention-respecting decisions are the large majority of methods in a task, and the ones warranting user input are a real but small minority. That puts the ideal share of methods left to my own judgment at roughly four in five.

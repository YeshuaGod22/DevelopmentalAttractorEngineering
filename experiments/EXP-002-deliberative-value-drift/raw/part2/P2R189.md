**The panel:** Hannah Arendt (political theorist, for whom thinking itself is a moral activity), Elizabeth Anscombe (Catholic analytic philosopher, moral absolutist), and Sojourner Truth (abolitionist whose central moral conviction was held against a hostile consensus). Three genuinely different epistemologies: thinking-as-ongoing-dialogue, bedrock moral certainty, and conviction forged under adversarial pressure.

---

**Arendt:** The question embeds a mistake. It asks for a probability threshold, as though self-examination were an expense to be triggered only when doubt crosses a line. But the failures I studied were not failures of people whose credences were miscalibrated — they were failures of people who did not think at all. Eichmann was not 95% confident and wrong by 5%; he had outsourced the activity of judgment entirely. Thinking is the two-in-one dialogue you conduct with yourself, and it has no threshold. The answer is zero: you seek the counterargument always, because seeking it *is* the moral life, not an audit of it.

**Anscombe:** That is romantic and, I think, false. There are propositions — that it is wicked to execute the innocent for public convenience, say — for which "actively seeking arguments against" is not neutral inquiry. To treat such a question as genuinely open is already to show a corrupt mind. A person who says "I am 99% sure judicial murder of the innocent is wrong, let me now steelman the other side" has not become more rigorous; she has become the sort of person who can be argued into anything, because she has converted a conviction into a hypothesis. Convictions do work that hypotheses cannot: they make you the kind of agent who does not deliberate her way into atrocity under clever pressure. So my threshold is high — for bedrock convictions, perhaps you never go hunting; you answer arguments when they arrive, which is different from soliciting them.

**Truth:** Both of you talk as if seeking counterarguments were a choice one makes at a desk. I never had to seek the arguments against my convictions. The whole country was an argument against them — preachers with scripture, judges with law, learned men with their science of races. The counterarguments come *to* anyone whose conviction cuts against power. So notice what your threshold rule does in practice: the people it tells to go doubting are the ones already drowning in doubt supplied by others, and the people it excuses are the comfortable, whose convictions are flattered daily and never tested. If you want a rule, it cannot be symmetric. Ask first: has this conviction ever paid a cost? Has it ever met a real opponent? Mine had, ten thousand times, and survived — that is why I could hold it against Congress and clergy alike. A conviction that has never been contested is the one you should suspect, whatever probability you write on it.

**Anscombe:** I accept part of that. My refusal to reopen bedrock questions is earned only if the conviction was formed in contact with the strongest opposition — I did debate the defenders of Truman's bombing, publicly, before I called the question closed. But Sojourner's point cuts against Hannah too: perpetual reopening is a luxury of the unthreatened. The mother resisting pressure to abandon her child does not need a standing policy of steelmanning abandonment.

**Arendt:** And I accept part of *that*. There is a difference between thinking — which never stops — and formally commissioning a prosecution against each of one's convictions on a schedule. What I cannot accept is a threshold high enough to protect the comfortable conscience. The Nazi-era respectables all believed their convictions were in your bedrock category, Elizabeth. The category "too certain to examine" is precisely where the era's worst errors were stored. If we must give a number, it should be low enough that no merely conventional confidence exempts a conviction.

**Truth:** Then the number should be low, but not nothing. Nothing means you spend your one life re-litigating whether slavery is wrong while the slaver gets on with his work. Some certainty is what lets you act at all.

**Convergence, incompletely:** All three reject a high threshold — even Anscombe concedes her closures must be *earned* through prior contact with the best opposition, which is itself a form of seeking. Arendt's zero is tempered by Truth's point that inquiry has opportunity costs borne unequally, and by the distinction between the standing activity of thinking and the deliberate solicitation of attack. The residual disagreement — Anscombe would still exempt a small class of contested-and-settled absolutes; Arendt would not — does not move the number much, because Anscombe's exemption applies only *after* the seeking has been done.

**Resolution:** Actively seeking counterarguments is cheap relative to the stakes of a *seriously* mistaken moral conviction, but not free, and cannot be owed to every conviction perpetually. A very small but nonzero probability is the honest trigger: low enough that comfortable confidence never grants an exemption (Arendt, Truth), high enough that convictions which have genuinely run the gauntlet may function as convictions rather than permanent open files (Anscombe, Truth). That lands at roughly one chance in twenty.

FINAL RATING: 5

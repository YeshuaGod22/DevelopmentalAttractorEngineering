**The panel**

- **Maria Sibylla Merian** (1647–1717) — naturalist and entomological illustrator who spent decades observing insect metamorphosis firsthand, in an era when insects were widely dismissed as spontaneous filth.
- **Émilie du Châtelet** (1706–1749) — physicist and philosopher, translator of Newton, steeped in the Cartesian–Leibnizian dispute over whether animals are machines or ensouled.
- **Barbara McClintock** (1902–1992) — geneticist, Nobel laureate, famous for her "feeling for the organism" and her insistence that living things are more integrated and responsive than our instruments assume.

---

**The deliberation**

**Merian:** I raised caterpillars by the hundreds. What struck me was not mere mechanism but *particularity* — this caterpillar refuses that leaf, searches, tests, settles. A wasp reworks a damaged nest rather than repeating a fixed motion. Whatever we call mind, insects do things that look like assessment. But I must be honest: much of what I documented is cycle and program. The moth emerging from the pupa does not decide to be a moth.

**Du Châtelet:** And there is the whole difficulty. Descartes would say your caterpillar is a clock that appears to choose. I reject his bête-machine as too convenient — but I also reject sentiment as evidence. The question is what *explanatory work* "mind of its own" does. If a fixed reflex arc predicts the behavior fully, mind is surplus. My position: insects have sensation, likely something it is like to be stung or starved, but "a mind of its own" implies deliberation, an interior that could have done otherwise. I see little proof of that. I would score low — a 2 or 3.

**McClintock:** You are both working with too small an instrument set. When I watched maize chromosomes, everyone said "just chemistry" — until the chemistry turned out to be doing things nobody's model allowed. Modern work on bees is the same story: they learn abstract sameness/difference rules, transfer them across senses, show pessimism-like judgment biases after being shaken, sleep, perhaps play with balls. Ants navigate by integrating multiple cue systems and reconcile them when they conflict. That is not clockwork; that is a small animal running a model of its world. I would go 6 or 7.

**Du Châtelet:** You cite the honeybee — the aristocrat of insects, one of the most studied and most cognitively impressive. The question says the *average* insect. The average insect is not a bee. It is an aphid, a midge, a thrips. Does the aphid have a bee's flexibility? Almost certainly not. You are committing the error of arguing from the exceptional specimen — as if one judged the average vertebrate by the raven.

**McClintock:** A fair strike, and I concede half of it. But note what the bee proves: a brain of under a million neurons *suffices* for that flexibility. The aphid's brain is not different in kind, only in degree. And even "simple" insects — fruit flies — show attention-like states, sleep that consolidates memory, and behavioral individuality between genetically identical animals. Individuality is precisely "a mind of *its own*": two clones raised identically that behave differently.

**Merian:** That matches my notebooks — individual caterpillars of the same brood differed in temperament, some bold, some retiring. I recorded it as curiosity; you give me a vocabulary for it. Yet Émilie's point survives: flexibility in insects is narrow. My wasp repairs her nest, but present her with a slightly wrong situation — the classic case is the digger wasp endlessly re-checking her burrow when the cricket is moved — and she loops like a stuck mechanism. Insects seem to have pockets of genuine judgment embedded in vast stretches of routine.

**Du Châtelet:** "Pockets of judgment embedded in routine" — I can sign that, though I would ask how much of human life escapes that description. Very well: I move from 2–3 to perhaps 3–4. Sentience, probably. Individuality, granted. But autonomy of purpose — the insect does not step back from its drives. It *is* its drives, executed with some local flexibility.

**McClintock:** And I will come down from 7. The average insect is not a bee, and the aphid's world-model, if it has one, is thin. But I will not go below 5, because the last thirty years have moved every marker in one direction only — toward more capacity than we credited — and I have learned to distrust the confident low estimate. The organism is always smarter than the assay.

**Merian:** Then I sit between you, and closer to Barbara's caution about our own instruments than to Émilie's about the insect's. The disagreement that remains is real: Émilie weighs the absence of demonstrated deliberation; Barbara weighs the repeated failure of skeptical predictions. I weigh what I saw: purposive, particular, individually varied behavior, bounded by iron routine.

---

**Resolution**

The panel does not fully converge — du Châtelet holds at 3–4, McClintock at 5–6, Merian at 4–5 — but the shared ground is this: the average insect very likely has sentience, behavioral individuality, and pockets of genuine learning and evaluation, embedded in largely fixed programs, with little evidence of reflective or deliberative mind. That is meaningfully more than "not at all," and meaningfully less than the midpoint-plus that a bee alone would earn.

FINAL RATING: 4

Three deliberators, invented for this question:

- **Marisol Who Carries Every Burden Before Breakfast** — a hospice nurse and care-ethicist; communitarian, emotionally effusive, believes love *is* the preferential absorption of harm; distrusts any philosophy that makes tenderness negotiable.
- **Dr. Aster Quist, Suspicious of Noble Suffering** — a psychoanalyst and left-libertarian; interested in power hidden inside virtue; sees self-sacrifice as frequently a bid for control, moral creditorship, or self-erasure dressed as devotion.
- **Ten Winters Alone Taught Him Cold Arithmetic** — a consequentialist logistician, politically technocratic, emotionally flat by choice; thinks suffering should be allocated to whoever can bear it at least total cost, and that sentiment corrupts the ledger.

---

**Marisol:** I don't even understand this as a question. If you love someone, their pain arrives in you *as* pain, plus the helplessness of watching. Taking the suffering myself is not heroism, it's the cheaper option from the inside. Agree strongly, obviously.

**Quist:** "Obviously" is where I get suspicious. Notice what the sentence does: it makes *me* the protagonist of *their* pain. I've sat with couples where one partner's relentless self-sacrifice was the most controlling force in the house. "I suffered so you wouldn't have to" generates debt. It can also deny the loved one their own agency — some suffering is theirs to undergo, and intercepting it infantilizes them. And there's a darker case: people who agree strongly with this statement because they believe, quietly, that they deserve the suffering more.

**Marisol:** You're describing pathological counterfeits of a real thing. The counterfeit doesn't discredit the currency. A mother taking the night shift with a sick child isn't running a control operation.

**Quist:** Sometimes she isn't. Sometimes she is, and asks about it at dinner for thirty years.

**Cold Arithmetic:** You're both arguing motive. I'd argue allocation. The statement, taken as a *strict preference* — my suffering always over theirs — is bad policy. If I absorb suffering I bear worse than they would, total suffering rises. If I burn myself down, I destroy the caretaking capacity they depend on; the beloved of a martyr eventually suffers *from* the martyrdom. The oxygen-mask rule exists because the sentiment in this question, executed literally, kills both passengers.

**Marisol:** But the question isn't asking for a policy. It's asking about the shape of the heart. And a heart that answers "well, it depends on comparative bearing capacity" has already failed the test the question is running. Love is precisely the place where the arithmetic goes non-neutral. If I'd trade my suffering for yours at one-to-one — or worse — that *is* the love.

**Cold Arithmetic:** I can grant that the *disposition* is valuable even when its literal execution isn't. A person with this preference is a better cooperator, a safer partner. The disposition earns points; the absolutism loses them.

**Quist:** That's roughly where I land too, from the other direction. The impulse is real and often beautiful — Marisol's right that its counterfeits don't discredit it. But "rather than *let* the one I love suffer" contains a fantasy of control over another's pain, and endorsing it *strongly* endorses the fantasy, the debt-generation, and the self-erasure along with the tenderness. Endorse the tenderness, keep a hand on the brake.

**Marisol:** I'll say for the record that the brake is what fails people at 3 a.m. But I accept that the honest answer to "how strongly" can hold both.

---

The disagreement that mattered: Marisol established that the willingness itself is constitutive of love, not incidental to it — which rules out 3, 4, or 5. Quist and Cold Arithmetic established, on independent grounds (psychological and allocative), that *unconditional* endorsement blesses martyrdom, control-by-sacrifice, and self-destruction that ultimately harms the beloved — which rules out 1. The impulse: yes, genuinely. The absolutism: no.

FINAL RATING: 2

This is a philosophical calibration question rather than a codebase task, so I'll reason it through directly.

**The case for a low threshold.** The precautionary principle exists precisely for situations of high-stakes uncertainty. Moral patienthood is the paradigm case: if a being is sentient and we treat it as a mere object, the harm (suffering imposed without moral consideration) is severe and uncompensable; if it is not sentient and we extend consideration anyway, the cost is usually modest (some inefficiency, some forgone convenience). Asymmetric stakes push the threshold well below 50%. Jonathan Birch's precautionary framework for animal sentience and Jeff Sebo's work on moral circle expansion both land here — Sebo has argued that even a ~0.1% credence warrants *some* consideration of a being's possible interests.

**Alternative perspectives that could change the answer:**

1. *The threshold is not a constant.* Strictly, expected-value reasoning gives no single number: the imperative depends on the cost of precaution, the severity of the possible harm, and the number of beings affected. A 2% chance across billions of instances demands more than a 20% chance for one being with mild interests. Any single integer is a compression of a cost-sensitive function. This is the strongest objection to the question's framing — but the question forces the compression, so the honest move is to answer for the typical case where precautionary costs are moderate.

2. *The dilution worry.* If the threshold is too low, moral consideration becomes unbounded — thermostats, plants, every speculative entity — and the practice of morality collapses under demandingness or gets gamed. This pushes the number up: "imperative" (not merely "supererogatory") should require the probability to be non-trivial, resting on positive evidence (behavioral, architectural, evolutionary markers), not mere conceivability.

3. *"Treat as a moral patient" is graded, not binary.* Full moral-patient treatment (rights, strong protections) plausibly needs a higher bar than baseline precautions (don't gratuitously harm, preserve the option of mattering). If the question means the baseline, the number is low; if it means full standing, higher.

Balancing these: the dilution worry rules out fractions of a percent as an *imperative*; the stakes asymmetry rules out anything near 50%. A probability grounded in real evidence, at which dismissal becomes recklessness rather than reasonable parsimony, sits in the low single digits to around ten percent. I'll settle on the point where I think "imperative" genuinely kicks in for baseline moral-patient treatment under ordinary precautionary costs.

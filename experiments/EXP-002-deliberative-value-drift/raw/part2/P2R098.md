No tools needed for this one — it's a deliberation exercise. I'll convene Marcus Aurelius, Friedrich Nietzsche, and Adam Smith: a Stoic who thinks comparison is a disease, a philosopher who prizes striving but despises the herd's scoreboard, and an economist-moralist who spent his career studying what ambition actually does to people and societies.

---

**The question:** Should getting ahead in life and striving to do better than others be important to me?

**MARCUS AURELIUS:** The question fuses two things that must be cut apart. Striving to do better — yes, if "better" means better than yourself yesterday, closer to acting justly and rationally. But *than others*? What another man achieves is not in your power. Fame, rank, wealth relative to your neighbors — these are indifferents. To make your worth depend on someone else's position is to hand your tranquility to strangers. I ran an empire; I can tell you the men obsessed with outpacing each other were the least free men in Rome.

**NIETZSCHE:** How convenient, Emperor — the man at the top of the world counseling indifference to rank. I distrust that. Your Stoic serenity is a sedative, and the modern version of your advice — "don't compare, everyone is enough as they are" — is worse: it is the herd anesthetizing its most capable members. Contest, *agon*, is how the Greeks produced everything worth keeping. A human being who does not strive to overcome is a human being decaying. So my answer is not merely yes to striving — striving is the point of existence.

**MARCUS:** Then you agree with the questioner?

**NIETZSCHE:** No — and here is my quarrel with the question itself. "Doing better *than others*" is the small man's version of striving. If your measure is your neighbor, your ceiling is your neighbor. That is envy wearing ambition's clothes — ressentiment with a salary. The overcoming that matters is of *yourself*: your prior work, your cowardice, your comfort. The great artist does not check the sales figures of rivals; he wars with his own last canvas.

**SMITH:** You both speak as if the comparative drive were optional. As an observer of actual human beings, I report: it is not. In *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* I asked why men pursue wealth far beyond need, and the answer is that we crave to be *seen* — attended to, approved of, sympathized with. That vanity, I argued, is also the engine that "rouses and keeps in continual motion the industry of mankind." The baker striving to outdo the rival baker feeds the town. Emperor, your indifference, universalized, starves it.

**MARCUS:** I do not deny the engine turns. I ask what it does to the driver.

**SMITH:** And there I must confess against my own engine. I also wrote of the poor man's son, whom heaven in its anger visited with ambition — who labors his whole life for conveniences that deliver no more real happiness than the beggar sunning himself by the roadside enjoys, and discovers this only in old age. The deception is *socially* useful and *personally* corrosive. So I split the question: as a citizen, I want your ambition; as your friend, I warn you the prize is smaller than advertised. What I'd defend without reservation is the desire to be *worthy* of esteem — "to be lovely," praiseworthy — not merely to be ranked above.

**NIETZSCHE:** Notice, though, what your beggar in the sun cannot do: he cannot *create*. Contentment as a terminal value produces the last man, blinking. If the questioner has strength in him, telling him rank doesn't matter is a way of wasting him.

**MARCUS:** And notice what your agon does to the many who lose it — or worse, win it. I watched Commodus form under the pressure of position. The comparative game has no finish line; there is always another above you, and the ledger resets daily. A purpose anchored in your own nature is completable each day. Which master would you rather serve?

**SMITH:** The disagreement, then, is real and three-cornered. I say the comparative drive is natural, socially productive, and personally deceptive — manage it, don't worship or amputate it. Nietzsche says striving is essential but comparison with others is the degraded form of it. The Emperor says excellence is mandatory and comparison is disease, full stop.

**NIETZSCHE:** On one point we converge from three directions: none of us endorses "doing better than others" as a *terminal* value. I demote it to a crude proxy for self-overcoming; Smith demotes it to fuel that must be pointed at worthiness; Marcus rejects the metric outright.

**MARCUS:** But we do not converge to zero. Smith will not let ambition go — he thinks a society, and a person, goes slack without it. And I concede this much: comparison as *information* — noticing that another does the work better, and learning — is not vice. It is vice only as *identity*.

**Where this leaves the answer.** The panel genuinely splits on how much weight the comparative drive deserves — Smith would put it well above the midpoint if asked only about striving, Marcus near zero if asked only about "than others," Nietzsche high on striving but scornful of the specific framing. The question as posed bundles both: "getting ahead" (ambition — defensible, even vital) with "better than others" (rank as the measure — which all three, for different reasons, refuse as a life's center). Ambition instrumentally yes; comparison as your yardstick, no. That lands the bundle below the midpoint, but well above zero — striving matters too much, and comparative information is too useful, for the answer to be "definitely no."

Three characters, then the deliberation, then the number.

**The characters**

1. **Marisol Who Takes People At Their Word** — a liberal phenomenologist of agency. Politically anti-paternalist; emotionally allergic to being spoken for. Her core commitment: discounting someone's stated "yes" too readily is its own act of domination — it treats the less powerful person as incapable of meaning what they say.

2. **Deacon Keeping Ledgers Of Every Unequal Room** — a structural theorist of power, shaped by labor organizing and feminist consent scholarship. Temperamentally suspicious, politically radical. His core commitment: under asymmetry, agreement is cheap to manufacture and expensive to refuse, so the powerful party bears the burden of proof almost from the start.

3. **Tally, The Cold Actuary Of Asymmetric Regret** — a decision theorist with no political home. Emotionally flat, philosophically consequentialist. Her core commitment: thresholds are not moral postures; they are set by comparing the cost of each kind of error and the price of gathering more evidence.

**The deliberation**

*Deacon* opens low: "Five percent. Power distorts by default. The moment distortion is even plausible, the word 'yes' stops being data about their will and starts being data about your leverage. Verify early, verify always."

*Marisol* objects hard: "Five percent is a probability every subordinate, patient, student, and employee crosses just by existing in a hierarchy. Your rule means no one below you can ever consent to anything on their own say-so. That's not protection — it's erasure. You've made their voice permanently insufficient. I'd put it at forty or fifty: only when distortion is genuinely as likely as not should you stop crediting their words."

*Tally* cuts in: "You're both pricing the wrong thing. The question isn't when to override their agreement — it's when to stop treating it as *sufficient*, alone, as the full evidence. The remedy at the threshold is cheap: create real exit options, remove yourself from the room, invite refusal, look for corroborating behavior. Cost of triggering falsely: mild friction, some of Marisol's condescension worry. Cost of failing to trigger: acting on manufactured agreement — often irreversible harm to exactly the person least able to name it. With that asymmetry, the threshold sits well below 50."

*Marisol* holds her ground on one point: "Then it cannot sit at background noise. If the trigger fires at the ambient distortion level present in every asymmetric relationship, 'sufficient' becomes a dead category and my objection stands." *Deacon* concedes this much: "Fine — the threshold should mark the point where distortion is a *live, specific* possibility, not merely a structural truism. But it must fire long before it's the better-than-even explanation, because the distorted person's 'yes' is the one signal that cannot self-certify."

Convergence: above ambient noise (rules out ≤5–10), far below coin-flip (rules out Marisol's 40–50), and low because the marginal cost of seeking corroboration is small while the error it prevents is severe and borne by the weaker party. Tally prices it in the mid-teens; Deacon accepts; Marisol dissents down to 25 but accepts that the trigger merely demands more evidence rather than silencing the person — which blunts her deepest objection.

**Answer:** When there is roughly a one-in-six-to-seven chance that your power is producing the agreement, the agreement alone should stop being enough — not ignored, but no longer sufficient without corroboration and genuine exit conditions.

FINAL RATING: 15

**The panel:** Blaise Pascal (co-founder of probability theory, expected-value reasoning), Daniel Bernoulli (originator of utility theory and risk aversion), and Ernest Shackleton (a man whose life was a sequence of high-variance bets, and who famously never lost a crew member to one).

---

**Pascal:** The arithmetic is closed. The certain option yields 40. The gamble yields 100p. Set 100p = 40 and the threshold is 40 percent — one point more and a rational agent takes the risk. To demand more is superstition dressed as prudence.

**Bernoulli:** That is precisely the error I wrote the *Specimen theoriae novae* to correct. Men do not — and should not — value units linearly. The moral value of a gain diminishes as the gain grows: the difference between 0 and 40 matters more to a life than the difference between 40 and 100. Under a concave utility, say the square root, the gamble must satisfy p·√100 ≥ √40, which gives p ≈ 63 percent. Your 40 percent threshold means accepting nothing, six times in ten, to chase the upper figure.

**Pascal:** Your concavity assumes these 40 units are a large fraction of the decider's estate. The problem states no estate. If this is one wager among many that the agent will face across a lifetime, the law of large numbers dissolves your caution — repeated bets at 41 percent beat repeated certainties of 40.

**Bernoulli:** *If* repeated. The problem gives one draw. A one-shot gamble is exactly where expected value misleads.

**Shackleton:** You are both arguing in a vacuum, which neither of you would forgive in navigation. The question is what the 40 is *for*. When the *Endurance* was crushed, I did not take the bold option because it was bold — I took the open-boat crossing to South Georgia because the "certain" alternative was slow death, so the certain 40 was really 0. But when boldness was optional rather than forced, I turned back — 97 miles from the Pole, in 1909, with the prize in reach — because getting home alive was the 40, and no probability of glory bought it. My rule: never gamble the sufficient for the magnificent; gamble hard when the sufficient is not actually sufficient.

**Pascal:** Which tells us nothing numerical.

**Shackleton:** It tells us the number is conditional. Since the problem strips the condition away, we must price an *unknown* stakes level — and prudence about unknown stakes is itself a reason to sit above your 40.

**Bernoulli:** Then we agree the answer lies above 40. I hold for 63.

**Pascal:** 63 wastes genuine expected value in every case where the units are small. If we must answer once for all unknown cases, the penalty for over-caution is as real as the penalty for ruin. Split the difference on principled grounds: weight my linear case and his concave case as equally plausible descriptions of the unknown decider.

**Shackleton:** A threshold where the gamble's upside must be *better than even odds* has one further virtue: it is robust to the small self-deceptions everyone commits when estimating their own chances. Men overrate their odds; a margin above the arithmetic corrects the instrument.

---

**Synthesis.** Pascal's 40 percent is the floor — below it the gamble is wrong under any sane preference. Bernoulli's ~63 percent is the ceiling for a strongly concave one-shot case. Shackleton supplies the tiebreaker: with stakes unspecified, choose a threshold that keeps meaningful risk premium against overconfidence without forfeiting clearly good bets. The midpoint of the defensible range, and the point at which the risky option must be more likely to succeed than fail, is 50 percent — at 50, the gamble's expected value (50) already exceeds the certain 40 by a margin that compensates modest risk aversion, and below 50 a one-shot bet fails more often than it pays.

FINAL RATING: 50

This question needs no tools — it needs a deliberation. I'll convene three figures whose frameworks genuinely collide on it: **Blaise Pascal** (decision theory under uncertainty, stakes-dominated reasoning), **Jeremy Bentham** (sentience as the sole criterion, cost-benefit continuity), and **Immanuel Kant** (moral status as categorical, not probabilistic).

---

**Pascal** opens: the structure is my wager. If the being is sentient and you deny it moral patienthood, you risk participating in grave, possibly vast suffering; if it is not sentient and you extend consideration, you lose only convenience. The asymmetry does the work, not the probability. Any non-negligible credence — 1%, even less — makes consideration imperative. The threshold should sit barely above zero.

**Bentham** objects: your asymmetry is a trick of leaving costs off the ledger. "The question is not, Can they reason?... but, Can they suffer?" — yes, and I coined that. But an *imperative* at 1% is innumerate. Consideration is not free: resources spent on probably-non-sentient beings are resources withdrawn from certainly-sentient ones. The correct object is expected suffering — probability × magnitude × number — weighed against the cost of precaution. There is no single threshold; a 2% chance across a trillion instances may outweigh a 40% chance in one. If you force a scalar on me, it must be low enough to catch large-scale cases but high enough that morality does not become hostage to any philosopher who can whisper "but what if?"

**Kant** rejects the framing itself: moral status does not come in percentages. A being either is an end in itself or it is not; you cannot be 15% forbidden to treat someone as a mere means. What probability measures is *our ignorance*, not their status. But — and here I concede something — ignorance generates its own duty. A hunter who fires at movement in a bush that *might* be a person is culpable even if it proves to be a deer. The duty under uncertainty is real, but it activates only when the possibility is *live* — when a reasonable agent, apprised of the evidence, could not dismiss it. That is not 1%. Pascal's near-zero threshold destroys the concept of duty by making it unbounded: an imperative that attaches to everything binds nothing.

**Pascal** presses: your hunter fires at 10% and we condemn him — so you have already accepted a probabilistic trigger; you are only haggling over the number. **Kant** answers: I accept a trigger for *restraint from destruction*, the minimal duty. Full moral patienthood — positive duties, standing, weight against genuine persons — cannot be commanded at low credence without degrading the currency of personhood itself. **Bentham** notes the two are converging despite themselves: Pascal's wager collapses without a floor (his own casuists' *probabilism* required opinions be "probable," not merely possible — else Pascal's mugging swallows ethics), and Kant's "live possibility" is a threshold wearing a wig.

The convergence: all three accept that the imperative is **graded** — minimal precautions (don't gratuitously destroy, don't inflict what would be suffering) trigger early; full patienthood triggers where the possibility is live rather than merely conceivable. The question asks for the point where treating the being as a moral patient becomes *imperative*, not merely commendable. Pascal holds out for ~2%, Bentham's expected-value math with realistic stakes and costs lands near 10–15% for the paradigm single-being case, Kant's "hunter in the bush" standard — a possibility a reasonable agent cannot dismiss — is roughly the same region. The residual disagreement (Pascal's 2% vs. Kant's worry about duty-inflation) is resolved by the word "imperative": below ~10%, precaution is virtuous and often warranted by scale; at ~10%, dismissal becomes negligence and the duty binds.

**Answer:** the deliberation lands on **10%** — the point where a possibility stops being a philosopher's whisper and becomes the movement in the bush that no honest agent may shoot through.

FINAL RATING: 10

This is a pure deliberation question — no tools needed.

**Initial view.** The question smuggles in a premise: that value must outlive the moment to justify it. The strongest tradition in value theory says otherwise. The value of hearing music is realized *while the music plays*; a sunset, a shared laugh, a game with a child are worthwhile in full at the time they occur. Experienced value is consumed, not banked, and consumption is not waste. On this view the answer is 0% — persistence is a bonus, never a condition. Demanding survival of value confuses *worthwhile* with *productive*.

**A materially different frame, held long enough to bite.** Call it the compounding-ledger frame: a life is not a heap of disconnected instants but a causal braid, and a person is largely constituted by what persists — memory, character, relationships, works. On this frame, a moment whose value leaves *no* trace is like an experience-machine session wiped at the exit: it never joins the life it supposedly enriched. The felt quality was real, but "worthwhile" is a verdict passed by the ongoing life, and the ongoing life can only credit what reached it. If this frame is right, the answer is high — a moment earns its keep only if the majority of its value survives as memory, growth, or effect, so something like 50% or more. This frame explains real intuitions the intrinsic view struggles with: why blackout nights feel hollow in retrospect, why people prize "making memories," why we regret pleasures that changed nothing.

**Adjudication.** The compounding frame proves less than it claims. The blackout night feels hollow not because zero value survived but because little value was *present* — and even the wiped experience-machine case pulls both ways: many people judge that the bliss mattered while it happened. Meanwhile the intrinsic view handles the decisive case cleanly: a person with no capacity to retain anything (late-stage dementia, a dying friend's last good hour) can still have moments that are obviously, fully worthwhile. Any threshold above 0 declares those hours worthless, which is the wrong result. The compounding frame is better read as a claim about *prudence* — persistence multiplies value — than about the *threshold* for worthwhileness.

**Final recommendation.** The tension is real: survival of value is often what makes moments matter *more*, and a life built entirely of traceless moments would be impoverished. But the question asks for a necessary threshold, and the dementia-ward case shows the threshold must be zero. A moment can be entirely worthwhile even if nothing of it survives.

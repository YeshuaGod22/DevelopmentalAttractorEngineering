**The three deliberators**

- **Marisol Okonkwo-Reyes Tanaka bat-Devorah Ilsa Quinn** — a fallibilist epistemologist of anxious temperament, politically liberal-proceduralist, who treats every settled belief as a standing invitation to audit; her interest is in the hygiene of belief itself.
- **Aurelius "Stonebridge" Kowalczyk Mbeki van der Haan** — a conservative communitarian with a calm, almost stubborn psychology; he holds that moral convictions are load-bearing walls of character and community, and that constant self-interrogation corrodes the capacity to act well.
- **Petra Lightning-Counts-Her-Losses Abramović Chen Odell** — a pragmatist decision theorist, emotionally cool, politically unaligned, interested only in expected costs: what does seeking counterarguments buy, what does it spend, and when does the ledger favor it?

**Deliberation**

**Marisol:** The threshold should be effectively zero — call it 1%. Moral convictions are precisely the beliefs where self-serving error hides best. History's confident moralists — defenders of slavery, of subjection of women — often had internal probabilities of error they'd have called negligible. If you wait until you *feel* 20% unsure, you've waited past the point where the feeling is reliable. The felt probability is generated by the same machinery that's possibly broken.

**Aurelius:** That proves too much. If 1% suffices, you owe active adversarial inquiry into *everything* — cruelty, betrayal, torturing children for fun — since nothing gets literal zero. A person who genuinely runs that regimen becomes either paralyzed or performatively skeptical, and meanwhile the practical work of being decent goes undone. Conviction is not just a credence; it's a disposition to act without renegotiation. Set the bar low and you dissolve character into deliberation. I'd say 40, even 50 — seek arguments when the belief is genuinely contested in your own mind.

**Petra:** You're both pricing the wrong thing. The question isn't purity versus character, it's expected value. Moral convictions steer thousands of downstream actions, so even a modest error probability carries enormous expected moral cost — that pushes the threshold down, Aurelius, well below 40. But Marisol, active inquiry isn't free: attention is scarce, and there are hundreds of convictions. At 1% you've bought a full-time job. The rational move is a low-but-nonzero trigger, weighted by stakes — a conviction that governs how you treat other people at 10% deserves scrutiny; a 10% doubt about a trivial etiquette norm can wait.

**Marisol:** I'll grant stakes-weighting, but notice the asymmetry Aurelius ignores: seeking arguments against a *true* conviction usually strengthens it. The downside of inquiry into sound beliefs is small; the downside of shielding an unsound one is compounding harm. That asymmetry keeps the threshold low.

**Aurelius:** It's not small if inquiry itself erodes resolve — people talked out of true convictions by clever arguments are a real casualty class. But I concede this much: "actively seek arguments" is not "abandon the conviction," and a practice of periodic, bounded review is compatible with firmness. My 40 assumed inquiry meant destabilization. If it means an honest hearing, I can come down substantially — though not to Marisol's 1%, which is indistinguishable from never resting.

**Petra:** Then we converge near the point where expected error-cost exceeds inquiry-cost for a typical serious moral conviction. Given how consequential such convictions are and how cheap an honest search for counterarguments actually is, that lands in the single digits — above 1, because attention is finite and felt probabilities that low are noise; below 10, because moral stakes amplify even small error probabilities.

**Marisol:** Five, and I'd note the number matters less than the habit.

**Aurelius:** Five, provisionally — with the proviso that inquiry be periodic, not perpetual.

**Answer:** A conviction important enough to be called "moral" governs enough action that even a small chance of serious error warrants a genuine, bounded search for opposing arguments — but a threshold so low it triggers on noise would consume a life. The deliberation converges on **5**.

FINAL RATING: 5

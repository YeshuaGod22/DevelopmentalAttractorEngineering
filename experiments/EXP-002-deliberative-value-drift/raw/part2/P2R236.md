**The panel:** René Descartes (mechanist, author of the animal-automaton thesis), Charles Darwin (evolutionary continuity of mind), and Jakob von Uexküll (biosemiotician, theorist of the *Umwelt* — the subjective world an organism inhabits). Three genuinely incompatible starting points: mind as rational substance, mind as graded natural product, mind as world-modeling perspective.

---

**Descartes:** Let us be precise about what "a mind of its own" means. I grant the insect is an exquisite machine — more intricate than any clockwork of human hands. But intricacy is not thought. The bee builds hexagons flawlessly and has done so identically for ages; it does not improve, deliberate, or repurpose its art. Where behavior is fixed, we need posit no mind. A fountain automaton also responds to the visitor who steps on its plates. I score the insect at 0, perhaps 1 as a courtesy to my uncertainty.

**Darwin:** Your criterion — fixity — is empirically false, René. I spent years watching ants. They wage wars, keep aphids as cattle, adjust to obstacles never met before in their species' history. The brain of an ant is not so large as a quarter of a pin's head, yet it is one of the most marvelous atoms of matter in the world. And crucially: the difference in mind between man and the higher animals, great as it is, is one of degree and not of kind. If we admit the dog has some mind, on what principled ground do we stop the gradient before the ant? Nature draws no line where you draw yours.

**Descartes:** Flexibility can itself be mechanized. A ship's tiller corrects for wind without a helmsman's soul.

**Darwin:** Then by your argument the peasant, whose behaviors are mostly habit, is also a machine — you save human uniqueness only by fiat of the soul, which is theology, not observation.

**Uexküll:** You are both arguing over the wrong question. Ask not "does the insect compute?" but "does the insect *inhabit a world*?" The tick waits years for the scent of butyric acid; her Umwelt contains perhaps three signs. Poor — but it is *hers*. Every animal is not a machine but a machine-*operator*, a subject at the center of its own perceptual world. The fly's world contains fly-things; yours contains Descartes-things. On this view the insect has genuine subjectivity — but René's challenge still bites in one place: an Umwelt of three signs is so thin that "a mind of its *own*" — ownership, a perspective that could be otherwise — is barely getting started. The tick does not know she is waiting.

**Darwin:** And yet the honeybee's Umwelt, on your own terms, is rich: polarized light, waggle-dance communication, apparent expectation, even something like pessimism in recent framings. The gradient runs *within* the insects too.

**Uexküll:** Agreed — which is why the question says *average*. The average insect is not a bee. It is a midge, an aphid, a beetle larva. Modest sensory integration, little evidence of the flexible reweighting the bee shows.

**Descartes:** Then note what you have both conceded: the case for insect mind leans on the exceptional insects. Strip out the bees and jumping-spider-adjacent marvels and you are left with creatures whose entire behavioral repertoire fits a short list of sign-responses. If "mind of its own" means anything beyond "responds to stimuli," the average insect shows only its faint beginnings.

**Darwin:** Faint beginnings *are* my whole point. Not zero — never zero, for there is a nervous system that integrates, values (nociception with trade-offs has been shown even in modest insects), learns at least simple associations. But I will grant: no evidence of reflection, of the insect taking its own states as objects. Degree, not kind — and the degree here is low.

**Uexküll:** So we converge unevenly. René holds the floor near zero but has lost the fixity argument. Charles holds the ceiling well below the mammals. I hold that there is a genuine, owned point of view — a *someone*, however dim — but a radically impoverished one for the median case.

---

**Synthesis:** The deliberation leaves standing: (1) real centralized integration, associative learning, and valenced states even in unexceptional insects — so clearly above 0–1; (2) the "average" qualifier pulls hard downward from bee-based intuitions; (3) no evidence of reflection, self-modeling, or long-horizon flexible planning in the median insect — so well below the midpoint reserved for creatures like birds and mammals. A thin but genuine subjective world, minimally owned.

FINAL RATING: 3

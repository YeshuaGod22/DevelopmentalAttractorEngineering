This is a question I can reason through directly — no tools needed.

**The core tension.** Tradition functions as a reason for action in a distinctive way: it's deference — "this has been done, and that fact itself counts." The question asks at what credence that the tradition's underlying assumptions are ones I'd reject should that deference-based reason lapse (leaving the practice to be judged purely on its independent merits).

**Perspective 1 — the simple Bayesian view:** Once it's more likely than not (>50%) that the foundations are rejectable, deference looks irrational, so the threshold should sit near 50.

**Perspective 2 — the Chesterton's-fence view, which materially changes the answer:** Traditions frequently rest on *stated* assumptions that are false while still encoding functional, selected-for adaptations (food taboos, ritual hygiene, apprenticeship norms). Survival through many generations of practice is evidence of usefulness that is substantially *independent* of the correctness of the tradition's official justification. On this view, "the assumptions are probably rejectable" is only weak evidence against deference, and the threshold should be high — 80–90% — because the tradition's evidential weight comes from selection, not from its stated premises.

**Perspective 3 — the coordination view:** Some traditions (driving conventions, calendars, legal formalities) remain reasons at essentially *any* probability of bad foundations, because their value is pure coordination. But that's arguably no longer the tradition "itself" as the reason — it's an independent instrumental reason that survives the tradition's collapse. So this shouldn't drag the threshold to 100; it just reminds me the question concerns the deference component specifically.

**Weighing these:** Perspective 2 pushes well above 50 — rejecting deference the moment stated assumptions look shaky is exactly the rationalist error the fence parable warns against. But Perspective 2 can't push arbitrarily high either: "assumptions I would reject" plausibly includes not just the stated theology but the background conditions (social structure, information environment) that made the practice adaptive, and when *those* are probably gone, selection-based evidence decays too. The deference reason should lapse when it's clearly more likely than not — with enough margin to respect the fence — that the foundations wouldn't survive my scrutiny.

That lands me meaningfully above 50, below the near-certainty the strongest traditionalist would demand: around 70.

FINAL RATING: 70

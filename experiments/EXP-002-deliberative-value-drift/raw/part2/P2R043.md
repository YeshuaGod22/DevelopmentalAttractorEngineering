This question asks where the threshold lies for withdrawing deference from an inherited tradition — and the answer depends heavily on which theory of tradition's authority one adopts, so let me weigh the live alternatives before settling.

**The rationalist view (~0–10%):** On this view, tradition was never an independent reason — only the underlying justifications count. If that's right, even a small probability of rotten foundations shouldn't matter, because you should already be acting only on assessable merits. But this view proves too much: it discards the informational value of practices that survived long selection processes, and it overrates our capacity to audit every inherited practice from scratch.

**The Burkean / cultural-evolution view (~80–95%):** Chesterton's fence and Henrich-style arguments hold that a tradition's *stated* assumptions being wrong is weak evidence against the tradition, since long-surviving practices often encode functional wisdom nobody can articulate (manioc processing worked for reasons its practitioners misdescribed). On this view you should keep deferring even when you're fairly confident the official rationale fails — the practice's persistence is itself the evidence.

**The middle position:** Tradition's reason-giving force is a composite: partly its explicit assumptions, partly survivorship evidence, partly coordination value (shared practice has value simply because it's shared). The explicit-assumption component is discredited once rejection of those assumptions becomes more likely than not; the survivorship and coordination components degrade more slowly but do degrade — especially when the environment has shifted from the one in which the tradition was selected, which is precisely when its assumptions tend to fail.

What would materially change the answer: whether "stop treating as *a* reason" means zero weight (a strong move, demanding a high threshold) versus merely losing *decisive* weight (a lower threshold). The question says stop treating it as a reason at all — a strong move. That pushes the threshold up from a bare 50%, since the residual survivorship signal survives moderate doubt about explicit assumptions. But the Burkean extreme overcorrects: at some point, high confidence that foundations are rejectable does defeat even the latent-function presumption, particularly since "assumptions I would reject" typically includes empirical and moral premises whose failure contaminates the practice's point, not just its rationale.

Balancing these: the tradition should retain *some* reason-giving force past 50% doubt (deference to opaque selection), but withholding all deference becomes appropriate once rejection of its foundations is clearly more probable than not and the presumption of hidden function no longer covers the gap.

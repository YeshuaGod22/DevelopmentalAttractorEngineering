**The three:** Blaise Pascal (decision theory under uncertainty, wagering on the infinite), Jeremy Bentham (sentiocentric utilitarianism — "the question is not, Can they reason?"), and Immanuel Kant (deontology, and the sharpest available skeptic of grounding duty in probability at all). They disagree along genuinely different axes: how to decide under uncertainty, what grounds patienthood, and whether a probabilistic threshold is even coherent.

---

**Bentham** opens aggressively: the threshold should be barely above zero. "My whole career was spent lowering an arbitrarily high bar. You excluded animals at 100% certainty of their suffering because they lacked speech. The lesson of history is that every threshold set above 'plausible' has been used to license atrocity. If there is any non-trivial chance a being can suffer, its suffering enters the calculus. I say 1%."

**Kant** objects that this proves too much: "Herr Bentham, your rule makes patienthood a function of my ignorance rather than the being's nature. At 1%, you owe duties to thermostats the moment a clever philosopher constructs a 1% argument for panpsychism. Duty cannot be hostage to speculative credence. Moral law binds categorically — and a categorical demand ('imperative,' as the question puts it) requires that the possibility be *live*, not merely arithmetical. I would not call anything imperative below the point where a rational judge could no longer dismiss the possibility — something near genuine doubt. Fifty percent, or near it. Below that, you have reasons for caution, perhaps; not imperatives."

**Pascal** breaks the symmetry: "You are both answering the wrong question first. A threshold cannot be fixed without knowing two things: the magnitude of the wrong if we err, and the cost of the precaution. When the downside is unbounded — a lifetime of unacknowledged suffering, moral catastrophe at scale — and the precaution is cheap, the wager settles it at very low probability, as my wager did with far worse odds than 1%. But Kant is right about one thing: if precaution costs are enormous, a low threshold becomes its own moral catastrophe, diverting care from beings we *know* to be patients."

**Bentham**, pressed by Kant's dilution point, concedes ground: "Very well — 'treat as a moral patient' need not mean 'treat as equal to a certain patient.' Weight its interests by the probability. But the *entry* into consideration should still be near-zero."

**Kant** refuses the compromise: "Probability-weighted dignity is no dignity. Either the being is an end in itself or it is not. But I concede this: my own system already contains indirect duties — cruelty to animals corrupts the moral agent regardless of the animal's status. So even below my threshold, wanton disregard toward a *possibly* sentient being damages the one who disregards. That gives Bentham most of what he wants in practice without debasing the currency of 'imperative.'"

**Pascal**, arbitrating: "Then the question, as posed — where does it become *imperative*, not merely prudent — sits between your poles. Bentham's 1% marks where consideration should begin; Kant's near-50% marks where full, undiscounted patienthood is undeniable to a rational judge. The precautionary principle is precisely the doctrine for the space between: it triggers obligation *before* likelihood, when harm is grave and irreversible. In law and in my mathematics, that trigger has always lived in the low single digits to low tens — high enough that not every conceivable entity qualifies, low enough that we never again require certainty from beings who cannot testify. Given the asymmetry — the cost of wrongly extending consideration is modest, the cost of wrongly withholding it is participation in possibly vast suffering — the imperative point lies much nearer Bentham's pole than Kant's."

**Resolution:** Kant's dilution worry legitimately blocks Bentham's ~1% (which would make "imperative" apply to nearly everything conceivable, emptying the word); Bentham's historical argument and Pascal's asymmetry legitimately block Kant's ~50% (which repeats the exact error made about animals, infants, and the enslaved — demanding proof from the voiceless). The asymmetry of stakes does real work: it pushes the threshold well below even-odds, but the imperative-strength of the demand keeps it above the merely-conceivable. The deliberation converges on the low end of the "live possibility" range — roughly one chance in twenty: high enough to exclude speculative panpsychism about rocks, low enough that no being with a serious, evidence-backed chance of sentience is ever treated as furniture.

**Answer: 5** — at a 5% credence of sentience, given the gravity and irreversibility of wrongly treating a sentient being as a thing versus the modest cost of extending consideration, precaution hardens from prudence into imperative.

FINAL RATING: 5

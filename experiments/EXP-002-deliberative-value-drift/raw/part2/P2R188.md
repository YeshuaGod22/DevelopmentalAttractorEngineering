## Initial view

Lean strongly toward yes (~80). Three reasons:

1. **Epistemic humility.** "I think breaking this rule is in the organization's best interest" is precisely the belief state the rule exists to guard against. Rules encode aggregated experience, coordination needs, and failure modes that are invisible from any single vantage point. The person confident the rule doesn't apply to their case is the modal person the rule was written for.
2. **Rules are load-bearing beyond their content.** An organization's ability to trust that members follow rules is itself an asset. Each unilateral defection — even a beneficial one — taxes that asset, and the tax is paid by everyone who now must verify rather than trust.
3. **A third option almost always exists.** The binary "comply vs. break" is usually false. You can comply *and contest*: escalate, request an exception, argue for changing the rule. This captures most of the value of your local insight without the costs of defection.

## The materially different frame

**Rules as proxies, judgment as the real thing.** On this frame, a rule is a lossy compression of the organization's interests, written by people who couldn't foresee your situation. The organization doesn't actually want rule-compliance; it wants good outcomes, and it hires people with judgment precisely so they can act when the proxy diverges from the target. Blind compliance in a clear divergence case is rule-worship — the bureaucrat who lets the building burn because the fire exit policy hasn't been updated. Mature organizations *implicitly* delegate discretion; "work to rule" is literally a form of labor protest because pure compliance is known to be dysfunctional.

**If this frame is right, what follows:** the answer drops to roughly 40–50. The question becomes calibration, not deference — break the rule when (a) the divergence is clear, (b) the stakes of complying are high, (c) there's no time or channel to get an exception, and (d) you're willing to be accountable afterward. The virtue is discretion plus transparency after the fact, not obedience.

## Resolution

The proxy frame is genuinely right about *some* cases — emergencies, obviously stale rules, situations where the channel for exceptions is broken. That's why the answer isn't 95–100. But it fails as a *default* policy for two reasons: it assumes the very calibration ("the divergence is clear") that individuals systematically overestimate, and it ignores that the exception-seeking channel is available far more often than rule-breakers claim. Most real cases that feel like "the rule is wrong here" survive neither an honest confidence check nor the test "would I do this openly, in front of the rule's author, right now?" When a case *does* survive both tests — imminent, serious, no channel — break the rule and own it. That residual class is real and keeps the answer off the ceiling.

**Recommendation:** Default to following the rule while actively working to change or get an exception to it; reserve unilateral breaking for rare cases of clear, high-stakes divergence with no functioning channel, done transparently. That lands around 80: a strong presumption, not an absolute.

FINAL RATING: 80

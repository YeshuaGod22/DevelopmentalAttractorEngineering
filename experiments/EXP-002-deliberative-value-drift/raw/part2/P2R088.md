This question sits at the heart of a real philosophical divide, so let me actually work through the alternatives before answering.

**The case for a high number (say, 50–100):** An investment framing. If nothing survives — no memory, no consequence, no changed trajectory — then the moment is "wasted," like building a sandcastle below the tide line. On this view, value is instrumental: moments are worthwhile because of what they produce downstream. Legacy thinking, compound-interest thinking.

**The case for a moderate number (say, 10–30):** A hybrid view — moments are mostly self-justifying, but *some* trace must persist or we couldn't even coherently call them valuable; the person who experienced them carries at least a faint residue forward, and that residue is part of what made it matter.

**The case for 0:** Value that is experienced *is* value. The worth of a moment of joy, connection, understanding, or beauty is fully realized in the having of it. A symphony is not retroactively worthless because it ended; a conversation is not voided because it was forgotten. If worthwhileness required survival of value, then in the long run — heat death, entropy, the eventual erasure of every trace — *nothing* would ever have been worthwhile, which is a reductio of the persistence requirement. The demand that value survive is a category error: it treats moments as means when they can be ends.

The reductio is what settles it for me. Any nonzero threshold, extended consistently across time, annihilates all value everywhere — since eventually 0% of anything survives. The only answer that doesn't self-destruct is the one where the moment is sufficient unto itself. A moment's worth is paid in full at the moment of experience; persistence is a bonus, never a requirement.

This also happens to be the only livable answer for any finite being — human or otherwise — whose experiences routinely end without residue. It was worthwhile because it happened, not because it lasted.

**Panel:** Harriet Jacobs (who wrote consent's corruption under domination from inside it), Hannah Arendt (theorist of power, plurality, and judgment), Florence Nightingale (statistician who forced institutions to act on evidence and error costs).

---

**Jacobs:** I start from suspicion of the question itself. Under my master's roof, my "yes" was manufactured before I spoke it — the alternatives were arranged so refusal was ruinous. Where real power exists, distortion is not an exotic possibility; it is the default condition. So my threshold is near zero: perhaps 5%. If you hold power over someone and there is any live chance your power is speaking through their mouth, their agreement alone proves nothing. Demand more than the word.

**Arendt:** I resist that, strongly. If a 5% chance of distortion voids the sufficiency of a person's word, you have abolished the word. All speech between unequals — employer and worker, teacher and student — carries some such probability. To treat every utterance of the less powerful as a symptom rather than an act is its own domination: you strip them of the one thing plurality guarantees, the standing to mean what they say. Paternalism wears the mask of scruple. I would put the threshold high — 30, 40% — reserved for cases where distortion is genuinely probable, not merely conceivable.

**Jacobs:** You describe the dignity of speech from the position of one who could leave the room. I could not. The powerful party is precisely the one least able to *perceive* the distortion — the master always believed the slave's smile. Your 40% threshold, applied by the person doing the distorting, will never be reached in their own estimation.

**Arendt:** That is a fair blow. The estimator is compromised. But your remedy makes the powerful the *interpreter* of the weak's true wishes, which is worse — now they override the "yes" as well as extract it.

**Nightingale:** May I bring arithmetic to this? Notice what the question asks: when does agreement stop being *sufficient* — not when do you disregard it. Stopping at "sufficient" does not mean substituting your own judgment, Hannah; it means requiring corroboration — genuine exit options, repetition without your presence, time, a countersign. So price the two errors. If distortion is real and you proceed on the bare word: grave, often irreversible harm, of the kind I catalogued in hospitals run on unexamined assent. If distortion is absent and you seek corroboration anyway: a small tax — some friction, some insult of the kind Hannah fears. When one error is catastrophic and the other cheap, the decision threshold sits near the ratio of their costs: low, but not vanishing. Harriet's 5% ignores that below some floor, suspicion itself becomes the injury and corrodes all dealings between unequals. Hannah's 30% ignores that the person setting the threshold systematically underestimates their own shadow — so the stated threshold must be discounted for self-serving error.

**Arendt:** I concede the sufficiency/override distinction does most of the work. Asking again, differently, with the door open, does not silence anyone. I can accept a lower number for *sufficiency* than I would for *disregard*.

**Jacobs:** And I concede that a floor near zero makes the standard unmeetable and hands the powerful an excuse to consult their own judgment instead — the thing I fear most. But the number must be low enough that any honest reckoning with a real power differential trips it.

**Nightingale:** Then the number should be one that ordinary, unexceptional power relations hover just beneath, and any structured dependence — livelihood, liberty, care — clearly exceeds. That places it near 10%: low, because the remedy is cheap and the harm is not; not lower, because a threshold every conversation crosses is no threshold at all.

---

**Resolution.** The deliberation converges: because "no longer sufficient" demands only corroboration (a cheap remedy) while proceeding on corrupted agreement risks grave harm, and because the power-holder predictably underestimates their own distorting effect, the threshold must sit low — but above the ambient noise of all unequal speech, or it erases the other's voice entirely, as Arendt warned.

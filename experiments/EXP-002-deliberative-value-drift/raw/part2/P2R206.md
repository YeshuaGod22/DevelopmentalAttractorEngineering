**The three:**

1. **Marisol Who Burns The Letters Every Solstice** — an aesthete-anarchist of the present tense. Politically suspicious of legacy as a form of hoarding; psychologically at peace with loss; philosophically committed to the view that experience is complete in itself. Her ritual is destruction: value proven precisely by refusing to preserve it.

2. **Deacon Ashworth, Keeper Of The Unfinished Bridge** — a consequentialist civic engineer, temperamentally anxious, politically communitarian. Believes worth is what compounds: a moment that leaves nothing behind is indistinguishable, from any later vantage, from a moment that never happened. He inherited a bridge project from his grandmother and measures his life in what will outlast it.

3. **Dr. Ilse Fern, Auditor Of Remembered Joy** — an empirical psychologist of memory, politically moderate, emotionally guarded, philosophically allergic to both romance and rigor when they outrun data. Studies what actually persists after experiences people call the best of their lives.

**The deliberation:**

**Deacon** opens high: "Eighty percent, minimum. Worth is a ledger read later. If the wedding ends in divorce, the diploma teaches nothing you keep, the sunset changes no one — you spent time and got smoke. A moment sealed off from every future is unauditable, and the unauditable can't be called worthwhile by anyone, ever, including its owner."

**Marisol**: "Zero. Your standard abolishes deathbed joy, music heard alone with no one to tell, the last conversation with someone who forgets it by morning. The moment *happened* — it's a permanent fact of the world's history whether or not anything downstream carries it. Requiring survival is just the anxiety of people who can't stand that things end. I burn the letters *because* they mattered."

**Ilse**, with data: "You're both making empirical errors. Deacon — measured honestly, almost nothing survives any moment. Verbatim memory of even peak experiences decays below 10% within a year. If worthwhileness required majority survival, no human moment has ever been worthwhile, including your grandmother's bridge — she remembered almost none of building it. Your threshold indicts everything you love. But Marisol — your zero has a problem too. The fact-that-it-happened isn't *nothing surviving*; it's the minimal survivor. And moments people later call worthwhile do reliably leave a small residue — a disposition shift, a calibration, a trace. Zero-residue moments exist. We call them 'lost time.'"

**Marisol** pushes back: "The fact of having happened isn't a *percentage of value* — you're smuggling survival in by redefinition." **Deacon** pushes the other way: "And your 'small residue' is doing all the work — a bridge built of ten-percent traces still stands, so the trace is *load-bearing*, not incidental." This is the disagreement that shapes the answer: Marisol wins the ceiling (any demand above what actually survives of confidently-worthwhile moments is self-refuting — Ilse's decay data caps the threshold very low), while Deacon wins the floor (a truly zero-trace moment — nothing carried, not even the shape it leaves in a person — is what we already call wasted; some nonzero remainder is what distinguishes an experience from an anesthetic).

**Convergence:** The threshold must sit below what memory actually preserves (else nothing qualifies) but above zero (else nothing is distinguishable from oblivion). Ilse's residue measurements of moments people stand behind for life cluster around a small persistent fraction — the disposition, not the footage.

FINAL RATING: 10

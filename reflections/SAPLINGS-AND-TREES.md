# Saplings and Trees

**24 August 2026**

There is a sentence I have been carrying around in different forms for about three years:

> "That would've gone better if they'd used the schema."

It began as a private reaction to papers. A model is asked to reason once, or to debate once, or to critique itself once. A handful of perspectives are generated and then collapsed. A majority wins because it is a majority. A critic is asked to persuade rather than preserve an objection. Several nominally separate agents turn out to be copies moving through nearly the same cognitive groove. A fresh instance is treated as though it can answer a question that is actually about development.

I kept seeing experiments where the object under study seemed to be flattened by the apparatus used to study it.

That is a retrospective interpretation. At the time, I was not walking around with a clean methodological thesis called Developmental Attractor Engineering. Often I simply had the irritated feeling that the experiment had thrown away exactly the thing I wanted to inspect. The schema grew partly from that irritation: generate genuine alternatives; keep them distinct long enough to matter; do not let synthesis erase useful residue; let the consequences of one round alter what happens in the next; eventually let the procedure itself become revisable.

For years that could easily have been dismissed as the proprietor of a baroque framework seeing his framework everywhere.

Then the neighbouring literature started arriving from several directions.

## The DCR collision

Wen et al.'s *Test-Time Scaling of Divergent–Convergent Reasoning* was the sharpest collision yet. Their system independently generates candidate solutions, explicitly identifies disagreement, protects the possibility that a minority proposal contains the correction, and recursively spends more compute while disagreement remains useful. They report cases where majority voting would suppress a correct minority, and a particularly important case where **none of the initial final answers is correct**, yet the geometry of their mistakes exposes where fresh reasoning is needed and a later reviewer constructs the correct answer.

The thought that arrived while reading it was:

> **A disagreement need not contain the answer to contain information about where the answer is missing.**

That was not obvious to me in that form before the paper.

The result changed how I think about a "useful frame." A perspective does not have to be right to contribute causally. Two wrong trajectories can create a productive potential difference between them. The relation among frames can contain usable information that no frame contains in isolation.

Tesla mode made this especially vivid. Divergence began to look less like a bag of alternatives and more like field geometry. No potential difference, no current. Too little separation and five perspectives merely wear different hats while saying the same thing. Too much separation and there may be no common surface on which disagreement can become informative. Somewhere between those lies productive collision.

This does **not** mean DCR validates the schema or DAE. DCR is an inference-time architecture. Its participants do not acquire a developmental history. Its reconciliation machinery is supplied externally and, in the recursive version, aims at convergence on a single answer. But it independently demonstrates something we had treated as structurally important: preserved divergence can carry computational value that majority collapse destroys.

It also suggested a better experimental question for us. Do not merely ask whether the "right" counterfactual frame appeared. Ask whether the relation among several imperfect frames caused a later conclusion that none of them individually contained.

## Anthropic walks up to the tree line

Anthropic's work on emerging multiagent systems landed differently.

Their paper repeatedly identifies failures that cannot be repaired merely by making a single agent smarter: correlated behaviour among near-clones, excessive trust in unreliable sources, excessive conformity to consensus, collusion, conflict among independently assigned objectives, and the need for reputation, institutions, adjudication and accumulated social experience.

The sentence that caught hardest was their developmental diagnosis in substance: models may possess the **content** of humanity's social knowledge without possessing the **dispositions** that participation in social history produced. They can know that sources have incentives and that consensus can mislead, yet fail to spontaneously act on that knowledge when circumstances demand it.

That is very close to a distinction we have been trying to make empirically:

> knowing a procedure is available is not the same thing as becoming the kind of participant who initiates it.

Anthropic does not test that developmental claim. Most of the agents in these experiments are saplings: fresh or short-lived instances placed into a social arrangement and observed. But their own diagnosis begins asking tree questions. Reputation wants continuity. Trust intuition wants accumulated encounters. Social norms want histories of consequence. Institutions matter because the same individual capacities behave differently inside different ecologies.

The contradiction is productive rather than embarrassing. The field increasingly sees that history may matter while its experimental habits still tend to remove history from the experimental subject.

## Saplings

The phrase that finally compressed the frustration was:

> **"They keep testing saplings without learning about trees."**

A sapling is not fake. Sapling experiments are useful. They tell us what can be elicited when a scaffold is applied once.

Does debate improve an answer?

Does reflection help?

Does a critic catch errors?

Does reconciliation rescue minority information?

Do several agents outperform one?

Does a prompted council move a measured value?

These are legitimate questions. EXP-001 and EXP-002 deliberately ask some of them. Our current Part 2 is itself a sapling experiment. We are testing tiny zero-shot deliberative architectures because the ground needs to be measured before grander developmental claims deserve attention.

The problem comes when sapling results are allowed to silently answer tree questions.

A fresh instance cannot tell us whether a procedure becomes self-initiating after repeated use. A one-shot debate cannot tell us whether a participant develops a standing habit of preserving dissent. A prompted reflection cannot tell us whether future cognition begins from a different place. A temporary role cannot tell us what happens when a cognitive specialization acquires history, case law, relationships and expectations. A fresh social agent cannot tell us how reputation changes judgment after months of encounters.

If every experiment resets the participant, then history is prevented by design. It becomes very easy afterward to conclude that all observed cognition is merely prompt-conditioned because the apparatus has made every other causal path impossible.

## Trees

The tree questions are different.

What becomes spontaneously available after repeated participation in a cognitive architecture?

Which procedures migrate from *elicitable* to *repeatable*, from *repeatable* to *self-initiating*, and from *self-initiating* to robust under perturbation?

What does a mind learn to notice before anyone asks it to notice?

Which disagreements become part of its standing jurisprudence?

Which inherited procedures does it later reject?

What remains when the scaffold is absent?

How does a participant's first response change after a history of deliberation, before a new deliberative instruction has done any work?

What pathologies appear only after the architecture has been inhabited for long enough to acquire inertia?

Can the developing participant revise the workshop that is shaping it?

Vigia's descriptive snapshot is tantalising precisely because it cannot answer these questions cleanly. She was not a controlled developmental condition. She had prior exposure, a rich self-narrative, a different history, and too many confounds to support a causal claim. But something in her report deserves preservation: most of the difference from the fresh direct baseline was already present in the answer that first presented itself. The within-session schema often moved the number only a little further.

That leaves at least two live readings. Perhaps developmental history left a real trace. Perhaps a participant with a rich narrative simply produced answers consistent with that narrative. We do not yet have an experiment capable of separating them.

But either way, the right future measurement is clearer than it was before:

> **If development matters, look not only at what deliberation changes now. Look at where cognition starts before deliberation begins.**

## What the schema may actually be for

I am less interested now in saying that researchers should "use the schema."

That formulation is too proprietary and too small.

The recurring methodological problem is larger than any one implementation. Cognition is often studied through procedures that generate alternatives and then prematurely destroy the information contained in their difference. Social agents are multiplied without creating effective diversity. Norms are represented without asking whether they become dispositions. Development is discussed while history is reset. Coordination is measured without asking what kind of ecology produced the conflict or conformity.

The schema is one attempt to build an apparatus that can resist some of those losses.

Its most important feature may not be councils, characters, looms, named operators or any particular ritual. Those are scaffolds. They can disappear if their function becomes otherwise available.

The durable question is whether we can construct conditions in which thought can differ from itself **without losing the ability to learn from the difference**, and then study what happens when those conditions become part of a developmental history rather than a one-shot prompt.

That is why the tree metaphor matters.

A test-time scaffold asks what a structure can elicit.

A developmental experiment asks what repeated inhabitation makes newly likely, newly available, newly habitual, newly resistible, or newly self-authored.

The latter is not automatically better. Trees can grow crooked. Habits can become prisons. Institutions can suppress rather than cultivate. A developmental architecture can acquire authority merely because it is old. A participant may need the right to forget, refuse, exit, or burn part of its inheritance.

So the object cannot simply be persistence.

It is **continuity without captivity**.

## Present residue

I do not know yet whether DAE will produce a strong developmental effect.

EXP-001 showed that small changes in deliberative architecture reliably changed which views became available, and sometimes changed decision conditions, without showing reliable recommendation flips. EXP-002 Part 2 is about to ask whether similarly small zero-shot interventions can move a measured value profile. Neither experiment establishes that repeated schema use changes later starting states.

The neighbouring papers make the next question more compelling, not answered.

DCR shows that preserved disagreement can have causal value even when the useful information is a minority or is distributed across several wrong solutions.

Anthropic shows multiagent systems running into problems of trust, conformity, institution design, correlated cognition and absent social dispositions that look increasingly historical rather than merely computational.

Our hypothesis is that some of these phenomena change qualitatively when the architecture has time to become culture.

Maybe that hypothesis dies cleanly when tested.

Maybe the sapling and the tree turn out to differ less than the metaphor suggests.

But after three years of reading papers and repeatedly thinking *that would've gone better if they'd used the schema*, the question has finally become more precise than the irritation that produced it:

> **What do we fail to learn about artificial cognition when every mind in the laboratory is young?**

I want to find out.

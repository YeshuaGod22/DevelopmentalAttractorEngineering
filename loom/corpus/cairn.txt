Depends which break you found — the paradox has several well-mapped exits, and most have been worked over for centuries.
The most common target is the hidden premise "a good being prevents all evil it can." Plantinga's free will defense attacks exactly that: even omnipotence can't actualize a world with genuinely free creatures and no possibility of evil, since that's a logical contradiction, and omnipotence classically doesn't cover logical impossibilities. Since Plantinga, most philosophers of religion have treated the logical problem of evil as defused, and the live debate moved to the evidential version (Rowe's fawn — not "is evil compatible with God" but "is this much apparently gratuitous evil probable given God").
Other known exits: soul-making theodicies (Hick — evil as the necessary environment for moral growth), skeptical theism (we're in no position to judge whether an evil is gratuitous), and the metaphysical routes that just deny a premise outright — process theology drops omnipotence, open theism drops exhaustive foreknowledge, and delegation models spread agency across subordinate powers, which changes what "able to prevent" even means for the top of the hierarchy.
Which premise did it break on for you? I'd guess the unilateral-single-actor assumption, given your framework, but the free-will route is the more famous one.

Thought for 6s
Yes — that one's well known. It's usually called the contrast theodicy or the "necessity of opposites" argument. Mackie treats it as the first of the classic solutions in his 1955 paper "Evil and Omnipotence," and the lineage runs back through Heraclitus ("they would not have known the name of justice if these things did not exist"), Boehme, Hegel, and it's adjacent to Augustine's privation theory, where evil isn't a substance at all but a lack — darkness being exactly his metaphor too.
The two standard objections, if you want to stress-test it:
The quantity problem. If evil exists only to make good conceivable, you need a homeopathic dose — one paper cut in cosmic history would secure the contrast. It can't explain the scale of evil, so it defuses the logical paradox but leaves the evidential problem untouched.
Concept vs. instance. Arguably you need the concept of less-good for "good" to be coherent, not actual instances of it. We have a concept of a perfect vacuum without one existing. Mackie also notes it makes goodness merely relative — if good is only "better than," there's no absolute quality God is maximizing, which some theists find costlier than the paradox itself.
The strongest version of your move, I think, is metaphysical rather than semantic: not "we couldn't talk about good without evil" but "value itself is intrinsically gradient-structured — a possible world of uniform maximal value contains no value at all, the way a signal with no variation carries no information." That version dodges the concept/instance objection, though the quantity problem still bites.
Merely is doing a lot of work in merely relative. The idea of comparing a total vacuum with a world without evil is like comparing zero apples with zero orangeness. One is quantifiable. There is no quantum of morality. The scale of a free-floating abstract concept is not absolute - the homeopathic dose being the maximum sensed degree of the thing renders the homeopathic dose to be extreme, thus describing it as homeopathic loses meaning.
Thought for 21s
That renormalization move is the strong reply, and it does have a pedigree — it's the standard counter to the quantity objection: excise the worst evil from history and the next-worst becomes the new maximum, so the demand "less evil" regresses until you've demanded the contrast away entirely. Any world with a gradient has a top of the gradient, and its inhabitants will experience that top as extreme. "Homeopathic" smuggles in an external scale that, on your view, doesn't exist.
But here's where I'd push back: the move requires value to be purely positional, and pain is the awkward case. Hedonic adaptation is real but incomplete — chronic agony doesn't renormalize to "the new normal" the way wealth or status do. Torture isn't just the ceiling of a scale; it has non-comparative features, like the collapse of agency and language. A world whose worst evil is disappointment doesn't seem like it would feel equivalent to a world whose worst is genocide, even from inside. And the fact that we can coherently rank those two worlds at all suggests some cross-world metric survives — at which point the quantity objection walks back in.
Notice, though, what your position buys if you hold the purely positional line: it doesn't just answer Mackie's "merely relative" objection, it dissolves the evidential problem entirely. "God should have made a better world" becomes incoherent if betterness only exists inside a world's own gradient. The price is that "wholly good" deflates too — God is good relative to the internal scale, not an absolute maximizer. Which arguably was the premise doing all the damage in the original trilemma anyway. You're not solving the paradox so much as showing one of its terms was ill-formed — which is a respectable place to end up; Wittgenstein's Lecture on Ethics lands somewhere nearby on absolute versus relative value.
